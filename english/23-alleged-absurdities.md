“Having spoken briefly of these consequences,” says Bertius, “let us now examine the following.” {pag.69.l.13}

<!-- B002 -->
# Absurdities that follow from the opposing opinion.

<!-- B003 -->
> Those who maintain that true righteousness and true faith cannot be abandoned necessarily fall into many absurdities. We shall draw a few drops from the whole sea.

<!-- B004 -->
**Answer.** Bertius makes a show of treating us with great courtesy and in a spirit of love. Though he could drown us, it seems, in a whole sea of duplicity, he will content himself with sprinkling us with a few drops. Yet it seems to me that, had he not been able to suck duplicity out of our thoughts, he would have spat still more of it at us.[^drops] But to the point.

<!-- B005 -->
> {1.} First, it must follow from their opinion, says Bertius, that there are absolutely {pag.69.l.20} no true virtues in anyone except those alone who, according to their opinion, are called elect. For if these virtues were also in others, they would certainly persevere in them and consequently would also be elect, etc.

<!-- B006 -->
**Answer.** Christian virtues are a different matter from philosophical or indeed Pharisaic virtues.

<!-- B007 -->
> True righteousness, says Bertius, is a virtue of the person who has been converted and justified by God in Christ, rendering the love owed to God and one's neighbour, and those services that follow from love. {pag.47.l.14} True faith and true love can also be described after this pattern.

<!-- B008 -->
Therefore there is no true righteousness or love, and consequently no true virtue either, in any except those who have been converted and justified by God in Christ.

<!-- B009 -->
> All the works, too, of those who continue in sins and impenitence, says Bertius, displease God, even though they have an outward appearance of virtue.

<!-- B010 -->
{pag 66.ll 9} For example, the Jews who were in God's covenant daily offered many rams, oxen, incense, burnt offerings and grain offerings, which God had indeed commanded them to offer. Likewise they diligently observed the new moons and other annual seasons and feasts that God had also commanded them to keep. Yet this displeased God, because the Jews who did this had hands full of blood. {1 ebt.2.3.4° en volgende caput.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M06">Marginal translation: 1 ebt.2.3.4° and the following chapter.</span> {Num.28.16 Deut.16.1. etc.} {Esai.1.11. en cap.66.3} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M08">Marginal translation: Esai.1.11 and chapter 66.3.</span> If these works displeased God, they must surely have been evil; and no work is evil except a sinful work. All this being so, as it is, why does Bertius say that it is so absurd to say that there are no true virtues in any except the elect alone? And that everything the reprobate do, notwithstanding that it appears to be true, honourable and good, is in fact nothing but sin? {pag.69.l.25,} We speak this way about their works not because those who do them are elect or reprobate, but because they are believers justified in Christ, or unbelievers who have not been justified. Indeed, it is impossible for anyone without faith to please God. {Ebræ.11.6.} Thus all unbelievers must displease God. And since all true virtues please God, and no works displease God except sinful works, all the works of unbelievers that displease God must also be sinful works—even though not everything unbelievers do is always sin in every respect.

For sin is committed on the one hand when one does what God has forbidden or omits what he has commanded; on the other, when one does a commanded work or refrains from a forbidden work for other reasons and to another end than those for which such a work ought to be done or left undone. Whoever gives his neighbour what is due to him does not thereby commit sin. But when one gives one's neighbour what is due to him in order to win the esteem of others, or at any rate not to God's glory, such a work is then no true virtue before God, or one that pleases God, but is sin. {Matth 6.1.}

That someone does not steal is no sin. But if someone now refrains from stealing out of fear of being caught and punished, or so that, concealing his wickedness for a time, he may afterwards steal more conveniently and profitably, that person certainly sins greatly: not by refraining from the forbidden act, but by failing to refrain from it in the right way.[^manner] So it is with all other similar moral works. {Genes. 6.2. Rom 13.14. I. Co. 7 39. En cap. 10.31} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M12">Marginal translation: Genes. 6.2; Rom 13.14; I. Co. 7 39 and chapter 10.31.</span> So even if someone said the same of natural acts such as eating, drinking and marrying, Christians ought not to find it so very strange or absurd.

The philosophers, as they themselves well know, judge all these things differently, for they have not known the command concerning Christ or the honour of the true God. Nevertheless, they too say that in all people [text unreadable] which accords with right reason is no true virtue, though it does not follow that everything such a person does must be called a vice, as is evident from the example of those they call *continentes*, that is, self-restrained people. Whoever desires more and closer proof of this should read Augustine, lib. 4. in Iulian. c. 3. & Epistol. 99; Lactant. l. b. 1 c. 10, and lib. 6. c. 5 and following; Prosper, de vita contemplat. lib. 3. c. 1, and de vocat. Gent. lib. 1. c. 3, and others.

<!-- B011 -->
> {2. pag. 69. l. 31.} Secondly, it follows that there is some property without a subject or person in whom such a property is found: namely, falling away, etc.

<!-- B012 -->
**Answer.** We say that the elect fall away, and also that the reprobate fall away. The elect fall away from true faith and true holiness, but they do not fall away entirely or finally. Conversely, the reprobate fall away entirely and finally, but not from true faith or true holiness: rather, from such faith and holiness as they have. {Siet ons Vertoogh pag. 49. l. 7. ende volgens.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M14">Marginal translation: See our Demonstration page 49, line 7 and following.</span> And it is certainly absurd to say: the elect do not fall away entirely or finally; therefore they do not fall away in any manner. And: the reprobate do not fall away from true faith; therefore they do not fall away from the faith that they have either. {pag. 15. l. 35. I. Cori. 13.2.} There are two kinds of falling away, says Bertius himself: one partial, the other general. Therefore someone who falls away partially also falls away. Faith without love is not true saving faith. But if anyone fell away from such faith, would he not be someone who falls away?

<!-- B013 -->
> {3. Pag. 70. l 1.} Thirdly, it follows that good works differ not in their own essence, but only in the subjects or persons by whom they are done; and that one and the same deed can be honourable and pleasing to God in an elect person, and in a reprobate, however he conducts himself, cannot fail to be sin, etc.

<!-- B014 -->
{Pag. 70. l. 7.} **Answer.** It is true that the same sins are curable in the elect, but not in the reprobate; that even the gravest and most hideous sins must be forgiven in the elect, whereas in the others even the very least sin can never be forgiven. But this happens not because they are elect and reprobate, but because they are believing and penitent, or unbelieving and impenitent. For the elect cannot claim and appropriate for themselves forgiveness of any sins before they believe in Jesus Christ.

<!-- B015 -->
It is also true that one and the same deed, though not performed in the same {pag. 70. l. 23} manner, can be honourable and pleasing to God in an elect person, and in a reprobate, however he conducts himself, cannot fail to be a sin. But this too happens not because they are elect or reprobate, but because they are regenerate or unregenerate, as was also shown in the answer to the first absurdity. Yet it does not follow from this that these moral works do not differ in their essence, even though they differ in their subjects.

For no work can exceed its worker's power, as Bertius himself teaches, since the work proceeds from the power. Thus {pag. 55. l. 23.} as the worker is, so must the work also be. If the worker has power to perform a perfectly good work, the work is perfectly good when the worker acts according to the power he has. If the worker has only the power to perform a half-good work, the work too can only be half-good. And since believers alone have the power to act according to God's command, to God's glory, from a true faith, they alone can perform such a work.

In such a case, then, the goodness or essence of such a moral work will not depend on the subject or person who performs it; rather, the character of the work itself will be its essence: namely, that it is commanded by God and done by the person in the way and manner, and also to the end, in which it ought to be done. Nevertheless, that the work is done in this way and receives such an essence depends on the subject or person who performs that very work according to the power he has. As for the rest that Bertius piles on here, it has already been answered elsewhere.

<!-- B016 -->
> Fourthly, it follows that the cause of the temporary falling away of the godly is a temporary withdrawal of grace, a withdrawal {4. pag. 70. l. 22} God brings about in order to make known both to them and to others that the godly can do nothing of themselves, etc.

<!-- B017 -->
**Answer.** I cannot see how this can be drawn from our teaching on the perseverance of the saints. If indeed Bertius saw before him so great a sea of absurdities as he says, he could and ought, I think, to have come somewhat closer to us. As for the matter itself, we are greatly wronged when such a teaching as Bertius here proposes and mocks in a {Pag 70. 71. 72. en 73.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M21">Marginal translation: Pages 70, 71, 72 and 73.</span> long-winded discourse is attributed to us. This is what we teach: when the regenerate sin, this comes about because some grace they formerly had is withdrawn from them, or because some grace is not given to them without which they nevertheless cannot refrain from such a sin.

The cause for which some grace is withdrawn from them is always and solely the sin committed by them, by which they have grieved the Holy Spirit. The purpose for which such grace is withdrawn is not that henceforth they might sin all the more, although this sometimes follows upon the withdrawal of such grace. Rather, it is so that, now lacking that grace—whether they have lost a portion of their goods and prosperity, or whether they have partly lost the inward comfort and strength to resist temptations—they might learn all the better through the grace remaining to know that without God's Spirit they are utterly wretched and weak. It is also that they might learn better to observe the deceitfulness and ugliness of sins, for whose sake they have been thus far forsaken by God; and that accordingly in times to come they might be better on their guard, watch more diligently, pray more fervently, hate and flee sin more, and, in short, that their salvation might thus be furthered more effectively. And we ought not to think it strange, says Augustine, that God, withdrawing some grace from his own, thereby furthers their salvation, since {1. Corin 5.5. 1. Tim.1.20.} the apostle Paul, through the service and operation of the devil, sought to abolish and remedy the devil's works, such as incest and blasphemy.

As for the withholding or refusal of grace—which the regenerate nevertheless cannot lack if they are to refrain from certain sins—its cause is not some previously committed sin. Rather, God sometimes withholds such grace from his own so that, then sinning, they might learn to humble themselves thoroughly before God and to watch themselves somewhat better another time. {Tegen Perkins. Pag.165.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M23">Marginal translation: Against Perkins, page 165.</span> D. Arminius, whom I introduce here especially to accommodate Bertius, writes thus concerning this:

<!-- B018 -->
> The permission of the sin of ignorance arises from the suspension of the revelation of God's will; that of the sin of wickedness, from the suspension of the operation of God by which the heart's wickedness is corrected and changed; that of the sin of weakness, from withholding the power to stand firm in temptation; that of the sin of heedlessness, from the suspension of the operations of God by which there is brought about in us an earnest and holy care and concern to attend closely to our steps and to walk in the law of the Lord.

<!-- B019 -->
{Pag 166.} And shortly afterwards:

<!-- B020 -->
> God wished Peter, who thought somewhat too much of himself, to come to know himself, and suffered or permitted him to deny his Master from fear of death, not giving him that steadfastness of his Holy Spirit by which he would despise death and boldly confess Christ. David, having been freed from his enemies and having conquered almost all his neighbouring kings and peoples, began to keep watch over his ways somewhat more heedlessly. From this it came about that he fell into that hideous adultery with Uriah's wife. And God permitted David to fall into that heedlessness and through that opportunity to commit that sin, so that he might guard himself more diligently, his sins being written down as a mirror for others, display a glorious pattern of humility and penitence, and rise from his sin all the more gloriously.

<!-- B021 -->
Thus far the very words of D. Arminius. They express the opinion of the Reformed churches concerning the point we now specifically have before us so aptly that Bertius will not be able henceforth to contradict us on this point without also having to declare D. Arminius wrong. I do not think he will wish or dare to do that. Why, then, has he so inveighed against us? Why has he made our opinion—which after all is not ours—so odious? Why has he mocked it so? One ought always to understand, interpret and receive everything in the best sense, and hold it certain that it is no small sin in God's eyes to twist any individual person's words to his disadvantage. What, then, will it be if one seeks to fasten such a perverse opinion on an entire church—indeed, on so many praiseworthy churches throughout Christendom—to make them suspect and foul-smelling everywhere? Small is the love Bertius bears our churches, if it is love at all.

<!-- B022 -->
> Fifthly, it follows that there are some sins that cannot be washed away with Christ's most holy blood, and this not because of the greatness of the offence, but because of the offender's state, etc. {5. Pag.73.l.18}

<!-- B023 -->
**Answer.** Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, and all the other sins of the impenitent, cannot be washed away with Christ's most holy blood; and this not because of the greatness of the offence, but because of the offender's state. Is that absurd? I should like to hear that demonstrated with reasons. {Matt.12.31}

<!-- B024 -->
> Sixthly, it follows that an entire particular congregation can lapse into apostasy more easily than a single truly believing person. For a particular congregation, indeed an entire nation, can depart altogether, but someone absolutely elected cannot, etc. {6. pag.73. l. 14}

<!-- B025 -->
**Answer.** Those who are only in the covenant of grace can fall away more easily than those who are also in grace itself. All the regenerate are indeed in grace, whereas of an entire congregation, in which there is chaff and grain, nothing else can be said than that it is in the covenant of grace. Thus a congregation, considered generally in this way, can lapse into apostasy more readily and sooner than a single regenerate person. For the more someone is enlightened, the harder he is to lead astray, says Bertius. {pag.123 l.7}

Nevertheless, properly speaking, no entire congregation can fall away either: at least, not from true faith, but only from the profession of faith. For no one can fall except someone who stands; and whoever stands in faith itself never falls out of it, as has been demonstrated in the preceding first part. That is the good crop, the {Matth. 3 12 en cap. 13.30} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M29">Marginal translation: Matth. 3 12 and chapter 13.30.</span> good wheat, which will be gathered into heaven's barn. But those who stand only in the profession of faith can fall from that profession and also from that faith, when God sends such people the operation of errors because they have not received the truth so as to be saved, {2 Thess. 2.10} in order that they may believe the lie. Thus Jerusalem fell; thus all those churches fell that the apostles themselves planted, so that where Christians formerly lived, Turks and heathens now live. Yet the true Christians did not therefore become Turks. The same congregation does fall, but not the same persons.

<!-- B026 -->
> {7. pag. 74 l.4} Seventhly, it follows that baptism does not certainly assure all the children of believers of God's grace, since among the children are some who have been absolutely reprobated from eternity by an antecedent decree of God; and that believers must therefore doubt the truth of God's covenant: “I am your God and the God of your seed after you.”

<!-- B027 -->
**Answer.** This might suit Bertius if he were disputing predestination; but what has it to do with perseverance? Yet it seems to have been all the same to him wherever he drew from, provided he could strike us with it. As for the last demand, baptism can assure members of the covenant and their children of no more than God has promised in his covenant. But in his covenant God has not absolutely promised all covenant members and all their children that he would bestow on them his grace, that is, forgiveness of sins, the Spirit of regeneration, and eternal life. For if this had been promised in that way, God would also have to do it, since he is always truthful in his promises. But he does not do it: for many covenant members baptized in youth or in later years are condemned, as is well known.

Therefore baptism cannot certainly assure all the baptized of God's grace either. Consequently believers cannot and may not believe infallibly—that is, as certainly as they believe the articles of their faith—that God will forgive all their baptized children their sins for Christ's sake, govern them through his Spirit and finally save them. Rather, they may, can and must only firmly hope for this, since love commands it and God does not forbid it.

Each parent may hope for his own children that, although God knows as soon as a person is born—indeed, has known from eternity—whether that person is elect or reprobate, the parent’s children will not be numbered among the reprobate, and that baptism will thus seal to the child not only the covenant of grace but grace itself. There is room for this hope or trust both among those who affirm unconditional predestination and among those who hold the opposite view. For who can know for certain whether God foreknew that his child would become a believer? No one. Thus no one can know for certain either that his little child is assured through baptism that God will certainly give him the grace of the covenant—that is, forgiveness of sins, the Spirit of regeneration, and eternal life.[^baptism]

<!-- B028 -->
> Eighthly, it follows that the cause of falling away and ultimate despair must be attributed not to voluntary choice, but to the compulsion of an external disposition, etc. {8.} {[unreadable citation]}

<!-- B029 -->
**Answer.** The cause, if strictly speaking one may call it that, of someone's falling away or not being converted is that he has not received from God the power to remain standing or to turn himself to God. Yet God could have given that person this power, had he wished to give it to him. And why did he not wish to give it to him? Because he has not elected such a person to this, we say. Is that now absurd? Let it then be shown that anyone was ever converted or remained standing who had not received such power; and that anyone ever failed to be converted or fell away to whom God had given such power.

To say that God has given many people sufficient power to be able to stand and turn themselves to him, but that people did not remain standing or become converted because they were unwilling, does not bear on this matter. For sufficient power to be able to stand and turn oneself to God is not yet sufficient power actually to turn oneself to God and actually to remain standing. No one turns himself to God or remains standing except someone who can and wishes to do so. Does it now rest with God to give a person only the ability? And does the person have that willing—not simply willing, but willing that good—by and from himself, or in his own power? That must be demonstrated. I say that both the ability and the willing come from God.

“Cain fell away,” {Philip. 2.13.} says Bertius. Why? “Because he did not wish to remain standing,” he will say. But why did he not wish this? I shall ask. Bertius likewise asks, “Why did he not come?” God did not give him this will. Well! Why did he not give him that will, when he had nevertheless, according to Bertius's opinion, given him the ability, and knew well that if he did not also give him a will, he would nevertheless not remain standing? God did not wish to give him this will. Why, then? Here I should now like to hear what Bertius will say, for he does not wish the decree God made concerning Cain to be assigned as its cause. And nevertheless it is certain that God's will, through which Cain does not receive this willing, is God's antecedent and not his consequent will, as they are commonly called.

<!-- B030 -->
For on account of what preceding work did God not wish to give Cain the will to persevere? On account of some sins of his? That would have to be shown. And how could Cain have refrained from sinning if he did not wish to refrain? Or how could he have wished to refrain from sinning if God had not given him the will to refrain?

Perhaps someone will say that Cain deserved to be deprived of this grace through the sin he committed in Adam. But why, then, did God not deprive all people of this grace? For all people sinned in Adam. God was not obliged to give Cain this will. That is true. But was he obliged to give it to so many other people? And was he obliged to give Cain the ability? Why, then? So that, by not doing what he could, he might be punished more justly and also more severely. Was that God's intention and purpose when he gave him the ability, or rather that he should turn himself to God and remain standing? Well, why did God not also give him the willing, so that he might thus do that for which he had now given him the ability? Did God wish Cain to remain standing, and then not give him that without which he would not remain standing? Did God wish Cain to be converted, and not himself convert Cain? Or did God wish only that Cain should be able to turn himself to him and able to remain standing, not that he should actually turn himself to him and remain standing as well? How, then, did Cain still have to do what God did not wish him to do? For indeed, if God wished and decreed that Cain should be converted and remain standing, I do not see how it came about that Cain was not converted, since God's decree cannot be left undone and unfulfilled. But if God wished only that Cain should be able to turn himself to him, then Cain had to be able to do so, but did not have to do so in actual fact. For God did not wish that, since he gave him only sufficient ability, but not also a willing, without which it was nevertheless impossible that he should actually turn himself to God.

“But,” Bertius will say, “then God is the sole cause of all evil, of impenitence, of falling away, etc.” Answer. God does no evil, nor does he command evil or force anyone to evil. Rather, the immediate cause of evil is the person's evil will and perversity. God did not create that evil will; rather, the person acquired it through his own sin. Nor does God govern or move this evil will to will; innate wickedness does that. Thus a person must blame himself alone for all the evil he does. Meanwhile, this too is true: wicked people, willing and doing evil, would not will and do that same evil if it pleased God to work in their will as he works in the regenerate when, tempted by desire, they nevertheless do not carry out the evil. For they have this not from nature but from grace.

“I deny,” Bertius says further, {pag.74 l.28} “that repentance is commanded or can be commanded of a person who is reprobated by God's absolute will.” Then let it be commanded of a person reprobated by God's conditional will: that is, a person whom God has reprobated because he foreknew that he would not turn himself to him, and who would not do so because he—God—would not give him the power to turn himself to him. Does that remove the whole difficulty Bertius raises here? By no means. Let discerning people compare the one with the other and weigh everything maturely; they too, I think, will say so. What Bertius further adds here at the end, {Pag 75.ll.8.}

<!-- B031 -->
> that according to our opinion the reprobate are condemned because they do not persevere in the good that they do not have; secondly, because they do not have the true good, which an eternal and immovable decree prevents them from having; thirdly, because they do the evil that they cannot avoid—indeed, to do which they are ordained by God's counsel:

<!-- B032 -->
by this he greatly wrongs us. For we say that God has ordained no one to do evil, but that he wished to leave some people lying in their sins, into which they had fallen through their own free will. To these people, now by nature inclined to commit sin, he would not give the grace by which they would escape such dominion of sins; thus by sinning they would also bring eternal condemnation upon themselves.

Accordingly no one is condemned because he does not have the true good or does not persevere in it—if we are to speak properly of the cause that merits condemnation—but because he does evil: not the evil to which he is ordained, for God ordains no one to evil, but ordains evil to a good end; rather, the evil from which he is not ordained to be delivered and freed. He cannot avoid it because he has, as it were, sold and given himself captive under evil through his own free will.

One must distinguish well the decree from its execution, and the causes of both, as well as the causes themselves from one another. One must not call something a cause that is no cause, or a meriting cause that is no meriting cause, or an efficient cause that is only a non-efficient cause and consequently a cause improperly so called. All this can be understood and gathered from what has been related above.

<!-- B033 -->
> Ninthly, it follows that believers may well be without fear and care, for they are out of danger and assured of their outcome; {9. Pag.75.l.35} and that, even if they are careful, they achieve nothing in the world by their care, etc.

<!-- B034 -->
{1. Petr. 5.8. Ephes. 6.12.} **Answer.** Believers are not beyond all danger; therefore they must fear and be careful. But about what? That they may not fall, may not sin, may not anger God, may not grieve the Holy Spirit, and may bring no sorrow or any chastisements upon themselves, etc. This care of theirs is not useless or in vain. For by fearing and taking care in this way they watch all the more, pray, walk circumspectly, and avoid sins—indeed all opportunities and inducements to sin, etc.

Meanwhile they are nevertheless also assured of their outcome, that is, their salvation, knowing it is impossible for the elect to be lost, since God's decree is unchangeable and inviolable. {Rom. 8.16. Eph. 1.13/14 Joan. 3.36. Galat. 5.24. In cap. 2.20.} They also know they are elect, since God's Spirit bears witness with their spirit that they are God's children, and that in them there is a living faith in Jesus Christ, together with an unfeigned desire to love God and their neighbour, hate sins, and crucify and mortify the flesh. And not only that, but they also find that they have died to sins. They actually do good works and refrain from evil works, increasing daily in this desire and deed; in short, they are so disposed in their hearts and conduct themselves so in their outward life as befits good-natured, well-loved, obedient children. {1. Joan. 3.9/ 10. Rom. 8.12.} See more on this subject in our answer to Bertius's fourth proof in the first part.

<!-- B035 -->
> {10. Pag 77. l.7} Tenthly, it follows that in the regenerate, as long as they struggle here, there is no power for both ultimate and mutually opposed ends; or, if there is, that such power never comes into operation. From this it follows that they are as though in the state of glory, etc.

<!-- B036 -->
**Answer.** If Bertius understands by these ultimate ends perseverance and falling away, or condemnation and salvation, then I say that in the regenerate there is no power for both these ultimate ends. And why is it absurd to say that in the regenerate there is never such power? “Then,” says Bertius, “they are as though in the state of glory.” {Ibid. l.7.} Why, then?

<!-- B037 -->
> For being unable not to sin belongs to those corrupted beyond all hope; being able to sin belongs to Adam before the fall and to the regenerate in the state of struggle; being unable to sin belongs to the glorified and to those who remain victors after the struggle.

<!-- B038 -->
Very fine! Bertius means to say it is absurd that the regenerate should not be able to fall away. For on his reasoning, if they cannot fall away altogether, they are in the state of glory, and may also in this life say to a reprobate—if, that is, they knew him to be one—what stands in Luke: {Ibid. l.13.} {Luc. 16.26.}

<!-- B039 -->
> Between you and us a great gulf has been set, so that those who wish to descend from here to you cannot, nor can those who wish to cross from there to us, etc.

<!-- B040 -->
His argument is as follows.

<!-- B041 -->
Those who are in the state of glory cannot sin.

<!-- B042 -->
The regenerate can sin, for we readily acknowledge that.

<!-- B043 -->
Therefore the regenerate are not in the state of glory.

<!-- B044 -->
Was that what Bertius wished to say? Then he would have done better to remain silent. Nevertheless, he imagines that he is very much in the right, for, he says:

<!-- B045 -->
> If anyone says that the truly regenerate cannot fall away, {Pag. 77. l.22} this must be either because they do not sin, or because they do not sin unto death, or at any rate because their sins are not mortal sins for this reason: they are committed by them, that is, by the elect, and are therefore forgiven them. All this is absurd.

<!-- B046 -->
But here Bertius still forgets what ought to have been added, namely: or because these sins are committed by them—that is, by regenerate, penitent believers—and are therefore forgiven them. For surely it is evident that we are not discussing the elect merely as such, but those elect {Galat. 3. 27. Eph. 4. 24.} who have now put on Christ and the new person. All their sins are forgiven such people, however many and grave they may be, as has often been related before and is well known among all Christians.

<!-- B047 -->
> Eleventhly, it follows that perseverance, faith, righteousness {11. pag. 77. l. 38.} and love are not moral but natural goods. For nature is infused into things by God without their choice. It is a principle of movement in that in which it is, and does not change its form, but is uniform, etc.

<!-- B048 -->
**Answer.** We teach that faith, perseverance, etc. depend on God's predestination, not on human choice, so that no one becomes truly believing or persevering, etc., except the elect alone.

<!-- B049 -->
> “The inward stirring of the Holy Spirit,” says D. Arminius according to Augustine's opinion, {contra Perk: pag. 57. en 58} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M48">Marginal translation: Against Perkins, pages 57 and 58.</span> “is twofold. The first is sufficient, through which a person to whom it is applied obtains the ability to will, believe and turn himself to God.” (That is, it gives a person only the ability to will, to believe him and to turn to God.) “The other is powerful or effectual—*efficax*—through which a person, to whom it is applied, comes to will, believe and turn to God. The first is applied according to the decree of providence, with certain foreknowledge that it will be rejected by the person's free will.” (That is, although this stirring shows that the person can believe, he will nevertheless not believe.) “The second is applied according to the decree of predestination, with certain foreknowledge that the person to whom it is applied will be willing, will believe, will turn himself to God, since it is applied by God in the manner he knows to be most suitable to convert and to move or persuade the person to whom he applies it.”

<!-- B050 -->
In sum, then, a person's obtaining faith, love, righteousness and perseverance does not depend on the person's willing or choosing—although the person nevertheless becomes believing and righteous, loves, perseveres, etc. only willingly. Rather, it depends on that special application of God's grace just related. This application or applied grace not only makes the person able to believe and persevere if he wishes, for then it would still depend on human will and choice. It also makes him wish to believe and persevere, indeed actually believe and persevere.

Bertius now wishes to contradict this teaching of ours, saying that it would follow from it {pag. 78.l.16.} that faith, love, etc. are natural goods, and that predestination would serve the elect as a nature, because the aforementioned goods flow from predestination. He proves this in such a manner that I do not really know whether he seeks to contradict himself or us. For nature, he says, is infused into things by God without their choice. What then? Are love, faith, etc. the nature of the elect? For they are infused without their choice. How so? Are they then natural goods; are they nature itself? And how is predestination then also the nature of the elect? Surely they do not have two kinds of nature in this respect. Or if predestination is their nature, how is this predestination infused into them? It is an unheard-of manner of speaking: predestination is infused into people.

<!-- B051 -->
Further, nature is a principle of movement in that in which {Ibid.l.21.22} it is, says Bertius. But is predestination in the person? Who ever spoke so? And if it is in the person, how was Saul changed into Paul by an external principle? Nature is surely an internal principle, since it is in things, not outside them. And nature does not change its form. How, then, was Saul changed into Paul by nature, that is, by predestination? Surely either Saul was not changed by predestination, or predestination is not Saul's nature, or nature can change its form.

That a fish is a fish, {Ibid.li.4.} it has from God without any choice of its own, says Bertius. And that is true, just as believers also have from God their being believers, without their choice. But is predestination therefore their nature? Then God's providence or good pleasure, by which he ordained these creatures that are now fish to become fish, must also be the nature of fish. And a fish, he says further, does not change into an ox or horse, nor a good fish into a bad one. Is it their nature that does this, or rather the author of nature, who is God? He can make an ox from a fish and a fish again from an ox, but has so determined the nature of fish, as also of all creatures, that they must remain what they are, each according to its kind, having in their own nature no principle by which they could change themselves.

But is this ordinance or determination made by God concerning creatures' nature itself the nature of the creatures? How, then, must predestination be the nature of the elect, even though the elect are, as it were, determined by God's decree to remain what they are? Let us rather say, then, that God could also change his eternal predestination because he can change the nature of things; and that there was no nature in the elect before predestination was infused into them; or, if there had formerly been a nature in them, that predestination has completely now taken it away; or, if not, that there are then two natures in them. And what more? Let us think and speak spiritually of the goods of the Spirit.

> Twelfthly, it follows that the elect cannot die in a mortal sin, and consequently that, continuing always in mortal sins, they will never die. {12. pag.78.l.32.}

<!-- B053 -->
**Answer.** I grant this. But the question is whether the elect can always continue in sins.

<!-- B054 -->
> Thirteenthly, it follows that believers cannot be separated from God's love even by their sins. {13. Ibid.l.35.}

<!-- B055 -->
**Answer.** And I firmly believe that this is true. That does not mean that a believer's sins could never deserve rejection and the loss of God's love. Rather, since believers are always forgiven all sins, and forgiven sin cannot separate us from God, I say that believers—all the regenerate are also believers—cannot be separated from God's love by their sins, which are forgiven them because they believe. And although all believers, having sinned, do not always immediately lament their sin and pray for grace, so that they now neither see nor feel so full an evidence of God's love towards them as they used to feel before, nevertheless faith is not immediately lost through every kind of sin. And so long as anyone believes, he is in Christ; and God loves all people who are in Christ.

Thus a believer, provided he retains faith, even though he sins, nevertheless remains in Christ and in the Father's love. Through this it also happens that God, although now angry with such a person for the deed committed, nevertheless looks on him with gracious eyes for Christ's sake, in whom he is and is loved, so that, recognizing his sin, he might lament it and call upon God for forgiveness.

In this way the Lord Christ looked upon his apostle Peter, who sinned so terribly and who, during all the time he was occupied in committing that sin, did not even reflect on himself and did not let himself be warned by the cock's crowing; {Luc. 22.61.} and Peter remembered the Lord's word, went out and wept bitterly.

So too God deals daily with all believers. Though they have sinned and well deserved to be forsaken by God for first denying him in word or deed, he looks more to the conversion of the sinner and the preservation of those he once gave to Christ for salvation than to the exercise of his just severity against the fallen and guilty. That is sign enough of God's love towards believers when they have sinned against him. The Catechism has been discussed previously.

<!-- B056 -->
> {14. pag.79.l 28.} Fourteenthly, it follows that sins are not only no hindrance to believers' salvation, but even further it.

<!-- B057 -->
**Answer.** We understand this as Augustine, cited by Beza, also understands it: namely, that sin as sin does not further us, but in respect of our thereby {Pag 80.l.3.} becoming all the humbler and better instructed. This has been shown more fully in the answer to the third absurdity.

<!-- B058 -->
> {15. Pa.80.l.32} Fifteenthly and lastly, it follows that perseverance and other goods of the spirit—that is, of the mind—are indeed necessary for the saints, but nevertheless not with a moral necessity, etc.

<!-- B059 -->
**Answer.** No one can do good without preceding grace, and no one does good without accompanying grace. Even if, according to Bertius's opinion, this grace enabling one to do good is [text unreadable] given to those who will be condemned, it is still beyond all doubt that God gives the grace actually to do good to none except those whom he has previously decreed that he wishes to save.[^decree] In other words, saving grace depends on election; or, saving and effectual—*efficax*—grace is applied according to the decree of predestination, as we heard D. Arminius say above. And does Bertius call this absurd?

How, then, does it all depend on human choice? And is only one kind of grace applied by God to all people, for one and the same end or in one and the same way, so that thereafter accepting that grace rests with human will and pleasure, without God needing to give, alongside the ability, also the willing and the accomplishment?[^giving] {Pag.81.l.16}

“But,” says Bertius, “the decree of election produces in the persons and their dispositions an unavoidable compulsion.” Yes, we say, an unavoidable necessity, but no compulsion. For God's decree does not force a person to believe or persevere, as is evident from the manner of executing this decree. For the person believes willingly and perseveres willingly. {Ibid.l.18.}

<!-- B060 -->
> Nevertheless, we are deprived of the choice of the end and of the means leading to it.

<!-- B061 -->
But what need is there for choice when only one means presents itself for attaining the end? Or is one to make some choice of the end itself? Bertius himself knows well that this conflicts with nature and reason. For choice follows deliberation, and both concern only finding means and then taking the best of those found, in order to reach the end one has already actually set before oneself before entering into any deliberation.

“This opinion,” Bertius says further, {Ibid.II.19.} “places an active power in God and a passive power in the person.” That is true. And why not? Where there is an agent, there must be something acted upon. God {Prosper. cont. colla c. 3ᵉ sept avont.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M61">Marginal translation: Prosper, against the collator, chapter 3ᵉ, sept avont..</span> then works in the person; thus the person must surely undergo as much as God works. Yet the person is not always merely acted upon, but also cooperates, namely whenever he willingly does something. {Willende bidden wy, ende nochtans heeft God sijnen Geest in onse hertē gesondē die daer roept Abba, lieve Vader. Willende, sprekē wy maer ist wat goets dat wy spreken, so en zijn wy niet die spreken, maer de geest onses Vaders die in ons spreeckt. Willende werckē wy onse salicheyt: ende nochtans ist God die dat willen ende wercken in ons werct, &c.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M62">Marginal translation: We pray willingly, and yet God has sent his Spirit into our hearts, who cries there, ‘Abba, dear Father.’ We speak willingly; but if what we speak is something good, it is not we who speak, but our Father's Spirit who speaks in us. We willingly work out our salvation, and yet it is God who works that willing and working in us, etc.</span>

For willing is a human work, as is everything a person willingly produces. Believing, persevering and practising righteousness are human works, for the person believes and perseveres, etc., not God. Yet neither does the person do so without God, who works upon him towards good before he moves himself, and who gives him the ability, willing and accomplishment before he can, wills and accomplishes. Thus one can also scarcely understand how a person may be called a co-worker, says Augustine:

<!-- B062 -->
> For someone who is governed acts himself, and is governed for this reason, that he may act rightly. But when someone is impelled, as God's children are, one can scarcely understand that he himself cooperates in anything.

<!-- B063 -->
{Prosp lib. sen. L. ex August. 310.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M63">Marginal translation: Prosper, book of sentences, L., from Augustine, 310.</span> “Secondly, this opinion transfers moral necessity from the person to God,” says Bertius. It does not, I say. For the person seeks to attain his end, that is, salvation, and aims at it as at a target, through necessary means, just as God seeks to attain his end: to save the person to whom he applies the means of salvation in so special a manner. Is that not the truth? Or will one therefore also have to implant a moral necessity in God? Let Bertius take heed.

Moreover, he says, God has the choice here, not the person, for God establishes the subjects. Well! How, then, would Bertius like it? That the subjects should establish themselves? And that the person should become believing and steadfast because he chooses to become believing and steadfast? But where {Rom 8 14. Ps.81.1.23. Ibid.l.9.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M64">Marginal translation: Rom 8 14; Ps.81.1.23; Ibid.l.9.</span> does this good choice come from, and the good deliberation that must precede it? And that which must precede it in turn: that good will to be saved? For without such a desire for salvation as an end, there can surely be no deliberation or choice concerning the means of attaining it.

If a person has all this by nature, God's election rests not on grace but on nature. For then God has elected to eternal life those whom he has foreknown to be naturally inclined towards eternal salvation, and who, therefore driven by that same nature, would search for suitable means of attaining that salvation. Indeed, even if many means of attaining it were to present themselves, they would nevertheless regard and accept Christ as the best means. They would find in themselves, that is, in their nature, the particular power to obtain that means; for deliberation and choice concern only means within our power. But is that not a godless opinion! Let us then say that it is all grace: not only being able to do what has been related, but also doing it in actual fact. And with that, {Ibid.l.31.} everything will go rightly.

“God ordains substances with a natural necessity towards their end,” says Bertius. With a natural necessity: that I deny. It is true that an elect person {Pag 82 l.8} must absolutely be saved: he cannot but persevere; he cannot depart entirely or finally. Yet he is not therefore saved by a natural necessity. For he is saved willingly, as has now been said, and also becomes believing and persevering willingly. Not merely not unwillingly, or with an inclination—*non invitus & sponte*, not unwillingly and of his own accord—but willingly, I say: so that he can also refrain from willing it, if you consider the power and inherent nature of the will, which, being undetermined and free, can will and not will one and the same thing. But it is impossible for the will to do this, though it can do it.

It must therefore be determined to will one of the two, which happens through the virtue or vice that is master in the person who is to exercise his will. Thus, where the Holy Spirit has the upper hand over the understanding and fleshly desires, the will can will good. Where corruption has the upper hand over the understanding, or at least over fleshly desires, the will cannot will any good. And although the will by nature, before its determination, could will good as well as evil, evil as well as good, nevertheless, now thus determined towards evil, it cannot will any good; and, determined towards good, it cannot will any evil or refrain from willing good. This inability does not deprive the will of its freedom, {Aristoteles lib. 5. Nicom. c. 1.} since it willingly wills what it wills, and could have refrained from willing that same thing. Indeed, it would never have willed it had it not been determined to will in this way.

Therefore, although it cannot happen otherwise than that the elect must become believing and steadfast, since God has ordained to work in their understanding and will in such a way that they could not refrain from believing and persevering when he worked thus, nevertheless they could indeed have failed to believe and persevere, and also have been unwilling to believe and become persevering, if we consider the power and nature of their will apart from this ordinance of God.

And I cannot understand that anyone wishes to believe to whom God does not give the willing of this, or before he gives it; or can believe to whom God does not give the ability, or before he gives it; or actually believes to whom God does not actually give faith, or before he gives it; or that he gives this to anyone other than those whom he wishes and has decreed to save; or that he does not give this to someone whom he has nevertheless purposed to save; or that a person would not necessarily have to come to faith and perseverance concerning whom God has made such a decree—that he wished to make him saved, believing and persevering, and would also actually make him so. Yet this is necessary only with respect to the outcome, and by no means with respect to the application and conduct of the means for reaching such an outcome, as has now been said.

Thus this necessity does not deprive the person of moral necessity, nor of deliberation about maintaining the various particular works of whose accumulation perseverance consists, or through whose intervention perseverance is obtained. For it belongs to him to deliberate and decide, says Bertius, to whom it belongs to accomplish; and it belongs to him {Pag. 82. l. 3.} to accomplish for whom it matters that he should not miss his end. All this can truly be said of the elect. But that God, purposing to save some people and applying the means of salvation to them in a particular manner—for no one can deny that God does this—must therefore necessarily also deliberate about perseverance, as Bertius concludes: that is speaking altogether too humanly {Ibid. l. 12.} of God.

What follows is that election is in God actively {Ibid. l. 15.} and in the person passively. Does that not please Bertius? Then let him reverse it and say that election is in the person actively and in God passively, and consequently so is perseverance: so that God perseveres because the person perseveres, and not the person because God perseveres. Will it sound better now? Indeed, much uglier! For God elects and is not elected, whereas the person is elected by God and does not elect himself. The person also perseveres in faith, righteousness and holiness, not God; but because God perseveres in working through his Spirit in the person's heart, so that he not only can persevere or wishes to persevere, but also actually perseveres, therefore the person also perseveres.

As for what is further brought forward here again about {Ibid. l. 35.} the necessity of perseverance, it has now been answered. The statement that operative necessity proceeds thus—“If you wish to be saved, repent and believe the {Pa. 83. l. 5.} gospel”—does not overturn what we said before. For the elect also wish to be saved, and therefore also repent and believe the gospel. But, someone will say, the elect must necessarily be saved, repent and believe. That is true with respect to the outcome; but with respect to the command it does not happen necessarily, for they wish all of it as much as they must; and they do it not because they must, but because they wish to. “With the kind of necessity these people insist upon,” Bertius goes on to say of us, “all boasting is taken away.” {Iac.1.7.} Not all boasting, I say, but the boasting of the flesh is taken away. {1.Cor.1.26.}

> If you wish to say openly, says Bertius, that it is possible for one and the same thing to be both necessary and contingent, then say at the same time that it is possible for one and the same thing to be absolute and conditional, and for one and the same operation to be both God's and the person's.

<!-- B077 -->
But we do not say that one and the same thing would be necessary and contingent. Rather, we distinguish between God's work and the person's work. The person perseveres, believes, prays, watches, gives alms, obeys God, etc. God does not do that. But God gives the person the ability and will to believe, persevere, watch, obey, etc.; the person does not give this to himself.

What God does, he has decreed to do thus, and he decreed it freely, for he could have refrained from decreeing it. But now that he has decreed it, it is necessary that he should actually do it at the appropriate time. What the person does, however, is contingent, for he could have refrained from doing it; indeed, he would never have been able or willing to do it, nor would he ever actually have done it, had God not first worked in him as he decreed to work.

Nevertheless, just as all moral and the most contingent works may also be said to occur necessarily when all the causes from which such a work arises actually operate together and remain unhindered in their operations, so one may indeed say, in this respect and no other, that believers' works occur necessarily, although they occur according to another kind of usage and by their free will, as was also related before.

For example, God enlightens the person's understanding so that he rightly knows his misery and Christ, who can deliver him from it. He then gives him in his will the power to overcome fleshly desires—*appetitus sensuum*, the appetites of the senses—and gladly to accept the known good himself. And because Bertius thinks it so impossible that one should do something freely and nevertheless also do it necessarily in a different respect, I should like to learn from him how it can happen that the angels freely love and obey God and yet cannot refrain from loving and obeying him—as also happens with all those people who are in the state of glory. Enough, then, {Pag.83.l.20.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M87">Marginal translation: page 83, line 20.</span> of the absurdities that Bertius draws from our teaching. Because this happens so much nowadays, we are none the better for it.
