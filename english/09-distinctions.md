<a id="s09-b000"></a>

Bertius next asks us to accept these distinctions as well:

<a id="s09-b001"></a>

> The first is that there are two kinds of falling away, the one particular, the other general, etc. {1. pag.15.li 25}

<a id="s09-b002"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s09-b003"></a>

I grant that this is true. I have no quarrel with his lengthy explanation of this first point on pages 16 and 17. What I cannot bear is the needless concern Bertius shows for pastors when he admonishes them

<a id="s09-b004"></a>

> that, in matters concerning conscience, they neither can nor may unconditionally promise anyone the forgiveness of particular sins on God's behalf, so long as they are not assured of the other person's repentance. {pag.17.li.4.}

<a id="s09-b005"></a>

For what is the point of this? Has there ever been a pastor in the Reformed Church who has acted otherwise? Or did Bertius want simple people to believe that in the Reformed Churches it was customary to promise someone the forgiveness of particular sins without any condition at all, without even being assured of the sinner's repentance? Or at least that they had to do so if they were to speak in conformity with their doctrine of predestination and perseverance? All these claims are lies and slanders. And although I cannot say that Bertius meant it so maliciously, I nevertheless know that there are people who have understood it in that malicious sense. Unless he meant it in the sense just mentioned, which is false, this point does little to explain what he had in mind. I too would rather he had left it unwritten.

<a id="s09-b006"></a>

> The second: either a whole nation or an individual may fall away, etc. {2. pag.18.li 7.}

<a id="s09-b007"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s09-b008"></a>

I grant this too, along with all he says to explain it on pages 18, 19, 20 and 21. One short passage needs a closer look. He writes:

<a id="s09-b009"></a>

> All who depart from the covenant of the whole people also depart from the particular covenant, for such a departure brings with it contempt for God and an absolute rejection of grace. Such was the sin of Cain, Esau, and several others who departed from the Church. {pag.19.l.23}

<a id="s09-b010"></a>

How does Bertius understand this? Does he mean that all who depart from the covenant of the whole people and from the Church also fall away from the grace in which they had come to share? {pag.19.l.4.} That is an error. For there are many in the covenant of grace who are nevertheless not themselves in grace, as Bertius says: Cain, Esau, the first wicked world, and the like, of whom the Apostle speaks:

<a id="s09-b011"></a>

> They have gone out from us, but they were not of us. {1. Joan.2.19}

<a id="s09-b012"></a>

Everyone with whom God makes the covenant belongs to the covenant of grace. But only those who fulfil the conditions the covenant requires obtain the grace it promises. Whoever rejects those conditions therefore falls away both from the covenant of that people and from the particular covenant. For God makes the covenant not only with the people as a whole but also with each person who belongs to it. Whoever falls away from the covenant in this way also falls away from grace. He does not fall from grace he has received—unless we call membership in the covenant of grace itself a grace, as indeed it is. {2. Corin.6.1} {1. Petr.2.10.} Rather, he falls from the grace he was promised he would receive and enjoy if he did his duty. It is this grace we mean when we say the regenerate cannot fall away from grace. Mark this distinction well: we shall need it again.

<a id="s09-b013"></a>

> The third: sins are of two kinds. The first occur through a sudden disturbance, the others with a deliberate mind, whether a person thinks he is doing right or knows well that he is doing wrong, etc. {3. Pag.22.l.2.}

<a id="s09-b014"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s09-b015"></a>

I cannot let this pass, any more than I can let what follows pass. First, these two classes do not account for all sins. Second, not all his descriptions of particular kinds of sin are adequate. Why does Bertius not rather retain the division and description of sins now ordinarily presented in all the schools? Such is his fondness for novelty and [word unclear]. But let us examine the matter itself. Sins, he says, are of two kinds. {Pag.22.l.2.}

First, he leaves out sin committed through ignorance. For although Bertius does mention a kind of ignorance here, he understands by it only ignorance or lack of knowledge of the means of pleasing God, whereas people often sin through ignorance in other ways too, as will be said later. He should therefore have presented this differently and more clearly. {ibid.lin 24}

Second, he leaves out sin committed through weakness. For even supposing that the regenerate do not sin through weakness, as Bertius teaches, though without reason, the unregenerate can nevertheless at least sin through weakness, doing what they know to be evil and what they {Pag.15.l.19} would gladly leave undone if only they could.

Third, he leaves out the sin a person commits wholeheartedly, making no effort, either by reason or by the Spirit, to resist his passions. Once he has committed it, he does not lament it, then or later. Bertius makes no mention of this, yet it is entirely necessary to pay close attention to it, especially in this question. Now, as for the description of sins that Bertius offers here:

<a id="s09-b016"></a>

> Sin committed through being taken by surprise, he says, is when a person is overtaken by desire before consulting the Spirit or reason and preparing himself for battle, doing what he had not intended. Such is Peter's sin. {pag.22.lin 8}

<a id="s09-b017"></a>

What? Was Peter taken by surprise? Surely Christ had warned him beforehand. {Petrus heeft uyt swackeit ghesondicht seyt doct. Arminius disput.8. thes.5.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M14">[Marginal translation: Peter sinned through weakness, says Dr Arminius, disput.8. thes.5.]</span>

<a id="s09-b018"></a>

> I tell you, Peter, the cock will not crow tonight before you have three times denied me, saying that you do not know me. {Luc.22.34} [The Dutch has both “denied” and “that you do not know me”; their connection is unclear.]

<a id="s09-b019"></a>

When Peter had denied Christ once, the cock crowed and Peter went out. {Marc.14.c8} About an hour afterwards he denied him again. {Luc.22.59.} Is that being taken by surprise? It is true that nothing was further from Peter's intention than denying his Lord when he followed him at a distance to the house of Caiaphas. {Mat.26.58} Yet he had already denied Christ twice and stayed another whole hour in Caiaphas's house. I cannot believe he never once thought what he would do if someone asked him about Christ again. {pag.23.l.23.} And why may one not equally well say that Peter sinned against his conscience? {die uyt swacheyt sondiget, can ooc geseyt wordẽ te sondighen tegẽ de conscientie, &c, segt doct. Arminius disp 8 thes.6} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M20">[Marginal translation: Whoever sins through weakness can also be said to sin against conscience, etc., says Dr Arminius, disp 8 thes.6.]</span> For he sinned willingly and knowingly, since no one sins against his will, as Bertius acknowledges. {Pag.79.li 8} And that Peter knew well that he was denying Christ, and likewise that he ought not to deny Christ, is beyond all doubt.

Moreover, Peter consented to the desires of the flesh and let himself be overcome by them, not doing what the Spirit or reason commanded him. For reason and the Spirit were in Peter before his fall; that is certain. Now, if this reason and the Spirit did not work in Peter during that whole hour after the first denial, then Peter lost the Spirit before the first denial, which Bertius says is false. And if he retained the Spirit, and the Spirit worked repentance in him for his previous sin, also admonishing and commanding him not to deny Christ again, then Peter consented to the desires of the flesh and sinned against his conscience when he denied Christ the third time. Peter's example, then, does not illustrate sin committed by surprise. Nor is the definition adequate: our desires can take us by surprise and lead us to do what we know is evil. {pa.22.lin.19} That is, [the wording is unclear whether one can or cannot sin both by surprise and against conscience].

Therefore I adhere to the division of sins generally accepted and used by all ancient and modern teachers of the Church, namely, that some sins are committed through ignorance, some through weakness, and some through unrestrained wickedness. {De sonde geschiedt oft uyt onwetenheyt oft uyt swackheyt, oft uyt boosheyt, oft uyt onachtichheyt, Siet Jac. Arminium. disp.8. thes.1. ende sijn boeck teghen Perkinsum. pag 158. tot 165.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M23">[Marginal translation: Sin is committed through ignorance, weakness, wickedness, or carelessness. See James Arminius, disp.8. thes.1., and his book against Perkins, pages 158 to 165.]</span>

<a id="s09-b020"></a>

Through ignorance: first, when someone does something evil which he thinks is good, or leaves undone something good which he thinks is evil. Rom.10.2. 1.Timoth.1.13. Second, when someone does something he knows to be evil, but does not know that he is doing it. Genes.19.33. and cap.38.16. Actor.23.5. Levit.4.2/13/22. This class includes sins committed through carelessness or surprise, which we recognize only after we have committed them. Levit.5.4. Galat.6.1. Third, when someone does an evil which he does not know to be evil. Actor.17.30. Ephes.5.8.

<a id="s09-b021"></a>

Through weakness: when someone reluctantly does what he knows to be evil. Whether he deliberates long or briefly, the world's enticements and bluster, or the fear of sorrow and hardship, overcome him. Matth.26.70. Roman.7.22/23. 2.Samuel11.4. 1.Reg.11.4. Even the most godly regenerate children of God can fall into all these kinds of sins. But since after such sins they nevertheless retain God's Spirit, as Bertius acknowledges, they do not fall away from grace through these sins. Rather, through the words of the Spirit who remains in them, and through other divine admonitions and brotherly consolation, they are helped back onto the right path and brought to their senses.

<a id="s09-b022"></a>

Through unrestrained wickedness: when a person does evil wholeheartedly and afterwards feels no remorse. Roman.1.32. Ephes.4.19. The regenerate can never fall so far. 1.Joan.3.9. etc.

I have spoken briefly and plainly to explain what Bertius thought it necessary to put before the main body of his arguments and proofs. In this way he meant to prepare his way the better and reach his goal unhindered. What he has accomplished will be plain from what follows, though it is easy enough to see from what has gone before.

But now to the main business: we must attack his arguments one by one. See what a host of invincible warriors Bertius leads against the truth and against us, drawn up in fine order of battle in seven distinct regiments! {Pa.102 l.36} So highly does he rate this company and its strength that he thinks no one would dare face them or even look at them from afar. Time and the outcome will, I hope, tell a very different story.

<a id="s09-b023"></a>

> We shall bring forward two kinds of arguments, says Bertius: from Scripture and from reason. As for Scripture, it is so clear and forceful, and so consistently upholds this doctrine, that I am utterly astonished that anyone who reads it could ever have thought otherwise. But once absolute predestination had been established, it became necessary to thrust the whole of Scripture out of its place in order to uphold that principal doctrine. Now that this doctrine is properly understood, this dispute can readily and easily be settled. {Pag.24.V.2.}

<a id="s09-b024"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s09-b025"></a>

The more proudly and boldly Bertius now speaks, the more shameful it will be for him in the end, when children will be able to perceive that neither Scripture nor reason serves in the least to confirm his purpose. Wise people do not hail the catch before they have it in the net. [The precise cry is unclear.] But it seems Bertius imagines that he is free to do all this and that it suits him well; perhaps this will indeed be one of the chief articles of this new reformation. Still, I wish Bertius had spared himself a little. He professes astonishment that anyone who reads Scripture could ever have thought otherwise than he now does. He also says that people wrench the whole of Scripture out of place to uphold absolute predestination. What else can this mean but that he was once so worthy a man that he either did not read Scripture or overturned it? For while his late father-in-law D. Kuchlinus was alive, he defended and upheld, just as we do, the very doctrine of absolute predestination that now disgusts him. But that cannot be: I am mistaken. For Bertius, as he himself at least says,

<a id="s09-b026"></a>

> is a child reared by the Churches of God, instructed in Holy Scripture from his childhood under his parents' guidance, and by his own choice has embraced those same studies with great fervour and earnest diligence of mind. {In de voorreden lett.A 3.lin.15.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M26">[Marginal translation: In the preface, signature A 3, line 15.]</span>

<a id="s09-b027"></a>

So he will undoubtedly have read Holy Scripture well too. But why, then, did he not at least think about this matter as he now thinks? And since he has always praised

<a id="s09-b028"></a>

> an upright and candid mind, hostile to Jacob's guile, {Ibid.li.13.}

<a id="s09-b029"></a>

why did he then call abominations what he now calls truth, and truth what he now calls abominations? {In den brief aen Jan Utenbogaert, [onleesbare verwijzing] 1608.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M28">[Marginal translation: In the letter to Jan Utenbogaert, [unreadable reference] 1608.]</span> {Leest doch ooc eens Bertij lijk oratie over D. Arminium, en verghelijct de met de afgelogene brief.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M29">[Marginal translation: Do read Bertius’s funeral oration on Dr Arminius as well, and compare it with the disavowed letter.]</span> This is what so astonishes him now. I am not astonished at all. As everyone knows, the time had not yet come to speak as people do now. The Church first had to have respite from its external enemies before the good God was willing to allow it to be set ablaze from within in this way, and these restless spirits to reveal themselves, to trouble the right-minded [possibly: put them to the test] and to mislead the credulous, who cannot endure sound doctrine but heap up teachers for themselves according to their own desires, as their ears itch. {2.Tim.4.3.}

This, it seems, is what our Bertius too has aimed at. He knows very well how to seize his moment and make use of this fine opportunity: it is good fishing in troubled water. Indeed, someone who has little at home himself must get it from someone else in this way. No doubt Bertius also thinks so highly of himself in this that perhaps this is why he wonders so greatly—though he does not dare say it—that not everyone also casts his net here.

Be that as it may, let all the world know this, without boasting on our part: we have so far read Holy Scripture, and by God's grace hope to read it henceforth, with as much care and reflection, in the fear of God, as Bertius or any of these new evangelists and reformers ever has [the end of the comparison is unclear]. We have also always striven, and hope to strive to the end of our lives, to uphold Holy Scripture and not overturn it, as Bertius and his following overturn it, twisting and distorting it into a perverse sense. Thus we too, if saying it were all that was needed, would be able and entitled, with good reason and an upright conscience, to say that we are utterly astonished that Bertius thought as he does about the falling away of the regenerate. But words like these fill no sacks.
