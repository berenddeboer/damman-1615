<a id="s08-b000"></a>

But let us now also go further and examine the reasons that Bertius puts forward in such great numbers to defend his Apostasy. They are handsomely adorned and dressed up, it is true; but once the breath of God's mouth passes over them, it will become apparent that they are nothing but a whore's face-paint. They shine like diamonds, but when tested against Holy Scripture they will shatter like glass.

<a id="s08-b001"></a>

> {Colos.2.8.} Take care that there is no one who robs you through human wisdom and empty deception.

<a id="s08-b002"></a>

But to the point. Bertius complains {Pa.102.l.32}

<a id="s08-b003"></a>

> that there are some who, out of the whole multitude, have attacked a few of these same arguments, and here have laid ambushes for them as for scattered soldiers, and fallen upon them, plundering now one and then another, whom they would have feared even to face from afar had they stood in battle order under their banners.

<a id="s08-b004"></a>

It sounds as though you hear Goliath or Rabshakeh boasting. {1. Samu.18.} {Esai.37.} At any rate, it is certain that Bertius has spirit and courage enough in him, and that he would rather blow hard than burn himself. Nevertheless, we who live here on the frontiers and are somewhat more accustomed than Bertius to seeing the enemy are not, thank God, so quickly dismayed or frightened by loud shouting. To demonstrate this, in the name of the God of hosts we will attack Bertius's whole army and its prepared battle formations: first engage his vanguard, or advance guard; then the main body of his army; and finally also strike at his rear guard. The Lord, who has helped us this far, will without doubt continue to assist us. Amen.

<a id="s08-b005"></a>

{Pag. 11. li. 6.} To come, then, to the first troops: through them Bertius sends us word that he will ask for nothing unless we first concede eight points to him, all serving his advantage. {pag. 46. li. 1.} Indeed, he gives us sufficiently clear notice afterwards that, whether we wish it or not, he thinks we must concede all eight. But notwithstanding this, let us hear the articles themselves.

<a id="s08-b006"></a>

> {pa.11.lin.9.} First, we request, says Bertius, that it be conceded to us that there is no absolute election or reprobation. {Pa. 46. li. 6.} And if anyone disputes this, we are ready to wrest this concession from him by the force of proofs and the cogency of arguments, even against his will.

<a id="s08-b007"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b008"></a>

What are we to do here? This man speaks with his knife drawn. Yet if he were as easily taken in by mere words as he supposes us to have been taken in by them, it would present no great difficulty. For then I could simply say:

<a id="s08-b009"></a>

> We request that it be conceded to us that there is no election or reprobation other than an absolute one; and if anyone disputes this, we are ready to wrest this concession from him by the force of proofs and the cogency of arguments, even against his will.

<a id="s08-b010"></a>

That would be shouting into the woods just as Bertius shouts out of them. But it would still be mere shouting: saying a thing does not settle it. If Bertius regarded absolute election as the chief fortress we had, why does he demand its surrender without having displayed any force before it? Or does he think our mother never taught us to say no? Why does he not demand that we concede to him that the regenerate can fall wholly and utterly away from their faith and salvation? In that way he would indeed achieve his aim without a blow or thrust. But it must not go that way. We do not grant you this request at all, Master Peter; and if you have any power to compel us to it, do your best by all means: we are well disposed towards you. We will withhold it from you as long as we can, with God and a good conscience; if we can no longer do so, we shall not be ashamed to surrender ourselves to God and the truth. Yet I am not a little astonished that, in these four years in which the book on Apostasy has been in print, Bertius has not attempted to storm this absolute election, to strengthen his assertion and this book of his further, and thus deprive us of our greatest stronghold. No doubt he had his reasons for thinking this wise.

But why does he call this decree, or this election, an absolute election? Is it because, in the person whom he elects, God has no regard to any condition for which, or with which, he elects him? Then one ought to distinguish between the person and the decree made concerning the person, and not call the decree absolute or unconditional because the person is without a condition. Or is this decree absolute because God has purposed and decreed to bring this or that person to salvation without any condition? We reject that: they must believe in Christ and bear good fruit; if they sin, they must repent. They must thus persevere in faith and godliness to the end. Or is it an absolute decree because God has elected only some, and not others, in such a way that only those who are elected will and can believe and remain steadfast in faith, while those who are not elected neither will nor can believe or remain steadfast in faith? This we concede. [The wording is unclear: Damman may instead be asking what Bertius concedes.] And does Bertius say there is no such absolute decree?

<a id="s08-b011"></a>

Nevertheless, Scripture teaches us—and Bertius accepts it as the truth—{Eph.2.8.} that faith is a gift of God and that God does not give faith to everyone; likewise, that the ability to persevere, and perseverance itself, are also a gift of God, and that God does not give these to everyone either. {Pag.9.lin.5} Likewise, that in time God does nothing, nor does he do it in any other manner, except what he has decreed from eternity to do in that manner. Thus God gives faith or perseverance to no one except those to whom he has decreed from eternity to give them. And since he does not give them to everyone, but only to some, it must necessarily follow that God has also decreed from eternity to give faith and perseverance, and consequently salvation, not to everyone but only to some. {Dit zijn oock Arminij woorden / in sijn [onleesbare verwijzing]} <span class='marginal-translation' id='s08-m11'>Marginal translation: These are also Arminius's words, in his [reference unreadable].</span>

The only question here, then, is who these ‘some’ are. And indeed, if they are not simply those whom God wills, whom he has elected for this from eternity, by grace, according to his good pleasure, but are those who themselves will it and themselves choose the good and grace offered to them, then, considering the matter thoroughly, in all seriousness, with an upright conscience, as before God, I cannot, with all my five senses, understand how Bertius is not an outright Pelagian, placing the cause of election in the human being and not in God. For this reason I heartily wish that Bertius had left aside these other pious leaps and questions that no one disputes, and had treated this matter of absolute and conditional election at least briefly, but clearly, since so very much depends upon it. {Siet hier van wtgheleght int tweede deel deses boecks.} <span class='marginal-translation' id='s08-m12'>Marginal translation: See this explained in the second part of this book.</span>

<a id="s08-b012"></a>

The second thing that Bertius stipulates beforehand is {2. pa.11.lin.14}

<a id="s08-b013"></a>

> that the regenerate have flesh and spirit within them, etc.

<a id="s08-b014"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b015"></a>

I gladly concede this, on condition that it be added that in the regenerate the flesh has been crucified. See Rom. 6.2 / 6.7.11. and cap.8.2. Galat. 5.24.25. and cap. 6.14. Coloss.2.11.

<a id="s08-b016"></a>

The third: {3. Ibid.li.23.}

<a id="s08-b017"></a>

> that the gifts of the Spirit are given (namely, to the regenerate) so that with them they may first fight and then conquer, etc.

<a id="s08-b018"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b019"></a>

It is true. God would do his dear children little good if they could fight but not conquer. The same would be true if he gave them the ability to fight and conquer but did not also give them the victory itself after the struggle. See Rom. 8.37. 1. Joan. 2.13. and cap. 4.4. and cap. 5.4.5.

<a id="s08-b020"></a>

The fourth:

<a id="s08-b021"></a>

> that the gifts of the Spirit can be strengthened, increased and multiplied. {4. pag. 12. lin. 9}

<a id="s08-b022"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b023"></a>

Not only can they be strengthened, but in the regenerate they are also actually strengthened daily. See 2. Cor. 3.18. and cap. 10.15. Ephes. 4.15. Col. 1.10. Psal 84.8. Matth. 25.28.

<a id="s08-b024"></a>

The fifth:

<a id="s08-b025"></a>

> That sins can increase. {5. ibid.lin.22.}

<a id="s08-b026"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b027"></a>

In the regenerate sin cannot increase, but diminishes daily; yet in such a way that this diminishing sin also attacks them daily and does lead them into particular transgressions, sometimes less, sometimes more, according to how much they are on their guard. See Roman. 6.6.11.18. and cap. 8.2. As for what Bertius relates here from Matth. 13.12. and Apocal. 20.13., it is not to the point. For neither the person who does wrong and is defiled nor the person who does not have can be regarded as regenerate. 1. Joan. 3.8.9.

<a id="s08-b028"></a>

> He who commits sin is of the devil; but whoever is born of God commits no sin.

<a id="s08-b029"></a>

The sixth:

<a id="s08-b030"></a>

> That the grace of the Spirit is more powerful than the flesh, etc. {6. pag.12.l.27.}

<a id="s08-b031"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b032"></a>

I concede that. But what follows—{lin.35.}

<a id="s08-b033"></a>

> That these gifts of the Holy Spirit, just as at first they are not received except with the will, so also afterwards do not work except with that same will—

<a id="s08-b034"></a>

I do not understand sufficiently. For it seems as though the gifts of the Spirit are being separated from the human will, as though the will with which one receives the gifts of the Spirit were not itself also a gift of the Holy Spirit. No one will readily say this except someone who has been to school with Pelagius; for this Pelagius used to teach:

<a id="s08-b035"></a>

> that God's assistance and grace do not reach the will, but only the understanding;

<a id="s08-b036"></a>

as Bertius himself attests and relates in his contested disputation on the heresies of Pelagius and Caelestius. {litt.c iij.lin.7.} It is true that the human will is not from the Holy Spirit, but from nature. Nor does anyone do anything unless he wills to do it. The will is also entirely free. But meanwhile this too must be kept in view: it is one thing to speak of willing, and another to speak of willing what is good or evil. Willing, as has been said, we have by nature; willing the good, we have by grace; willing evil, we have from corruption. {Augustijn lib. de gratia, & libero arbitrio c. 16. Fulgent. Epist 6. ad Theod. c. 7. & 8.} <span class='marginal-translation' id='s08-m20'>Marginal translation: Augustine, book On Grace and Free Choice, chapter 16; Fulgent., Letter 6 to Theod., chapters 7 and 8.</span> And this corruption is not only in the understanding but also in the affections and desires, so that it often happens that a person does not do the good that he knows and praises, because the aforesaid evil affections prevail over reason and over the will. The Holy Spirit, then, first enlightens the understanding of those to whom he is given; and not only that, but he also makes the will so powerful that it follows the enlightened understanding and puts to death and overcomes the works of the flesh (that is, those evil desires). Thus it is indeed true that the gifts of the Holy Spirit are not received, nor do they subsequently work, except with the will; but in such a way that the Holy Spirit also works such power in the will. See Roman. 7. 19/23. Philip. 2. 13. This must be carefully attended to.

<a id="s08-b037"></a>

The seventh: {7. pag.13.li.11.}

<a id="s08-b038"></a>

> That the regenerate can act from both the aforesaid principles, namely flesh and Spirit, etc.

<a id="s08-b039"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b040"></a>

They can do this, and can do so as long as they are in this life; but they cannot do both equally well, for the Spirit is master in them, as was said before. See 1. Joan. 3. 9.

<a id="s08-b041"></a>

The eighth: {8. Ibid.lin.26.}

<a id="s08-b042"></a>

> That the gifts of the Spirit and his equipment or instruments are sufficient for victory, if anyone simply uses them, etc.

<a id="s08-b043"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="s08-b044"></a>

It is true; and likewise, if anyone does not use these gifts, he himself is overcome, as we see happen daily, even to the most upright and godly. For what King Solomon says, 1. Reg. 8. 46., and St James, c. 3. 2., is also to be understood of them. But why will Bertius not allow us to say that the sins of the regenerate occur through weakness? {pag15.l.19.} Would it then be more fitting to say that they occur through strength or perfection? Christ says to his apostles: {Mat.26.41}

<a id="s08-b045"></a>

> The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak.

<a id="s08-b046"></a>

And Paul—or God to Paul: {2.Cor12.9.}

<a id="s08-b047"></a>

> My power is brought to fulfilment in weakness.

<a id="s08-b048"></a>

The ancient and modern teachers of the Church generally speak in the same way. And Bertius himself: {pag.112.l.7}

<a id="s08-b049"></a>

> The reason that believers can fall away is the weakness that is in human beings.

<a id="s08-b050"></a>

These, then, are not weaknesses of the Spirit, but weaknesses of believers, who consist of spirit and flesh. {Siet pa.123 lin.7.} <span class='marginal-translation' id='s08-m27'>Marginal translation: See page 123, line 7.</span> The more of the Spirit someone has, the stronger he is and the less he sins; the less of the Spirit someone has, the weaker he is and the more easily he sins. Bertius himself will not be able to deny this.

<a id="s08-b051"></a>

That, then, is our answer to Bertius’s opening advance.
