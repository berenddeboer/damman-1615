But let us come a little closer to the matter itself and try to unravel Bertius’s argument. He writes as follows: {pag.47.l.31}

<!-- B002 -->
> They have devised three evasions. First, the faith of those who fall away was only a feigned faith, so they were not righteous but hypocrites. Second, Scripture promises the saints much on condition that they do not abandon faith, righteousness, and holiness; from such conditional propositions or statements no categorical or firm conclusion can be drawn. Third, some people’s faith is only temporary and therefore not true faith.

<!-- B003 -->
## Answer.

<!-- B004 -->
It is no wonder Bertius calls it an evasion when we distinguish between interpretations of Holy Scripture and between the senses in which a word can be understood. He knew no better. But he does us a great injustice when he imagines that we try to apply all three explanations indiscriminately to every passage of Scripture he wrongly cites. If he does not do this against his own conscience, I cannot marvel enough at his ignorance.

<!-- B005 -->
> First, says Bertius, there are those who think it was a feigned faith. {Pa.48.H.5.} But abandoning righteousness is quite different from bringing hidden hypocrisy to light. So when Ezekiel says, {Ibid.lin.19} “If the righteous man relies on his righteousness and does something evil,” he does not mean, “If the hypocrite reveals himself.” This will be clearer still from Christ’s words to his apostles: {Ibid.lin.29.} “You are the salt of the earth, and salt is good. But if salt loses its saltiness, what will you salt it with? Have salt in yourselves.” Suppose Christ is speaking of hypocrisy and hypocrites. Then his meaning will be: “You are hypocrites, and hypocrisy is good. But if your hypocrisy is exposed, how will it be restored? Have hypocrisy in yourselves,” etc.

<!-- B006 -->
## Answer.

<!-- B007 -->
Here, right at the outset, you see an example of what I taught earlier. “There are those who think it was a feigned faith,” says our Bertius. True: we are among those who think so. But who thinks that in those words to his apostles Christ is speaking of feigned faith, or of hypocrisy and hypocrites? Can Scripture not speak of feigned faith without having to do so in every passage that speaks of faith? Here, then, is the first great injustice Bertius does us. Christ is not speaking there of faith at all, true or feigned, but of the gift given to the apostles to preach the gospel throughout the world.

As for Ezekiel’s words, I grant that some understand them to refer to feigned righteousness. But is that so absurd? And must we then interpret what follows as Bertius does? {Pa 48.l.24}

<!-- B008 -->
> That is, none of his piety—that is, none of his hypocrisy—will be remembered. But will he die for the wickedness he commits, that is, because he now ceases to be a hypocrite and becomes openly godless?

<!-- B009 -->
I do not think so. No one—no one, I say—teaches that a man who does what is right and good from the heart, who does not eat on the mountain or lift his eyes to idols, and so on, has only a feigned righteousness. By no means! {Ezech.18.5.} What they teach is this: suppose a man has done what was right and good, neither eating on the mountains nor lifting his eyes to idols, and so on. If he later does what is wrong and evil, eating on the mountains and lifting his eyes to idols, and so on, then he never did good from the heart. He was righteous only in appearance. So none of the piety he seemed to practise will be remembered. Because of the wickedness he commits in abandoning even his outwardly pious conduct, he will not only die an eternal death but also be punished and afflicted here. {Hier dier vā wonder int eerste deel [unreadable] Ezechielis [unreadable] claert.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M07">Marginal translation (fragmentary): Here … in the first part [unreadable] of Ezekiel [unreadable] explained.</span>

<!-- B010 -->
Is there a word in the prophet Ezekiel that overturns this teaching? Are not those who merely appear righteous also called righteous, though not in the proper sense? {Mat.9.13.} Are not their outward works of the same kind as those of the truly righteous? Did not God sometimes spare not only those who feigned righteousness but even the godless themselves certain bodily punishments they deserved and that he had threatened, if on some occasion they did what was outwardly right? {1.Reg.11.13 en ca 21 29. 1.Cor.10.30/31 en op andere plaetsen.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M09">Marginal translation: 1.Reg.11.13 and ch. 21 29. 1.Cor.10.30/31 and in other places.</span>

Or does God in Ezekiel mean by living and dying, in their proper and primary sense, eternal life and eternal death? That directly contradicts the text. {Ezech.18.9 10.etc.} Besides, if the words meant that, it would necessarily follow that by the good works he does now a person could obtain forgiveness for all his former sins and eternal life hereafter. {Ezech.33.16} Yet our Catechism teaches from God’s word that this comes only through faith in Jesus Christ, and Ezekiel makes no mention at all of that faith. {Vrage 21. ende 60. siet daer.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M12">Marginal translation: Questions 21 and 60; see there.</span> So the interpretation some teachers of the Reformed Church give of these words of Ezekiel is not absurd.

<!-- B011 -->
But suppose it were absurd, though it is not. Does not Bertius himself acknowledge, if we consider the matter in itself, that there is such a thing as feigned faith and feigned righteousness? {Pag.49.l.4 en l.10.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M13">Marginal translation: Pag.49.l.4 and l.10.</span> Why, then, does he accuse us of seeking evasions when we say so? No one can judge infallibly from a person’s words, gestures, or outward deeds whether his faith and righteousness are true and sincere. As far as outward appearances go, often no human eye can distinguish true faith from feigned. {2.Cor.11.14} Why, then, when we see faith abandoned, is it absurd to say that it was feigned rather than true?

This much is beyond doubt: even supposing that both true and feigned faith could be abandoned, there is far more reason to suspect that the faith someone abandons was feigned than that it was true. For, as Bertius says, “Nothing feigned lasts long.” {pag.49.l.14}

<!-- B012 -->
Take another example. Hymenaeus suffered shipwreck in his faith, says Paul. {1.Tim.1.19} Many have now become antichrists, says John. {1.Joan.2.19} Well, were all these people true believers, or was their faith feigned? Hymenaeus, Bertius will say, was a true believer; {pag 27.l.16.} but those antichrists, he says, were feigned believers. {pag.49.ll.18} Why? Before he fell away, did Hymenaeus behave outwardly any differently from the way feigned believers used to behave? Or did those antichrists, before their fall, behave outwardly any differently from the way true believers used to behave? Why does he not call both feigned believers, then, or both true believers? Because it pleases him to say so. Is that not reason enough?
