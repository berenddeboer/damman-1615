<!-- source-block:001 -->
It remains, then, to show briefly that the Reformed churches’ teaching on perseverance {a Dit soeckt Bertius de eenvoudige wijs te maken / wt sijnen brief aen de ghemeyne kercke: item inde voorreden pag. 8. fac. 2. lin. 27. en pag. 2. lin. 10.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C001">[Margin: a. Bertius seeks to make the simple believe this, as appears from his letter to the general church; also in the preface, p. 8, side 2, line 27, and p. 2, line 10.]</span> is not new, nor advocated only by a few private individuals. Rather, it has always been known in God’s church, and was adopted with the Reformation not only here in these lands or at Geneva, but also in various other kingdoms and countries, where it is still taught and maintained unchanged to this day. This will also show that Bertius speaks untruthfully when he says that he has on his side not only the truth but also the antiquity and agreement of the orthodox church. {pag. 84. l. 5.}

## Bertius’s objection.

<!-- source-block:005 -->

<!-- source-block:006 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:007 -->
Justin did not write the work of orthodox questions and answers. Indeed, it could not have been written until a full 200 years after Justin’s death. For that book uses various words that first came into use during and after the Council of Nicaea (held in the year 328). It also names Origen, the Manichaeans, and others who did not live until long afterwards, besides the many other absurdities to be found in that same book. See questions 16. 17. 52. 60. 75. 76. 82. 86. 127. 139. 144. Thus scholars are in no doubt, as Bertius well knows, that this is not Justin’s book.

As for what Justin writes in his defence addressed to the Emperor Antoninus Pius, {Iustinus handelt vanden vryen wil/ niet vande volhardinghe.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C005">[Margin: Justin discusses free will, not perseverance.]</span> which Bertius also recounts here, read it carefully. You will not find a single word about believers’ perseverance or falling away. It all serves solely to defend free will, to which Justin, like many other Greek teachers, attributed far too much, following Plato’s teaching against those called Stoics. On this point, then, Justin’s teaching contradicts the Stoics, not us. For we do not side with the Stoics, nor do we side with Plato: we side with God’s Word, which takes a course between the two.

But this does not concern our subject; and if Bertius nevertheless wished to apply it to that, {Iustin. strijd teghen hem selven int stuc van de vryen wil.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C006">[Margin: Justin contradicts himself on the subject of free will.]</span> let him consider what the same Justin writes in this very defence, and also in the letter to Zenas and Serenus:

<!-- source-block:008 -->
> that we are wholly flesh and that nothing good dwells in us; also, that it is not possible to grasp heavenly things by nature or human understanding, but that a gracious gift of God is needed for this.

<!-- source-block:009 -->
And then let him compare these things with one another.

## Tertullian in the book On Flight in Persecution.

<!-- source-block:042 -->
{5. Desen heeft geleeft ende gheleert / Ao 200.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C013">[Margin: 5. He lived and taught in the year 200.]</span>

<!-- source-block:043 -->
> The devil will have no power against God’s servants unless the Lord permits it, and that is to bring him—the devil—to nothing through the elect’s faith, victorious in temptation; or to show openly that those who turn away to the devil have belonged to the devil.

<!-- source-block:044 -->
And shortly afterwards:

<!-- source-block:045 -->
> The multitude of devils had no power to enter the swine unless they had received that power from the Lord. So far are they from having any power against God’s little sheep.

<!-- source-block:046 -->
## Bertius’s objection.

<!-- source-block:047 -->
> {Pa. 86. l. 36. Pa. 90. voor aen.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C014">[Margin: p. 86, line 36; p. 90, at the beginning.]</span> Yet Tertullian speaks quite differently elsewhere: someone previously praised later falls back. Also: those who have been inside are cast out, etc.

<!-- source-block:048 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:049 -->
In recounting Tertullian’s first saying, Bertius has omitted many words that chiefly express the writer’s meaning. {Pag. 90. li. 1} After the words “Agnoscere gl. correcti”, Tertullian continues:

<!-- source-block:050 -->
> For it belonged to the Son of God alone to be free of every transgression, or to remain without sin.

<!-- source-block:051 -->
Later, after the words “in the Lord’s barns”, he continues:

<!-- source-block:052 -->
> Did not some of the hearers, having taken offence, depart from the Lord? Yet the others did not therefore think that they too ought to cease following his—Christ’s—footsteps. Rather, those who knew that he was the Word of life and had come from God remained constant in following him, or in his company, to the end.

<!-- source-block:053 -->
From this it is clear that Tertullian is not only not against us, but is also expressly with us. {Pa.90.li.3.} Note his words also when he says: “Let the chaff of shallow faith fly away; the grain will be stored all the more purely.” {Pa.90.li.10.} What is that but to say that those who draw back and fall away are only chaff with shallow faith, and that those who are grain do not fly away but come into heaven? That is, the regenerate do not fall away, and those who die unrepentant do not stand!

<!-- source-block:054 -->
As for the second saying, “Those who have been inside are cast out”: understand “inside” to mean in the church, on the threshing floor, in the field, in the net, as is clearly apparent from what precedes and follows in the same book. Look it up and read it.

<!-- source-block:055 -->
The third and fifth sayings concern Solomon’s rejection, but they do not specify: 1. Whether Solomon had been regenerate. 2. Whether he was rejected in such a way that he completely lost all the benefits of faith. 3. Whether he also returned to God. For the ancient teachers do not all speak alike about this, as Bertius well knows. As long as Tertullian does not say that Solomon, having been regenerate, fell completely away from God through his sin, without ever again being brought to repentance, he says nothing that opposes us. For if Solomon lost the honour he had in God, he could afterwards have regained it. If God’s wrath came upon him, God’s grace may afterwards have appeared to him again. We indeed believe that both these things happened, and say, moreover, that it cannot be proved that God’s wrath against Solomon was so great—although he well deserved it—that God took away from him entirely all the gifts of his Spirit. {Pa.90.l.21. Prov. 11.35.}

<!-- source-block:056 -->
The fourth saying, concerning rejecting and accepting, must be understood of the things of this life, not of the life to come. This appears from the words Tertullian adduces here:

<!-- source-block:057 -->
> What person, he says, is without sin, so that God would always accept him in such a way that he could never reject him? Or who lives so wholly without good works that God would always reject him without being able to accept him? Show me someone who is always good: he will never be rejected. Show me someone who is always evil: he will never be accepted (or received).

<!-- source-block:058 -->
If Bertius understands all this of acceptance into certain offices or dignities, and rejection from those same offices or dignities, taking place here in this world according to whether someone behaves well or badly, then none of this serves our subject as it needs to.

If he understands it of acceptance and rejection concerning salvation, he must first declare whether he too holds that God daily diminishes and increases the number of the elect and the reprobate, now electing someone he rejected yesterday, and then rejecting again the same person he has just elected. For if he does not hold that opinion, why is something he himself rejects raised as an objection against us? If he does hold it, let him prove from God’s Word that it is sound, and then use Tertullian’s words to his advantage. I say that it is a horrible opinion, making God inconstant and fickle, though in Holy Scripture he is called an unchangeable God. {Jacob. 1.17. Malac. 3.6}

If he wishes to say that the change is not in God but in people: that God has once, actually and unchangeably, resolved to save all who believe and condemn those who do not believe, but that people who believe today become unbelievers tomorrow, and those who are unbelievers today become believers tomorrow, so that, on that account, someone who now believes may be said to be accepted or elected by God; and if the same person falls away from his faith tomorrow, he may again be said to be rejected by God, and so again each time, as often, namely, as the person changes—I answer that all this is unfounded, since God has elected not simply believers but those who persevere in faith (according to Bertius’s view). And since someone who has now abandoned faith does not persevere in it, he cannot be said to have been elected merely because he believed for one day and no more. Rejection must be understood in the same way. Thus these words of Tertullian either are not against us or are directly against God’s Holy Word. How does that help Bertius?

## Bertius’s objection.

<!-- source-block:068 -->
> Yet Cyprian says elsewhere that some of the confessors go so far astray that some among them also commit such great and grievous sins, etc. {Pag.91.l.2.} Also: we must do our best to attain growth after these beginnings, etc. {pag. 92. li. 4.} Also, that Judas betrayed the Lord, etc. {Ibid. lin. 22}

<!-- source-block:069 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:070 -->
The first saying concerns confessors of the true faith. But are all such confessors also truly regenerate children of God? If not, how does this serve our subject? To what he says about Solomon, I give the answer just given concerning Tertullian. None of it opposes us, as also appears from what precedes. We gladly accept the second saying as a necessary exhortation to steadfastness; but I do not see with what words he calls into doubt the steadfastness of believing, regenerate children of God. For walking in the Lord’s way is not being regenerate, or all hypocrites would have to be regenerate. Christ plainly teaches that not all confessors are regenerate, Matthew 7.21. See also 1 Corinthians 13.2 and 3. The third saying, although many scholars doubt whether it is Cyprian’s, is in no way against us. No one denies that a man called and chosen to an office, and behaving faithfully and peaceably in it for a time, can afterwards become faithless, as can be seen in Judas and others. Yet I cannot well understand on what grounds Cyprian here calls Judas faithful in God’s house, unless he was looking at outward appearances. But since God chiefly regards the inward person, and Judas was in his heart a thief, indeed a devil, he cannot be reckoned faithful before God. {1. Sam. 16.7} {Ioan. 6.70. en cap. 12.6.}

## Bertius’s objection.

<!-- source-block:082 -->
> {92. litt.} Yet in letter 181 to Antiochus he speaks differently.

<!-- source-block:083 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:084 -->
Although in that letter Basil does not speak of faith or believers, and exhorts his disciple not to perseverance in faith but to perseverance in a solitary life he has begun, we nevertheless gladly accept these words of his for this subject too. It is a very good and necessary lesson, which we ought indeed to take to heart. But it is no proof that God’s truly regenerate children can lose their faith. For Gehazi, Judas, and the righteous person of whom Ezekiel speaks were not regenerate; and Solomon did not entirely lose his faith, though it was greatly impaired by his accursed idolatry. We have already spoken of this, and hope to speak more of it in what follows.

<!-- source-block:085 -->
Let this suffice concerning the teachers who lived and taught in God’s church before the Pelagians were known, for they first became known around the year 380. {Danaeus in cap. 28. Augustin. de hæresib. Pag. 2. 49.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C029">[Margin: Daneau on chapter 28 of Augustine’s On Heresies, page 2. 49.]</span> Thus Bertius is greatly mistaken when he says that the teaching of predestination and perseverance, as taught in our churches, was chiefly introduced by Augustine to overturn Pelagius’s propositions. On this point Augustine himself especially deserves a hearing. {Int boeck vã het goet der volhardinge cap. 20.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C030">[Margin: In the book On the Good of Perseverance, chapter 20.]</span>

<!-- source-block:086 -->
> For he writes as follows: I am surprised that the Pelagians do not notice that—to say nothing of others such as Cyprian, Ambrose, and Gregory of Nazianzus—in many of my books written and published before the Pelagians began to be known, indeed before I knew that any Pelagian heresy would ever arise, I wrote and spoke of God’s grace and acceptance, and also of perseverance, as I do now.

<!-- source-block:087 -->
And afterwards:

<!-- source-block:088 -->
> On this matter—predestination and perseverance according to Augustine’s view—the church has never been silent in its prayers, from its beginning onward, although it has not always declared itself about it in express words as here, because it was not provoked to do so by an opponent. {Int seste boeck ca 23. Cap. 22. int eynde. Nota.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C031">[Margin: In the sixth book, chapter 23. Chapter 22, at the end. Note.]</span>

<!-- source-block:089 -->
Therefore he also says of himself:

<!-- source-block:090 -->
> After the Pelagians compelled us, I set forth this predestination more diligently and with greater labour. {Int seste boeck ca. 23.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C032">[Margin: In the sixth book, chapter 23.]</span> For we have found that each heresy has brought particular questions into the church, against which Scripture has also had to be explained and defended with greater diligence than would have occurred had such a need not arisen. {Nota.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C033">[Margin: Note.]</span>

<!-- source-block:091 -->
What could be said more clearly against Bertius? What could better be said to explain or [unclear word] everything that Bertius here adduces to his advantage from the teachers just cited and others? For just as among the pagan philosophers there were some called Stoics, so among Christians in the church’s beginning there were also the Marcionites, in the year 133, who strongly pressed the same Stoic doctrine of the necessity of all things. (For that reason ᵃ Isidore and ᵇ Tertullian also call Marcion a Stoic.) {a int achtste boeck cap. 5. b Teghens Marcionem.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C034">[Margin: a. In the eighth book, chapter 5. b. Against Marcion.]</span> Against this stood, both expressly and without naming names, Justin in the year 140, Irenaeus in the year 178, Clement of Alexandria in the year 195, Origen and Tertullian in the year 200, Cyprian in the year 259, Athanasius in the year 340, and others. Is it any wonder, then, that they, not yet having to contend with a Pelagius, wrote so much about human ability and free will? Indeed, so much that Pelagius in his time used their words to defend himself against their will, as Prosper testifies? When certain sayings from Paul’s letter to the Romans were set against him, he alleged that some teacher of the church had also understood them in that same way. {In den brief aen Augusti.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C035">[Margin: In the letter to Augustine.]</span>

Those good and godly teachers, to show how far removed they were from Marcion’s godless assertion, fell completely into the other extreme, although they nevertheless explain themselves differently in some places in their books. Bertius himself will not deny this. Indeed, I hold it certain that, if he considers it properly, he will flatly reject the opinion on free will that they generally present, partly owing to the aforementioned circumstances, and partly because they esteemed Plato and his followers too highly. {Ibid.} I think those excellent men can to some extent be excused, as can various others after their time, especially in the Greek churches. But I cannot excuse Bertius, who does not want to be regarded as a Pelagian, and yet in this controversy produces against us from that box and from other ancient teachers sayings which are the same as, or closely resemble, those Pelagius was formerly accustomed to produce in the same controversy against Augustine, Prosper, Hilary, and others.

As for holy Ignatius, from whom Bertius also adduces something, it is evident {Pa.84.li.19} that he feared he would not become a martyr, although he particularly longed for that. And this he feared partly because the congregations were making special efforts to obtain his release when he was imprisoned, and partly because he did not know what strength God would grant him for it. But becoming a martyr is different from being saved. Even if Ignatius had denied Christ through weakness, as Peter also did, he could indeed have escaped death on that occasion; nevertheless, he could afterwards have repented again and so been brought to salvation, as has happened to many. Thus this holy teacher too is not against us, nor is any of the others, if we attend precisely to this controversy. For on free will they speak so freely that they are opposed not only to us but also to Bertius himself. Let us now go on to the other teachers who taught and lived in God’s church in Pelagius’s time and after it.

## Bertius’s objection.

<!-- source-block:107 -->

<!-- source-block:108 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:109 -->
Gregory says nothing against us. He says that Saul had given himself over to the spirit neither proudly nor entirely, but had become a different man from what he had been before. How does that affect believing, regenerate children of God? Or was Saul regenerate too? Bertius seems to want to say so, and to prove it by Gregory’s saying: “Saul was then spiritual, so that I would wish to become nothing other than what he then was.” {Pag. 93. l. 33} But Bertius’s book must here be corrupted, or mine must be. For in my book the Greek reads thus: “He was then spiritual, for I do not wish to speak or judge otherwise of him.” Surely there is a great difference between quietly judging in charity that Saul was spiritual, and saying it with such certainty that one would wish to become nothing other than what Saul then was. Saul had spirit and flesh in him, as Gregory also says further there; and with respect to the spirit he was spiritual, spiritual according to the nature of the gifts of the spirit in which he had come to share. For whoever has received the spirit—that is, certain gifts of the spirit—is spiritual. Saul had received from God the spirit of wisdom and courage so that he might govern well: thus he too was spiritual. But Gregory does not say that Saul had received the spirit of faith, nor can this be proved from Scripture or with sound arguments.

<!-- source-block:119 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:120 -->
It is surprising that Bertius also adduces Augustine here, for earlier he gives the impression that it was almost Augustine alone who approved our view on this point, indeed that he was almost its first author and foremost defender. But it may be that Augustine contradicts himself, as Bertius also indicates there. We must therefore examine this.

<!-- source-block:121 -->
{pag.95 l.1.} In the first saying he asks: “What person can know that he will persevere to the end in the practice and progress of righteousness?” That is right. The children of God often stumble. Therefore, although they are sufficiently assured that they will persevere in faith to the end, they cannot nevertheless be certain that they will persevere in the practice of righteousness in such a way as not to fall into any horrible sins. Read the third chapter: it serves entirely to prove our case.

In the second saying he gives his view of Solomon, saying that he was rejected by God, like Adam and the fallen angels. But he does not mention whether he was accepted again like Adam, or remained fallen like the angels. Nor was his intention there to investigate whether a believer can fall away from his faith, but to teach that one must not place one’s hope in any human being. Thus it does not concern the matter. In the third saying he forbids a confident judgment about another person’s perseverance; but it does not follow that no one can be assured of his own perseverance. No one knows what is in a person except the person’s spirit within him. {1. Cor. 2. 11.}

In the fourth saying he teaches that a regenerate person—that is, someone regarded as such—can fall into an evil life and lose the grace he has received. But that is not to say that he will not return and rise from his fall, or that he completely loses everything he has. For through sin the Holy Spirit is not entirely driven out, but only grieved, in proportion to the greatness of that sin considered in itself. {pag. 16. l. 2.} This can be seen in David, who therefore, as Basil says, asks God that he may retain what he still had and regain what he had lost. {over den [unreadable reference] Psalm} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C050">[Margin: On Psalm [unreadable reference].]</span> The fifth saying has the same meaning, which will become clearer if one reads through the whole chapter from which it is taken. I ask you, take that trouble: you will find that Bertius misinterprets Augustine, so directly does he write there against Bertius.

## Answer.

<!-- source-block:151 -->
In the first saying he teaches only that one cannot be assured of other people’s steadfastness, as appears from the words, and we acknowledge this to be true. He also adds this reason:

<!-- source-block:152 -->
> For there are some righteous people who do works like those of the ungodly, and there are ungodly people who do works like those of the righteous. This is said because God alone can judge it with certainty. Those whom we think righteous are often found to be sinners; and conversely, those whom we think sinners, God knows to be righteous. And however hard a person labours to investigate this, he will still not discover it; and even if a wise man said he knew it, he will nevertheless be unable to discover it, etc.

<!-- source-block:153 -->
The second saying concerns not regenerate people, but Judas and people like him, such as all heretics, hypocrites, unbelievers, the heedless, and the disobedient. This appears from that same fifth chapter, and also from Hosea, chapter 13. But when such people are said to fall out of God’s hands, this must be understood in accordance with the way in which they had been in God’s hands. For God’s hand signifies God’s power, defence, and fatherly protection; and being in God’s hands means being in God’s power or keeping. Thus good and bad, regenerate and unregenerate, Judas and Peter, are in God’s hand, in God’s power, in God’s protection, but in quite different ways. God’s love towards such different people is likewise quite different; the benefits each of them possesses, and in which God preserves them, are also very different. Consequently what Jerome says here does not concern the matter. For the following inference is certainly unsound: the unregenerate person can fall out of God’s hand through his own will; therefore the regenerate person can also fall. Someone to whom God gives neither the ability to persevere nor actual perseverance can fall; therefore someone to whom God gives both—namely, that he can persevere and that he actually does persevere—can also fall. Those whom God preserves only in the benefits he has given them to discharge an office can fall out when they no longer want rightly to rely upon his hand, defence, and protection; therefore those whom God’s power preserves through faith unto salvation can also fall. {1. Petr. 1.5.}

## Answer.

<!-- source-block:184 -->
Had Bertius written down Bernard’s words which he omitted in between, everyone could readily have noticed that Bernard is not speaking here of regenerate people, whom our question concerns, but of those whom charity alone regards as regenerate. For he says:

<!-- source-block:185 -->

<!-- source-block:186 -->
Bernard therefore does not say that all who do good works are regenerate, but says that one must certainly regard them as such, and that through charity alone, not through certain knowledge. Charity can be deceived, so that those who are not regenerate, but whom one regards as regenerate, can also fall away from faith and all good works. We too grant that. For what else would be the case with the hypocrites, chaff, and weeds of which Holy Scripture speaks?

<!-- source-block:187 -->
Let this then suffice also concerning the ancient teachers of the churches—not all, for what need is there of that, but only some of the principal ones. Somewhat more clearly in one case than another, though they did not make it their task to deal with this question, they nevertheless testify together as with one mouth that Bertius wrongs them when he wants to make the simple believe that God’s church has always believed that God’s truly regenerate children can become children of the devil. It has never believed that. Indeed, it has always believed and taught the contrary, not only in earlier times but also from the beginning of the Reformation, now a hundred years ago, and in all churches that are rightly reformed, with which Bertius still sided ten years ago. In honour of the truth I shall now briefly demonstrate this too.

<!-- source-block:188 -->

All these, I say, when properly considered, speak and write the same things that we speak, write, and believe about the perseverance of the saints.

Philip Melanchthon and Martin Bucer in countless places, which Girolamo Zanchi points out and recounts at great length in his Miscellanies, part 1, page 118 and following. > Everything pure, upright, true, and good that is in us or can be done by us has its origin in that sweet root, that overflowing fountain, namely God’s goodness, love, ELECTION, and purpose.

<!-- source-block:202 -->
Observe: election is the origin of all the good that is in us, therefore of faith too, so that faith is given to the elect alone. The confession of the English church of the year 1562 reads as follows, article 17:

<!-- source-block:203 -->
> Those who have received such a glorious benefit from God—that is, those elected by God to eternal life—are called according to his purpose by his Spirit working at the appropriate time. They obey this calling through God’s grace; thus they are freely justified and adopted as God’s children, and finally through God’s mercy they are brought to eternal blessedness.

<!-- source-block:204 -->
Observe the same thing here again. Afterwards, in the year 1595, when some troubles arose at the university of Cambridge through one Peter Baro, a meeting of various bishops and distinguished doctors was held at Lambeth, in which, among others, they also drew up this article:

> True, living, justifying faith and the sanctifying Spirit of God are not extinguished, do not fall away or vanish in the elect, either finally or completely.

<!-- source-block:206 -->
What could be said more clearly?

From this arises that continual conflict between flesh and Spirit. First he recounts three passages from Philip Melanchthon in which he contradicts our doctrine. {pa. 98. lin. 1.}

<!-- source-block:214 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:215 -->
Bertius does not understand Melanchthon’s manner of speaking, to which one must nevertheless attend if one is not to wrong him. Melanchthon says: {In sijn ghemeyne plaetsen van de dadelijcke sonden. Pag. 165.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C081">[Margin: In his Commonplaces, on actual sins, page 165.]</span>

<!-- source-block:216 -->
> that the elect, sinning against their conscience, drive out the Holy Spirit and become liable to God’s eternal wrath; and this not only when they sin often and with pleasure or with full intention, but also when they sin once, and that even through great fear, to escape danger to their lives.

<!-- source-block:217 -->
And further afterwards: {Pag. 126.}

<!-- source-block:218 -->
> It is evident that the elect can lose grace.

<!-- source-block:219 -->
And once again: {Pag. 127.}

<!-- source-block:220 -->
> Those who assert that the elect always retain the Holy Spirit must be rejected.

<!-- source-block:221 -->
He speaks in this way in other places too. From this it can readily be gathered what Melanchthon means by the words “to drive out the Holy Spirit and become liable to God’s wrath”: namely, to lose some gifts and operations of the Spirit, and to anger God through sins. Yet this is not in such a way that one loses all the Spirit’s operations and remains completely without ever regaining God’s grace and love; for how could such people be elected to eternal life? Rather, those gifts are lost only for a time, and one comes to remorse and amendment of life, and so finally to salvation. In short, in the first saying Melanchthon does not teach that the regenerate can drive out the Holy Spirit and faith entirely—attend to his words—but only that they can drive out some gifts of the Holy Spirit and the power of faith, and that for a time. We too gladly acknowledge this. Therefore, in the same passage on good works, shortly after the words Bertius adduces, Melanchthon says:

<!-- source-block:222 -->
> Since the saints fight against their corrupt affections and believe that their weakness is forgiven them for the Son of God’s sake, they therefore remain in grace and retain faith and the Holy Spirit.

<!-- source-block:223 -->
He thus indicates that when the saints fall they retain, and also lose, grace and the Holy Spirit: they retain the gift and Spirit; they lose the heart’s tranquillity and satisfaction and the Spirit’s particular works. If we understand Melanchthon otherwise, we must also believe that the elect can be lost, and that they can be lost for one sin. For Melanchthon says both, as appears from the passages quoted above, and Bertius will say that both are false. See Bertius, page 16, line 1.

<!-- source-block:224 -->
In the second saying, “true faith” must be understood as what Bertius calls true faith when he says: {Pa. 98 li. 9. Pa. 58. li 10.}

<!-- source-block:225 -->
> The truth of faith depends upon the truth and goodness of the object to which faith looks. Since this is Christ, with his merits and promises, there is no reason why it should not be called a true faith, even if it lasts only one day.

<!-- source-block:226 -->
Therefore it is a true faith when one believes to be true what is true. “Someone who believes that the Law is God’s Word”—these are the words of Hemmingsen, whom Bertius esteems highly, in his handbook, class 1, chapter 12, page 108—“and holds for certain that the Law’s promises and threats are true, truly believes. Likewise, someone who believes that Christ’s Gospel is God’s Word and declares that God’s truth is contained in it also truly believes.” {Dit schrijft Hemmingius so hy seyt op datmen int uyt der Duytsch Melancthons boecken soude verstaen,} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C085">[Margin: Hemmingsen writes this, he says, so that one might understand it from Melanchthon’s books in German.]</span> Such a true faith can indeed be rejected, as Melanchthon says. But we are not speaking here of such a true faith, but of a faith which Hemmingsen there calls Fidem fiduciæ, a faith of trust, which he also says belongs to God’s children alone, whereas that other true faith can exist in the ungodly as well as in the godly, in devils as well as in Christians. Thus in this second saying Melanchthon says nothing against us. To make it clearer that this is true, and enable everyone more readily to observe how faithfully Bertius treats Melanchthon when he draws him to his own side, I must add here what Melanchthon writes in his explanation of that same chapter 10 of 1 Corinthians, from which Bertius cites this second saying, although I had omitted it for brevity’s sake. He says as follows:

<!-- source-block:227 -->
> {Over 13. ℣} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C086">[Margin: On verse 13.]</span> Since so many people, overcome by the devil and their weakness, are lost and turn away from God, how does the fact that so great a multitude is not preserved agree with what is said here? I answer: there are always two parts in the church. The one has the beginnings of faith and fear; and although its faith is weak, it nevertheless exercises repentance and invocation of God, and RETAINS the fear of God and faith. Christ speaks of this part: “To the one who has, it will be given”, etc. {Mat. 13 12.} The other part is careless, without the exercise of repentance and invocation of God, etc., like Saul, Ahithophel, Absalom, and countless others. These are overcome by the devil. But Paul speaks here of the first part, which God protects and supports as with his hand, so that when they fall and waver, they DO NOT FALL INTO DESTRUCTION, etc.

<!-- source-block:228 -->
Now compare these things with one another.

<!-- source-block:229 -->
In the third saying Melanchthon makes an express distinction between Saul and David, which Bertius has deliberately omitted. {Pa. 98. li. 13} For after the words I have recounted, Melanchthon writes as follows:

<!-- source-block:230 -->
> {pag 98. l. 22.} And Saul was utterly lost, crushed by eternal punishments. But David was converted again to God, yet in such a way that all his life he felt a burden of bodily punishments and offences.

<!-- source-block:231 -->
Thus he clearly shows that David was not lost, but was severely punished by God for his great sins. And who speaks against that? Who denies that the regenerate can fall into foul sins and be severely punished by God? The sole question is whether God’s believing, regenerate children can be lost. To that we say no, and Melanchthon does not say yes. Thus this too does not concern the matter. This will appear still more clearly if one reads what Melanchthon writes on 1 Corinthians 10, where he expressly says {Neemt sijn uytlegginge over het 13. Versken} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C090">[Margin: Take his exposition of verse 13.]</span> that:

<!-- source-block:232 -->
> the devil did not enter David’s heart as he entered Saul’s heart, and did not rob God’s Word from David’s heart as from Saul’s heart; also, that the devil laid many tricks and ambushes for David, but could never bring him to an Epicurean contempt and hatred of God, as he did indeed bring Saul to it, etc.

<!-- source-block:233 -->
Read also the same Melanchthon in his commonplace on the Spirit and the letter, not far from the beginning, page 612 and 612, and in the exposition of the fourth commandment, to pass over other passages.

<!-- source-block:234 -->
Afterwards Bertius adduces two sayings from the Reformation of Cologne, composed by Bucer and Melanchthon. {3. Pa. 3 & 129.}

<!-- source-block:235 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:236 -->
The first saying is entirely true, understood, then, as Bertius himself understands it: {pag. 98. lin. 1} namely, that the regenerate, when they sin, grieve the Holy Spirit in proportion to the greatness of that sin, but nevertheless do not drive out that same Spirit entirely; yet if they do not repent, they would be lost. This, I say, is entirely true. But how does it affect our subject? Our whole dispute is whether the regenerate, when they sin, have ever remained without repentance and died in their sins. Not one word of that is mentioned in this saying.

The second saying concerns those who had been accepted as God’s children. {pag 98. l. 30.} But by whom had they been accepted? By God? That is not written there. Read the words. By whom, then? By the church, through baptism and confession of their faith. This does not concern us, for we acknowledge that every day many who have been baptized and profess the religion fall away and are lost. But we are speaking here of those whom God adopts as his children. Yet it would not harm our case even if these words had to be understood of true children of God. For we also acknowledge that such people often turn away and reject grace and the Holy Spirit; but we add that they do not do so wholly or entirely, and this saying does not contradict that.

But so that one may know what the widely renowned and especially excellent Doctor Bucer thinks on this point—Melanchthon has now been discussed—I shall, in keeping with my purpose, put forward here just one out of a thousand sayings of his, from his exposition of Matthew 7, verse 3, where he writes thus:

<!-- source-block:237 -->
> Those who can at any time fall away from Christ have never belonged to Christ. Consequently they have never truly believed or truly been godly; nor have they ever received the Spirit of children. Rather, their whole being has been mere pretence, although for a time they behave in a holy way, outstanding in godliness. For those whom Christ loves he loves to the end; those whom the Father gives him he does not cast out; and no one can snatch them from his hand. {Joan. 13. 1. en cap. 6. 37. en 12. 10. 28.} Therefore, although they may fall, they never fall away completely, for they are elected to eternal life, an election which no creature can overturn, etc.

<!-- source-block:238 -->
Could anything clearer be said? Yet Bertius is brazen enough to call on this godly, sound teacher to defend his errors.

Thirdly, Bertius sets the Augsburg Confession against us. {3. Pag. 99. l. 8.}

<!-- source-block:240 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:241 -->
This confession contradicts not us but the Anabaptists, such as Nicolaas Storch, Jan Hutte, Thomas Müntzer, Caspar Schwenckfeld, and similar enthusiasts. They promoted every kind of licence, stirred up rebellion against the authorities, killed their brothers, wives, and others, and committed adultery and all sorts of misconduct, yet would not have any of this called sin, since they imagined themselves born of God, and whoever is born of God cannot sin. {Chemnitius in 2. part, exa Concil. Trident. fol. 370. col. 1; lin. penultima. legt desen artijckel also uyt.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C096">[Margin: Chemnitz explains this article this way in part 2 of his Examination of the Council of Trent, folio 370, column 1, penultimate line.]</span> {1. Joan. 3. 9.} This was a doctrine directly opposed to the repentance of which the appended article of the confession speaks. For one who does not sin has no need to repent. These Anabaptists said that they did not sin, and that all who believed as they did also did not sin, and neither lost nor grieved the Holy Spirit, who, they said, taught them such abominations. Therefore they did not need to repent either. This is an accursed doctrine, rightly rejected here by the Augsburg Confession, and likewise by us. But how does that now concern us? Can one not contradict the Anabaptists without also contradicting us? We are not Anabaptists and have nothing to do with all their fanaticism. We reject them in this, but do not thereby reject ourselves. To be brief, the confession speaks: 1. Of people who are justified but not sanctified, and consequently not regenerate, understanding by “justified” the believers—that is, those who said they believed and therefore justified themselves, as the Anabaptists did. {Luc. 16. 15.} 2. It also speaks of losing the Holy Spirit without specifying whether it understands this as losing all the Spirit’s gifts or only some. For my part, I think that, according to the confession’s meaning, if this article were to be correctly formulated, it ought to read thus:

<!-- source-block:242 -->
> People who have once been justified lose, or can lose, the Holy Spirit—that is, some gifts of the Holy Spirit.

<!-- source-block:243 -->
And so we gladly accept it, holding for certain that this is also its true meaning, because Melanchthon, who composed this confession, thinks about this question in the way explained above. If one wishes to understand it as teaching that the truly regenerate could lose all the Spirit’s gifts, Bertius must prove that there were ever truly regenerate Anabaptists before the year 1530, and that they taught or believed that they and all the regenerate might indeed lose some gifts of the Holy Spirit, and that for a time, but would never lose all the Spirit’s gifts. If Bertius names such people, I shall grant him that the Augsburg Confession is on his side. But what will he then have gained? God willing, I shall tell him then.

## Answer.

<!-- source-block:249 -->
I hold it almost certain that Bertius acts here with a half-guilty conscience. But the very words of this article 16, which Bertius cites, would have sufficed to deprive him of every excuse of ignorance before God and humankind, if only he had been willing to present them to himself and his reader as completely as he could read them in his book. <!-- source-block:250 -->
> Our confession rejects those who deny that a baptized person could depart from the grace given him—that is, as is immediately added there, sin. For the aim of article 16 is to refute those who say that the regenerate cannot sin as long as they live. We acknowledge that a person truly justified and regenerated can sin, anger God, deserve many punishments, and, as the article says, depart or withdraw from grace. Some most ignorantly explain this as falling out of grace. For departing from grace—that is, obeying the desire of the flesh and resisting the divine admonitions and motions of the Spirit—is a very different thing from falling away from grace, extinguishing the Spirit so that one retains no spark of it. That will not be found in any article of our confession, but the contrary will.

<!-- source-block:251 -->
Who understands the meaning of the English Confession and church better than a doctor and professor of divinity at the university of Cambridge in England, who was professor there from the year 1572 to the year 1595? He taught that Bertius acts far too discourteously in wishing to explain and take the words “depart from grace” as if they read “fall away from grace”. I raise the further point that Bertius acts most improperly when, knowing the view of the English church—as he did know it, or at least could and ought to have known it—he nevertheless comes parading the English Confession here, leaving a little word unquoted so that people would not notice the deceit. For the article reads thus:

<!-- source-block:252 -->
> After we have received the Spirit, we can depart from the grace given to us, and sin.

<!-- source-block:253 -->
The ordinary person was not to read those little words “and sin”; otherwise, even if they had only one eye and could see no farther than the length of their noses, they could readily have noticed that someone was trying to palm nonsense off on them. Shame! Rational souls ought not to act like this in such weighty matters.

## Answer.

<!-- source-block:256 -->
These words are adduced as if they were all Martyr’s words, whereas the first half is an objection and the other half Martyr’s answer. “They put forward, moreover,” says Martyr, “those who truly believe”, etc. “But we do not grant”, etc.—this is his answer. <!-- source-block:257 -->
> As long as someone is stuck in sins, he does not have faith, but a likeness and appearance of faith.

That is true, but how does it fit here? Or does Bertius wish to stir the objection and answer together and make a cake out of them? Namely, in this way: those who truly believe cannot be said to be without faith, even if they fall into grievous sins, provided only that they believe they have believed, although while stuck in such sin they nevertheless do not believe. This is far too disorderly. Or does he wish merely to raise against us what is raised against Martyr? Then let him not put it under Martyr’s name. First, Martyr does not say all this; but even if he did, it would nevertheless not conflict with us. For shortly before the words Bertius cites here, Martyr says as follows:

<!-- source-block:259 -->

<!-- source-block:260 -->
To prove this, he adds, among other things: “The Turks confess many things that we too confess and believe; nevertheless we shall never say that the Turks have true faith”, etc. Thus Martyr there calls “true faith” what Bertius, as has been said, also calls true faith. We have acknowledged before, and still acknowledge, that such faith as the Turks and devils possess can indeed be lost. What has that to do with this question? See what we answered concerning the second saying which Bertius adduced from Melanchthon. {Siet ooc de laetste predicatie D W. takerl. pag. 694. daer hy van Martyre ende Bucero spreeckt.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C104">[Margin: See also the last sermon of Doctor Whitaker, page 694, where he speaks of Martyr and Bucer.]</span> If you wish to know what Martyr thinks about this point, read his exposition of Romans 1, verse 5, and of 1 Corinthians 13, near the end, and the declaration of the church and university of Zurich, which Martyr also signed, articles 9, 10, and 11, in Zanchi’s Miscellanies, part 1, page 85, to pass over other passages. ## Answer.

<!-- source-block:263 -->
At the end of the words Bertius recounts here from Musculus, Musculus testifies that, well aware that others hold a different opinion on this matter, he wished only to indicate his own opinion, without wishing to wrong the others. Why should Musculus not have been free to do so? But since he does not want his opinion to serve to our disadvantage and injury, why does Bertius take the liberty of nevertheless setting Musculus against us here? Passing over this, however, it seems that Musculus has experienced what many scholars experience: they do not always think or speak alike, since, as the saying goes, one day teaches another. For in the same book Bertius cites here, in the commonplace on the forgiveness of sins, chapter 6, the same Musculus says as follows:

<!-- source-block:264 -->
> Not every kind of sin extinguishes previous grace. Rather, returning to one’s former ungodliness, as to one’s vomit, extinguishes grace. It is one thing to dirty one’s feet after washing, and another to wallow with one’s whole body in the mud like swine. It is one thing for a wife to wrong her husband now and then, and another for her faithlessly to break the marriage through adultery and cut herself off from all marital communion. Likewise, it is one thing simply to sin, which also happens to God’s children, and another to fall utterly from God’s grace, crucify Christ anew, and make a mockery of him. The elect do not do this, but only the reprobate. The epistle to the Hebrews speaks of this last kind of sinning, not of the first kind, etc.

<!-- source-block:265 -->
And once again, in a passage on patience or endurance, page 757:

<!-- source-block:266 -->
> A temporary faith, he says, is not a true faith.

<!-- source-block:267 -->
And in his exposition of 1 Corinthians 15, verse 2, “Unless you have believed in vain”:

<!-- source-block:268 -->
> He does not speak, says Musculus, of that true faith which belongs to the elect and cannot be made vain or brought to nothing. The elect do not believe in vain, for they retain the word of faith in a good heart and bear fruit with patience. But the reprobate do not believe effectively or profitably, for they do not retain the word in their hearts or bring forth fruits of new life. Therefore they believe in vain, etc.

<!-- source-block:269 -->
He writes in this way in many other places too. It is therefore necessary that Musculus not contradict Musculus, and that consequently he may help neither side here; or that he be heard only concerning his particular opinion, which Bertius attributes to him, leaving aside what agrees with the opinion of the other teachers of the churches. This is especially so since he frequently presents the common view in certain, undoubting words, but his particular view only as an opinion when he says “what I think of it”. {pag. 101. l. 25} Yet if necessity required it, the words of Musculus which Bertius adduces could readily be explained in such a way that they conflicted neither with Musculus nor with us: namely, if by a righteous, godly, believing, God-fearing person {ibid. l. 3.} we wished to understand those who have only acquired the beginnings of faith and of the good Spirit. {ibid. lin. 13.} We gladly acknowledge that these can fall away, since they are not yet regenerate. But I leave this aside: Bertius may see to it that he agrees with Musculus.

## Answer.

<!-- source-block:272 -->
It seems that to Bertius it was all one where he sought any assistance. For if David had lost all the Spirit’s gifts, how could he still pray, confess his sins, and desire that God should not take his Holy Spirit from him? {vers. 3 s. ende 13.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C111">[Margin: verses 3 s. and 13.]</span>

<!-- source-block:274 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:275 -->
Although Chemnitz and Hemmingsen agree with Bertius in many things, nevertheless they are not wholly against us and the truth either. I shall show this with their own words. Chemnitz, in the refutation of the Council of Trent, discussing justifying faith, page 172, column 2: {Martinus Chemnicius}

<!-- source-block:276 -->
> The doctrine of predestination does not serve to make true believers doubt whether they are numbered among the elect. Rather, as Paul in Romans 8 sets down these steps: “Those whom he elected, he also called, and those whom he called, he also justified.” Therefore those whom God calls and justifies must hold for certain that they are elect.

<!-- source-block:277 -->
And shortly afterwards:

<!-- source-block:278 -->
> Fourthly, they object to us with the sayings about perseverance: that true believers, granted that they are assured of the grace they presently possess, nevertheless cannot be certain whether they will remain steadfast to the end; and since only those who remain steadfast to the end are saved, believers’ confidence in their salvation cannot therefore be certain. I answer: Scripture and experience teach that many do not persevere, but fall away from grace. Yet this does not happen because God is unwilling to preserve to the end the believers whom he has once received into grace, but because many through despair, carelessness, and fleshly works drive out the Spirit and reject faith. Therefore people must not be taught that they cannot fall out of God’s grace however they behave. Rather, they must be exhorted to mortify the works of the flesh through the Spirit, cling firmly to Christ through faith, ask God for perseverance, and struggle so that they do not lose perseverance through the wantonness of the flesh. Doing this, they must not doubt perseverance, but believe that God, who began it in them, will also complete it until the day of Christ, etc. {Phil. 1. 6.}

<!-- source-block:279 -->
And immediately afterwards, having put forward various passages of Scripture that exhort us to fear, such as 1 Corinthians 10. 12, Philippians 2. 12, Romans 11. 20, 1 Peter 1. 17, Proverbs 28. 14, he answers concerning these sayings:

<!-- source-block:280 -->
> These are not exhortations to doubt in faith, but admonitions that faith, possessing the certainty of salvation, should not be extinguished, etc.

<!-- source-block:281 -->
Read that whole treatise of Chemnitz, beginning at the start of folio 166, column 1, and ending at the end of folio 174, column 1. Likewise he also writes in the second part of the same book, page 370, column 1:

<!-- source-block:282 -->
> Our people expressly teach that the baptized who sin here in this life against their conscience do not retain, but drive out, faith and the Holy Spirit; lose the grace of justification and eternal life; and become liable to God’s wrath and eternal condemnation unless they repent. Therefore the passage cited as 12. arti 6. of the Augsburg Confession rejects those who teach that one can become so perfect here that one cannot sin; and also those who blasphemously teach that a person once justified cannot lose the Holy Spirit, however great the wickedness in which they wallow.

<!-- source-block:283 -->
Do you think this opinion of Chemnitz and the Augsburg Confession conflicts with ours? I judge that it does not: rather, what they say and what we say is exactly the same. Now let us also hear Hemmingsen speak. {Nicolaus Hemmingius.} In his handbook, class 1, chapter 12, de pœnitentiæ (on repentance), page 109, he says as follows:

<!-- source-block:284 -->
> The faith of trust belongs properly to God’s children.

<!-- source-block:285 -->
And chapter 14, page 129:

<!-- source-block:286 -->
> The godly are distinguished from the ungodly in three things: first, in election before God; second, in love before other people; third, in the confidence of their hearts before themselves.

<!-- source-block:287 -->

Oock versekert zijn dat se haer gheloove en heylicheyt behouden sullen tont eynde toe: so moeten dan alle wedergeborene versekert zijn datse haer geloove/etc. behouden sullen.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C116">[Margin: Observe here: all who are called, justified, and sanctified—that is, all the regenerate—must assure themselves that they are elect. > First, calling. For Paul, Romans 8.30, wants each person’s calling to serve him as a testimony of his election. For God’s calling is firm and unchangeable, so that God will not depart from it. Second, the Holy Spirit inwardly working faith, assuring us of God’s promises, and stirring us to God’s service. And when we hear that kingdoms, countries, cities, and distinguished people fall away from the Gospel, we shall comfort ourselves with this doctrine of election, so that we remember Paul’s words: {2. <!-- source-block:299 -->
## Answer.

<!-- source-block:300 -->
Dear God, that this Bertius can set all shame so utterly aside! To what can a person not fall once he begins to turn away! Day and night cannot differ more than Junius and Bertius differ, and yet Bertius dared to make these two one. It is true: the words Bertius recounts here stand thus in Junius. But if Bertius has read Junius himself, he knows well that he does Junius manifest violence when he wants to smear him with such a foul opinion.

<!-- source-block:301 -->
The first saying Bertius recounts here reads as follows:

<!-- source-block:302 -->
> We are preserved in Christ through the same means by which we are joined to Christ. Now we are joined to Christ through faith and hope; therefore we are also preserved in Christ through these same means. Consequently we must strive to retain firmly to the end the faith we have in God, and also the glorying in Christian hope which we display before angels and people. Nothing whatsoever will be lacking on the Lord’s side, if we ourselves are not at fault.

<!-- source-block:303 -->
Is there anything here that can be drawn to Bertius’s side? Then let Junius explain himself still further from the second saying Bertius adduces here:

<!-- source-block:304 -->
> It properly belongs to Christ to call his companions back from an evil and unbelieving heart and from an ungodly departure; also, through the holy admonitions of the Word, by his Holy Spirit, to free them from sin’s deceit and from the hardening that might follow it.

<!-- source-block:305 -->
> But we are Christ’s companions—that is, we have come to share in Christ. Therefore Christ, who is the head and perfecter of our faith, will also properly bring about all these things in us. For Christ does for his own, without any condition, everything they need for their sanctification, and requires of us one single condition, namely that we remain in him and be watchful, keeping ourselves from all sin and unbelief, just as he promises to remain in us.

<!-- source-block:306 -->
Does Junius still side with Bertius here? Let us then put forward the third saying, although it is rather long. Here, says Junius, two questions arise:

<!-- source-block:307 -->
> First, whether it can happen that those who have received the gifts the apostle recounts here fall away from those same gifts. Second, whether it is honourable to God, or rather harmful, to say that it cannot happen that he—God—renews to repentance those who have fallen away in this way. To the first question we answer briefly that they can fall away. For if they could not fall away, the apostle would not make this proposition, nor derive so grave a sentence from it, nor apply this statement on the matter he treats to the Hebrews to whom he writes. Nevertheless, since all indefinite propositions, if they are contingent, are of such a nature that they cannot properly be called universal propositions, one must also understand that among those who have received these aforesaid gifts there are some who fall away, and also some who do not and cannot fall away. The apostle describes the condition and state of those who fall away in verses 4 and 5 so clearly that by contrast one can also perceive the condition and state of those who cannot fall away. Not all people are fit to receive the aforesaid gifts rightly and savingly. There are indeed some who receive them to some extent and in part, but afterwards fall away miserably through their own wickedness, although not all who receive these gifts rightly and savingly are those whom God has given to his Son according to the good pleasure of his will—that is, the elect. [“Not all” here sits uneasily with the distinction Junius draws below.] To the godly and ungodly are given the same gifts and the same light. But this light and these gifts, together with the other operations proceeding from God, differ partly in greatness and measure, and partly in appropriation and enjoyment.

<!-- source-block:308 -->
> In greatness: for the elect are given the root, the source itself; therefore they also cannot lose the little streams or fruits given them through the Spirit of sanctification, since none of those whom the Father has given to Christ will be lost from his hand. But the others are given only these little streams or branches to taste, and that without their root or source. Therefore they also readily wither and abandon their sanctification, drawing back from God. In appropriation: for these gifts adhere firmly to the godly, and are not only presented and set before their eyes or ears, but are also received with the heart through faith and a good conscience, and stored in the heart’s treasury, so that at the appropriate time good things may be brought forth from that same treasury. But to the others, who do not have the seed of godliness in Christ Jesus, these manifestations of light and other gifts of God are presented in such a way that they do not savingly appropriate them to themselves, since in them these are not mingled with a right—or justifying—faith through the Spirit of sanctification, etc. From all this it is concluded, as we said at the beginning, that those who have received the gifts the apostle mentions here can fall away: namely, all who have not been given to Christ by the Father, nor incorporated into him or rooted in him. For every planting which the Father has not planted will be uprooted. Conversely, it cannot happen that those who are in Christ Jesus through God’s good pleasure according to his election fall away from sharing in and enjoying these gifts of God, since they are truly built upon the true and strongest foundation, which even the gates of hell will never be able to weaken, etc.

<!-- source-block:309 -->
What do you think now? Does Junius still say here that the regenerate can fall away from their faith? Or does Bertius deal in bad faith with Junius, even against his own conscience? “You shall not bear false witness against your neighbour,” says God the Lord. {Exod 20.16 Mat. 26 61 1. Reg. 21.13} Why, then, does Bertius still do it? But so that everyone may know still better how badly Bertius treats this highly learned and excellent man, take also these few words as evidence of Junius’s view in this matter. In his disputation 33, θ [thesis]. 11, he says as follows:

<!-- source-block:310 -->
> This true faith, although weak, is nevertheless always joined with the gift of perseverance to the end.

<!-- source-block:311 -->
And in disputation 37, corollary 2:

<!-- source-block:312 -->
> Although that Christian life does not always manifest itself in believers, and although they sometimes fall into gross sins, we nevertheless firmly say that it cannot happen that their leaves should wither entirely, for they always retain moisture in the root, which bears fruit in its season.

<!-- source-block:313 -->
Psalm 1. In the exposition of the Epistle of Jude, verses 1 and 3, he also teaches the same thing very expressly, and in the book On Nature and Grace, in argument 6:

<!-- source-block:314 -->
> In the people whom God has elected, he says, a twofold grace appears: first, that they are freed from Satan’s power and tyranny; {Col. 1.13.} second, that by that same grace they are kept united with God in Christ through the unity of the Spirit, so that they are no longer deserters, as they were before, and cannot again desert the one they had deserted. For then they stood by nature’s strength and fell through inability; but now they are preserved by God’s power and stand in Christ, who is the utterly unchangeable power and wisdom of God. {1 Petr. 1.5.}

<!-- source-block:315 -->
Likewise in various other places.

<!-- source-block:316 -->
Thus it can now be seen without any mistake or doubt that all those ancient and modern teachers of the church whom Bertius so boasts of having on his side think very differently about this point under dispute from what Bertius is making the simple believe. {Pa. 84. li. 16.} Yet Bertius is also ashamed that people should say he was precisely the man who first devised out of his own head this fine piece of work, this doctrine of the apostasy of the saints—besides the fact that he himself, and all who know him, well know that he is not capable enough for that. I must therefore say what is true: the foundations of this doctrine were taught long before Christ’s birth in the schools of Plato and Aristotle, who were very excellent philosophers in Greece. {In de voorreden let. B. sac. 2. liii. 18. ende 25.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C125">[Margin: In the preface, letter B, side 2, liii. 18 and 25.]</span>

Afterwards, when Marcion arose among Christians, as recounted, and began to teach the unfounded tenets of the Stoic philosophers, to whom Plato and Aristotle were opposed, some ancient teachers of the earliest churches wrote so expansively about free will that not long afterwards the arch-heretic Pelagius sought thereby to defend and spread his falsehoods. Pelagius was the first—I must speak the truth, whether Bertius likes it or not—who, around the year 382, as some reckon, spoke in express words about the falling away or apostasy of the saints as Bertius speaks of it, teaching and advancing still other fine (so-called) little tenets, some of which Bertius also accepts. In this book he also uses very many arguments, indeed his foremost ones, taken or borrowed from Plato, Aristotle, and Pelagius, as anyone who merely reads the epistle of Prosper and Hilary to Augustine will be able to see clearly. {Dit is door desen wijtlustigher en claerder verhaelt.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C126">[Margin: This has been recounted previously at greater length and more clearly.]</span>

Consequently this should not be called a new opinion—one wrongs Bertius in that—but an old one, already known in God’s church about 1200 years ago, and also rejected and condemned by God’s church. But perhaps someone will think Bertius is the first and only person to have dredged up this filth again. That is a mistake. Socinus, Castellio, Coornhert, and similar restless spirits were at work before him. What is more, the churches of Saxony, says Bertius, of {Pa 19. l. 14. Pa. 101. l. 29} Lübeck, Hamburg, Lüneburg, Rostock, and others, and perhaps also some individual honourable and learned people here and there, all side with him.

That may be so. But is it not nevertheless surprising that Bertius draws this to his advantage? For—to say nothing for now of the opinion of these aforesaid churches and some of their teachers, such as Pomeranus, Hesshusius, Fabricius, and others of that kind who differ from Bertius’s view—is Bertius not in Holland? Does he not profess to be a member of the Netherlandish churches? Does he not acknowledge himself bound to serve, edify, and defend the Reformed church of Leiden, of Holland, and of the United Provinces, chiefly and more than the churches abroad? Why, then, does he boast of agreeing with churches that regard him as worse than a heathen and a tax collector, abandoning the churches that would gladly regard him as their brother? Why does he seek to strengthen in their opinion and gladden those far from him, while weakening and grieving so many godly souls among whom he moves daily? This boasting is not good, and this zeal is not pure. {1. Corint. 5. 6. Galat. 4. 17.}

If we too were to count all the churches and universities, together with all the preachers and doctors in Germany, Switzerland, France, England, Scotland, and elsewhere—not to mention these Netherlands—who have ever held, and still hold, the view Bertius contradicts in his book, we could produce a thousand where Bertius could not find ten. Why, then, are all those churches named here individually—Lübeck, Danzig, Hamburg, etc.? To make a show, so that Bertius would not stand entirely alone. For my part, I could well wish that he stood in their midst, and had stood there long before now: it would always have been little loss to the country and the College at Leiden, and great benefit to our afflicted churches. Indeed, there must also be sects among us, so that those who are sound in faith among us may become manifest. {1. Cor. 11. 19.}

<!-- source-block:317 -->
Thus Bertius deserves that honour and thanks as much as anyone else. For the time being he is already half a martyr:

<!-- source-block:318 -->
> I thank you, Lord Jesus, he says, that you have willed that I too should suffer something for your name.

<!-- source-block:319 -->
{In de voorreden litt. A 4. lin. 20.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-citation="C130">[Margin: In the preface, letter A, 4, line 20.]</span> It would not be good for him to become a whole martyr. For how could one then talk to those people? Yet we are not allowed to count it in our favour that so many thousands of people professing the Reformed religion in France, England, and the Netherlands have been miserably killed and confirmed with their blood the same doctrine which Bertius now besmirches with his ink and would gladly wrest from us by force against our will.
