<!-- B001 -->
But let us proceed.

<!-- B002 -->
> Some, says Bertius, {Pa. 53. l. 32.} having been driven out of both these strongholds, have devised a third refuge for themselves in the hiding places of a temporary faith. When they hear us argue thus—temporary faith is abandoned; now, temporary faith was a true faith; therefore some true faith is abandoned—when, I say, they hear this, they teach that a temporary faith is not a true faith; and they judge in precisely the same way concerning righteousness, love and hope. These people seem to me to be saying that Bucephalus, because he died, was never a real horse. For duration contributes nothing to the essence of the thing, etc. {Pag. 54. l. 3.}

<!-- B003 -->
Answer. Bertius once again twists our words here. We do not teach that duration, properly speaking, contributes anything to the essence of true faith. Otherwise a true believer would not be a true believer when he truly believes, but would first have to become a true believer a long time afterwards, namely after he had already believed for a long time. This is false. Nor, in the second place, do we deny that temporary faith can in some sense be called true faith. For, as Bertius describes true faith, we readily acknowledge that temporary faith may be called a true faith, since it too believes the promises of the holy Gospel, which are true, and accepts them as true. But when we say that temporary faith is not a true faith, we mean a true faith of such a kind that it also has the power to save all those who possess it. For all true faith, and only true faith, can save. Temporary faith, however, not only saves no one in actuality, because it is temporary: even if it were retained to the end (although it would then not be called a temporary faith), it could save no one. For Christ says: {Mat. 13.20. Luc 8.13.}

<!-- B004 -->
> The one sown in stony ground is the person who hears the word and immediately receives it with joy; and he no longer has any root within himself, but is inconstant.

<!-- B005 -->
Notice this: could a person in whom the good seed, falling as into stony ground, neither takes root nor bears fruit, {Hebr. 6.8.} really be saved? I think Bertius himself will say no. Thus, I say, this person does not have true faith either, and consequently temporary faith is not true faith.

<!-- B006 -->
Further: does the temporariness or perishability of temporary faith contribute anything to the essence of that same faith? Nothing at all. Rather, the essence or constitution of this faith causes and brings about that temporariness: for it has no root. Therefore it must perish and be temporary. In the same way, duration contributes nothing to the essence of true faith either; rather, the essence and constitution of true faith bring about that duration. For the seed of this faith is imperishable and has fallen into good ground, where it too has put down a good root. {1. Petr 1.23. Luc 8.15.} Therefore it is itself eternal and imperishable. Does Bertius think this so utterly absurd? Why, then?

<!-- B007 -->
> Sight that lasts only one year, he says, {Pa. 54.li.3.} is just as much sight as that which lasts twenty years in succession; and whether one loses one's sight after twenty years or after only one year, the loss of sight will nevertheless be called blindness, because real sight is lost. Faith stands in just the same relation: certainly it could not be lost if one did not possess it.

<!-- B008 -->
Answer. It is true: no one abandons faith except someone who has it. And someone who has true faith, even if he has had it for only one hour, {Matt.20.6. Luc 23 42.} nevertheless has a true faith. Everyone who believes also really has a faith. But what follows from that? Does not Bertius himself say {Pa. 57.li.22.} that faith is true, or false and feigned? Must everyone who has faith, and really has faith, and really abandons faith, therefore necessarily possess and abandon a true faith as well? What if it were a false faith? Indeed, what if it were a feigned faith? That blind man who had been brought halfway to health by Christ, {Marc 8.24} and said, “I see people, for I see things like trees walking”—he certainly had sight, for he says, “I see.” And he really had that sight, rather than merely imagining it, as he demonstrates by saying, “I see things like trees walking.” But did he therefore also have true sight, that is, proper, naturally complete sight? The text says: {Vers 25.}

<!-- B009 -->
> Afterwards he (Christ) again laid his hands on his eyes and made him see. And he was restored, and saw them all clearly.

<!-- B010 -->
So it is with faith too. Someone who has temporary faith has faith and some enlightenment; and he really has this, otherwise he would be a hypocrite. But he does not have true, proper, saving faith. Neither has he been restored, nor does he see clearly everything that God has revealed in his holy Word, as do those who were once darkness but have now become light in the Lord. {Coloss.1.18. ende ca. 5 8. 2. Cor 3 18.} And when such a person falls away from faith, he does not fall away from true faith, which he did not have, but from that temporary faith. He does not lose complete sight, but an initial enlightenment, which is indeed called sight and faith, though not as properly as true faith and complete enlightenment are called faith and sight. And just as the loss of such half-sight may be called blindness no less than the loss of complete sight, so there is every reason to call the abandonment of temporary faith a falling away, just as the abandonment of true faith—if that could be abandoned—would be called a falling away.

<!-- B011 -->
Bertius goes on to quote what Christ said to the sick man he had healed:

<!-- B012 -->
> See, you have become well; sin no more, {Joan. 5.14.} lest something worse happen to you.

<!-- B013 -->
He adds this conclusion:

<!-- B014 -->
> Suppose that he sinned again {pag.54. l.14.} and was severely punished by Christ—for that could have happened—who will be so senseless as to deny that it was a true healing?

<!-- B015 -->
I answer: no one. But what does this contribute to the matter? Do we say that what is truly good cannot be lost? By no means. Health, wealth, prosperity, outward peace and other such things can very well be lost. But who will be so senseless as to say that all God's goods and gifts are of the same nature, {mat.7.24.26 en c.17 19 23 en ca 25.29. 2. Ti. 2 18 19} so that all of them alike can be retained, and all of them alike can also be lost? The goods of the spirit as well as the goods of the body? The goods that God gives only to believers as well as those that he often shares with unbelievers too? The goods that God himself, according to his particular promise, constantly and always preserves in his children, as well as the goods {Jere.32.39.40. 1. Petr 1.5. Joan 10.28 1. Tim.4.10} for which God takes no such great care? It would be altogether too absurd to argue as follows: some goods can be lost; therefore all goods can be lost. Some creatures can die; therefore the angels can die. Some creatures can be brought from the state of damnation to salvation; therefore the devils too can be brought to salvation. Some people can believe, do good, attain eternal life; therefore all people can believe, do good and attain eternal life. Some people can fall away from their faith and good works; therefore all people can fall away from their faith and good works. Some faith can be abandoned; therefore all faith can be abandoned. This, I say, is altogether too absurd. For although, as regards their nature, the angels could die the eternal death just as the devils could, who were also once angels, and the devils could be saved just as well as human beings, and among human beings one person could reject or acquire faith, retain or abandon it, as well as another, nevertheless we say that God's good pleasure has so ordered this {Augusti Encher. c.57. Iudæ vers 6. 2 Pet 2 4. Ioan. 12.39. Ro. 11.10 14 2 Cor 10.5. Acto: 1.48.} that the angels now cannot die; the devils cannot be saved; not all people can acquire faith or reject it; and not all faith can be abandoned: namely, saving faith cannot be abandoned, for the reasons set out earlier.

<!-- B016 -->
> In falling away, Bertius continues, {Pag 54.22} there is a point from which one departs. Falling away is counted as guilt, and the guilt lies in abandoning the good. It follows, then, that the apostate possessed a true good.

*Reading note: The opening does not make clear how Bertius introduces the point of departure. The verb linking guilt to abandoning the good is also unclear; “lies in” gives the likely sense of his inference.*

<!-- B017 -->
Answer. The apostate who abandons the truth which he has known, accepted and professed abandons a true good: that is beyond dispute. But does he therefore also abandon true faith? Then every true good would be a true faith. Yet we have just heard that health, which is not faith, is also a true good—not to speak now of countless other goods that believers and unbelievers possess and abandon or lose, which are not faith, much less true faith.

<!-- B018 -->
> Finally, {pag. 54.l.26.} says Bertius, one cannot teach more clearly that some believing or holy people fall away than by saying that some believers or holy people do not endure, but are temporary, etc.

<!-- B019 -->
Answer. That some believers fall away from faith we acknowledge to be true. But to infer from this, “Therefore they fall away from true faith,” is to mock intelligent people. But not all faith is true faith. Therefore not everyone who abandons faith abandons true faith. Even children would understand this.

<!-- B020 -->
As for the discourse Bertius further adds here concerning predestination and reprobation, it is entirely beside the point. For our question is not to whom God gives true faith, and to whom he gives temporary faith: to the elect, namely, or to the reprobate? Nor why he gives this faith to one and that faith to another. Rather, the question is whether the faith that is abandoned is also a true faith. Bertius ought to have addressed this and left all other irrelevant questions alone. But it seems that he has merely sought an opportunity to make the teaching of our churches stink among ordinary people. Therefore, for the honour of the truth, I too must pause briefly over this matter.

<!-- B021 -->
> They allow the reprobate only temporary faith, he says, {Pag. 55. l. 8.} speaking of us, and say that true faith is given to no one but the elect.

<!-- B022 -->
Answer. It is true that we teach this; nor, when we teach it, do we know otherwise than that we teach the truth. For we say that God has not decreed from eternity to save all people, but believers alone. (I am speaking of ordinary adult human beings. Otherwise salvation is not for believers alone, unless one wished to exclude all little children from salvation, together with all the good, elect angels, and even the human nature of Jesus Christ.) Now it is certain that no one becomes a believer unless God gives him faith and has decreed from eternity to give it. Thus, since not all people but only some become believers, God has not decreed from eternity to give faith to all people, but only to some.

<!-- B023 -->
Who, then, are these people? Here lies the greatest difficulty. We say that they must be either those whom God foreknew and knew would by nature be more disposed and better fitted than other people to accept one and the same grace offered to them {Het van is in het eerste deel oock wat ghesproken.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M20">Marginal translation: Something has also been said about this in the first part.</span> and to persevere in it; or they must be those to whom God, knowing that by nature they were constituted just like other people, prepared in advance to show greater grace than to other people. That is, they must be either those whom something in nature would distinguish from other people, or those whom grace alone would distinguish from other people. It cannot be nature that sets these people apart. For, to use no other reasons now, nature and natural corruption—including unfitness and inability to believe {Rom.3.23. 1. Cor. 1. 2. 14. ende c. 4. 9. 2. Cor 3 5. Eph.2.3.}—are common to all people, and equally great in all people before grace is added. Thus grace must set these people apart. But grace is of two kinds: one common, the other particular. Common grace is what God shows to all those to whom he has his word preached. But this grace does not set these people apart. For God has not decreed from eternity to give faith to some in the sense of all those to whom he would have his word preached. This is clearly evident from the fact that not all those who hear the word preached become believers and are saved, whereas it is beyond all doubt that only those—and also all those—to whom God has decreed from eternity to give faith become believers.

It must therefore be a particular grace. It is this grace that, in the people of whom we are speaking, [unreadable]. This is the particular working of the Holy Spirit. Through it God works in the understanding, will and heart in a particular way, doing what he decreed from eternity to do in them. He does this not in all to whom the Gospel is preached—otherwise this too would be common grace—but only in those he pleases. All this serves the end for which he ordained them in his foresight from eternity, namely eternal salvation. Therefore we say that those to whom God has decreed from eternity to give saving faith and to whom he also gives it in time are the elect alone.

<!-- B024 -->
As for the reprobate, we do not believe that God has decreed to give them any faith: {Datio fidei, non ingreditur definitionem reprobationis.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M22">Marginal translation: The giving of faith does not enter into the definition of reprobation.</span> neither true nor false, neither temporary nor feigned, neither historical nor miraculous faith, or whatever else one might call it. For God does nothing, and does not decree to do anything, except with a definite end in view. And what end would all this faith have served for the reprobate? To damn them? They already lay in damnation before they were reprobated. Or to save them? Then they could not have been reprobate. Or to aggravate their damnation? {Luce 12.47.} I do not attribute that to the good God. Not that all the knowledge of God and of the good which the reprobate possess does not serve to aggravate their damnation: I am not saying that.

<!-- B025 -->
But that God should have decreed to give the reprobate any faith {Aliud est decretum providentiæ, aliud prædestinationis.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M24">Marginal translation: The decree of providence is one thing; that of predestination is another.</span> for this reason or end, in order that he might afterwards punish them the more severely: I do not attribute that to God. I therefore much prefer to believe that, when the reprobate acquire some knowledge, they acquire it not in accordance with God's decree, nor, for all that, against God's decree, but outside his decree. Thus, when God has his word preached wherever he pleases, his sole intention is thereby to call and gather his people and his sheep, {Ezech.36. Actor.26.0. 7.10. esa.18.10. Matt.9.13. esa11.28. Luce19.10.} that is, all those whom he has given to Christ from eternity, and no others. Yet because God, who alone knows his own, has them called through people who do not know them, it happens that many of the reprobate, hearing this outward voice which goes out to all but does not concern all, often accept the promises of the holy Gospel too.

<!-- B026 -->
It is like a sower going out to sow: {Matt.13.3.} his sole intention or purpose is to cast his good seed into good ground, yet outside his intention some falls on the road, some among thorns and some on stones. Likewise, a fisherman casting his net into the sea {Mat.13.47.} seeks only to catch living fish, yet also catches some dead ones. Likewise, a shepherd who knows his sheep {Joan.10.3.} and is known by them, when calling his sheep that are walking among the goats, cannot call them without the goats hearing it too; and sometimes these too run into the pastures with the sheep. So I believe it happens here with the preached word of God as well. Nor ought Bertius to find this so utterly strange and inappropriate. For is not God's law preached to bring the unrepentant to knowledge of their sins, sorrow for them, and abandonment of them? But just look: how often does the preaching of the law set sins before the eyes of those who cannot be converted or restored? For those who fall away completely, says Bertius, {pag 17 l.15.} cannot return. Yet the Jews who had fallen away completely {Jerem 2 21. Hos.4 1. Esa 18. l.19.}—as Bertius himself testifies concerning them—were daily addressed thus: “Repent, repent; turn away from your godlessness,” {Jerem.3 12 Ezech.4.4.14 Hos.14 2.} and the like. Why did God do this? So that they should all repent? They cannot all be converted, as has just been said. But God hoped, says Bertius, {pag 19. li.3.} that they would repent. What? Did God then hope that something would happen which could not happen? Or at least something which he knew with certainty would not happen? This is an altogether too unfitting way of speaking about God: no human being would wish to hope in that way. Well, why did God nevertheless have preaching addressed thus to the Jews? For the sake of the remnant, as Bertius calls them, whom God knew, but the prophets did not know. {siet Bertium pag.20.l 37 pag.21. l.30.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M33">Marginal translation: See Bertius pag.20.l 37 pag.21. l.30.</span>

<!-- B027 -->
Likewise, are not the promises of the holy Gospel preached in order to set Christ with his merits before the repentant, for their comfort and release from their sins and deserved punishments? {Luc.24.47.} Yet how often does it happen that the unrepentant too, hearing the preaching of the holy Gospel, acquire from it knowledge of the Saviour and a desire for salvation? {Luc.14.15. Apoc.3.17.} Indeed, how often does it happen that such people ascribe salvation to themselves with firm assurance and without any doubt—yet also without any foundation, as will be explained below? {siet Bertium pag.31.l.30.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M36">Marginal translation: See Bertius pag.31.l.30.</span>

<!-- B028 -->
But does all this accord with God's intention? By no means. For it has never in the least been God's intention to promise forgiveness of sins, to grant it, and consequently to have it proclaimed, to anyone but the repentant alone. {Mich.2.7. Jerem.3.12. Marc 1.15} Thus, when the unrepentant, who are mixed with the repentant, gain such knowledge, desire, hope, faith and trust as we have just described from the teaching that God causes to be proclaimed only to the repentant, this occurs outside God's intention, and outside, not against, his eternal decree. That is, those who are and remain unrepentant, and are therefore reprobate, do not acquire some knowledge and faith in order that the decree God has made concerning them may retain its force. For God has not decreed, as has just been said, to give the reprobate any faith. Rather, he has only decreed to leave certain people, as he pleased, lying in the damnation into which they had fallen through their own fault: that is reprobation. It is true that from this decree it necessarily follows that the reprobate neither will nor can obtain true saving faith. For otherwise they could, indeed would, be delivered from damnation, and God's decree concerning them would be nullified. But it is quite another thing to say that the reprobate cannot and will not obtain saving faith—that is, God has decreed not to give some people that by which they might be delivered from the damnation in which they lie—and another thing to say that God has decreed to give the reprobate some faith, namely temporary faith (as Bertius speaks of it) {pag.55.l.12.}. This latter statement means that God has decreed to give some people a measure of saving grace—for Bertius maintains that temporary faith, if retained, can save {pag.57.l.12.}—so that he should thereby have just cause to aggravate their damnation still further. I hold the first statement to be true, since it serves to demonstrate God's righteous severity and the free and plenary power that he has over his sinful creatures. I hold the second to be false, since it serves to slander God's eternal wisdom, righteousness and mercy.

<!-- B029 -->
When we teach, then, that the reprobate cannot obtain true saving faith, we do indeed acknowledge thereby that the faith they possess, however fine it may appear, is not true faith. But we add that its failure to be true faith is not because those who possess it are reprobate, although Bertius here accuses us of saying this. {pag.55.l.19.} Rather, we say, it is not true faith because it does not possess, all together, the properties previously described which belong to the essence of true faith, as will be shown more fully below.

<!-- B030 -->
In saying this, we do not blame God either for this faith being merely temporary or for the damnation of those who possess it, as Bertius imagines. {Ibid. lin.21} Rather, we put the blame for both on the reprobate themselves. Their faith is temporary not because God, through his decree, limits and, as it were, sustains or suppresses some power they already possess to persevere. [The next clause mentions this decree of God; it is unclear what it affirms or denies about the decree.] Rather, their faith is temporary because, being stony and uncircumcised in heart, {Luc.8.13.} it is they themselves who set the limit: the word cannot take root in them, and so they cannot persevere in faith.

<!-- B031 -->
It is true that God has not decreed to give the reprobate the power to be able to believe unto salvation and persevere in that faith. But does Bertius call that limiting their power? And would God therefore be a cause of their being temporary and being damned? What, then, of those to whom God has decreed never to have his word preached? For it is certainly the case that God has not decreed to send his word and his Holy Spirit to all people in general and to each person in particular. Otherwise, why does he not send them to everyone? He surely would if he wished, and he would wish to if he had decreed it beforehand. Nevertheless no person can believe unless God sends him his word and his Spirit. {Rom. 10.17.} On Bertius’s reasoning, then, God too, through this decree of his, must be a cause of so many people's not believing and consequently being damned, seeing that

<!-- B032 -->
> no work, as Bertius says, {pag.55.l.23.} can exceed the power of the worker; and consequently he who limits the power is a cause of the omission of the whole ensuing work and of the outcome arising from it.

<!-- B033 -->
> Next, Bertius continues, if this faith is from God, why is it not true? How is it possible that what flows from the truly good God should not be truly good?

<!-- B034 -->
Answer. In temporary faith there is knowledge, assent and trust. Knowledge of and assent to the good and the truth are good, and therefore from God. But the trust is not from God, for it is not good, because it is false. For those who are stony and uncircumcised in heart, and therefore not truly repentant, may not hope, believe or trust that their sins have been forgiven them and that they will be saved.

<!-- B035 -->
> Furthermore, {Ibid.lin 32} what wise man has ever distinguished qualities or attributes that differ in their own essences according to the persons in whom they are found?

<!-- B036 -->
Answer. We do not distinguish faith, righteousness and holiness according to the persons in whom they are found, but according to their own essence. We say that what is constituted as we have described above is true faith, righteousness and holiness; and that what is not so constituted is not true faith, righteousness and holiness.

<!-- B037 -->
Paul was chosen from eternity; nevertheless, the faith he had before his conversion was a false faith. One of the criminals crucified with Christ had been chosen from eternity; nevertheless, before his conversion his life was sheer godlessness. We say the same of all others too. Truth is always truth, even if the devil speaks it, {Matth.16.17.} and a lie is always a lie, even if an angel from heaven speaks it. {Galat.1.8.}

<!-- B038 -->
> Nevertheless, these same people {pag.55.l 38.}—which is astonishing—when it comes to the essential workings of faith, necessarily ascribe to this temporary faith of theirs all the things peculiar to true faith. See, he says, {Pag 56.li.3} so great is the power of truth that even those who fight it most fiercely often come to confess it unawares.

<!-- B039 -->
Answer. And so great is the power of error that even those who always boast of truth often come unawares to depart from it most shamefully. What, then? Do we ascribe to temporary faith all the things peculiar to true faith? Bertius, that particular lover of truth, says yes and no. {pag.56 l.34} He says no where he writes:

<!-- B040 -->
> What, then, is still lacking in this faith? Trust, they say. Let it be so. Trust, then, will belong to the elect alone.

<!-- B041 -->
Therefore by Bertius's own admission we do not ascribe to temporary faith all the things peculiar to true faith. Indeed, we do not ascribe to temporary faith anything peculiar to true faith, for true trust alone is peculiar to true faith, and we do not ascribe that to temporary faith, says Bertius. Nevertheless he also says yes: they ascribe to temporary faith all the things peculiar to true faith. And he proves this as follows: {Ibid.lin.6.}

<!-- B042 -->
> They ascribe the following things to temporary faith. 1. That it has a great likeness and kinship to the faith of the elect. 2. That those who possess it imagine that they possess true faith, and that they are affected by almost the same feeling as the elect—indeed, in their own judgement they do not differ from the elect. 3. That such temporary faith is imparted by the Lord to the hearts of people, whom he illuminates in such a way that they acknowledge his grace and feel that his mercy is with them. 4. That through this faith they not only believe that there is a God and that the Gospel histories and other parts of Scripture are true, but also hold God's word to be an undoubted truth; that they do not wholly despise God's commandments; that they are earnestly moved by God's threats and promises; that they know Christ as the author of life and salvation and willingly have themselves enrolled among his own; that they believe God is gracious to them and have a sense of God's grace. 5. That, as the Apostle says, {Ebræo: 6 4.} they are enlightened and taste the heavenly gift, and partake of the Holy Spirit, and taste God's good word and the powers of the world to come. 6. That all these things are often greater and stronger in them than in the elect.

<!-- B043 -->
Answer. Does Bertius really know all that he is saying here? He is to prove that we ascribe to temporary faith all the things peculiar to true faith. Yet, after recounting everything he knew to recount (without indicating which of our people spoke in this way, so that one might have looked it up to see whether it was actually said and meant as Bertius understands it), he does not bring to light a single point peculiar to true faith which we ascribe to temporary faith.

<!-- B044 -->
It is true that we ascribe to temporary faith many things belonging to faith. But one must observe a distinction between the properties of faith and the properties of true faith. For the properties of faith are present in false faith and temporary faith just as much, and just as completely, as in true faith; otherwise false and temporary faith could not be called faith. But because they are called faith and possess all the properties of faith, it does not follow that they also possess the properties of true faith. Otherwise they would no longer be, or be called, false or temporary faith, but true faith. Thus Bertius speaks falsely when he says that we ascribe to temporary faith all the things peculiar to true faith. For we do not ascribe the properties of true faith to temporary faith, but only the properties of faith, such as: 1. Having knowledge and understanding. 2. Holding what one knows to be true. 3. Finally, ascribing salvation to oneself on the basis of what one knows and holds to be true. If what one holds to be true is lies, it is a false faith; and this is the property of false faith: holding lies to be true. If what one holds to be true is truth, it is a true faith, as Bertius calls it. If there is then no trust accompanying it, it is an incomplete and devilish faith. {Jacob. 2.19} If trust accompanies it, but that trust is unfounded, then it is indeed complete faith, but nevertheless not saving faith. Indeed, in this respect it is a false faith: and this is the property of temporary faith. But if well-founded trust accompanies it, then it is true saving faith. Such trust is the property of true faith, a property which we do not ascribe to temporary faith, as is well known and admitted by Bertius himself. {pag.56 l.34}

<!-- B045 -->
> Yet meanwhile they say, {Ibid.lin.7.} says Bertius, that there is a great likeness between true faith and temporary faith, and that they have many things in common with one another.

<!-- B046 -->
Answer. That is true. But people commonly say, “Not everything similar is identical”: one finds more things alike than things identical. Satan knows how to transform himself so that he bears a very great resemblance to an angel of light, {2.Cor.11.14 15.} and his servants so that they greatly resemble the servants of righteousness. Yet they are not thereby those whom they resemble so well.

<!-- B047 -->
A human being has many things in common with beasts, but is nevertheless not a beast on that account. A fool often imagines himself wise; and someone who has a false faith thinks he has a true faith, and hopes or trusts that he will be saved through his faith, at least as firmly as those who have true faith do—as may be seen among the Jews, Turks and others. Yet one may not therefore say that a fool is wise, or that false faith is true and saving faith. It is the same with temporary faith too. It has a great likeness to true faith, but it is not true faith. Those who have temporary faith readily imagine that they have true faith, because they know the truth, know Christ as the author of salvation, do not wholly despise God's commandments, are to some extent moved by God's promises and threats, and are affected by almost the same sense of God's grace and mercy as true believers—indeed, in their own judgement they do not differ from true believers. But imagining deceives many, particularly when it is built only upon a “to some extent”, an “almost the same”, a “not wholly”, “their own judgement”, and other such reed-staves.

<!-- B048 -->
> If I had the gift of prophecy, {1. Cor.13 2.3} says the Apostle, and knew all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I had all faith, so that I could move mountains, but had no love, I would be nothing. And if I gave all my goods to support the poor, and if I surrendered my body to be burned, but had no love, it would not help me.

<!-- B049 -->
As long as those who have temporary faith lack this love, what good is all their knowledge to them? What good are their outward show, what they imagine themselves to possess, or even all the gifts of the Holy Spirit they may have received? Or would Bertius really say that all those who have temporary faith also have this love? On his reasoning it must be so; otherwise they cannot be saved, which Bertius nevertheless maintains that they can. {pag.57.l.11.} More than that: all who possess temporary faith must be called regenerate children of God, for unless someone is born again, {Joan.3.3.} he cannot see the kingdom of God. And all the regenerate have this love, just as all who have this love are regenerate. For although faith, considered in its own essence, can indeed exist before and without love, nevertheless this love cannot exist in any human being here on earth unless he has faith. And those who have true faith and this love are without {Galat.5 6.} doubt regenerate. But those who do not have this love are not yet regenerate, and those who are not regenerate do not have this love.

<!-- B050 -->
If, then, those who possess temporary faith will be saved, as Bertius says, {pag.57.l.1.} they are regenerate. If they are regenerate, they have the love of which Paul speaks. And what else follows on that reasoning? Temporary faith is a true saving faith; and consequently it necessarily follows that true saving faith can be abandoned, and also that it is in actuality abandoned by many.

<!-- B051 -->
The argument is as follows:
All the regenerate have true saving faith.
All temporary believers are regenerate. Therefore:
All temporary believers have true saving faith. And again:
All temporary believers have true saving faith.
All temporary believers abandon their faith. Therefore:
Some faith that is abandoned is true saving faith.

<!-- B052 -->
Nothing finer could be said on Bertius's behalf to stop our mouths at once. For my part, I would concede him the victory—I say so plainly—if he had presented this argument in this way and had also proved that all temporary believers are regenerate, as is asserted in the second part of the first syllogism. For I do not think that is true, since the truly regenerate have a heart of flesh, {Ezech.36.26} and are good ground, built {Matt. [unreadable]} on a rock, {Ebræ.6.7. Matt.7.24} rooted in Christ, in faith, in love. {Eph.3.17. Coloss.1.23. in cap.2.7.} Temporary believers, on the contrary, have stony hearts and are rocks, bad ground, founded on a sandy place, with no root within them. {Matt.13.20 21. Luc.8 13. Ebræ.6.8. Matt.7.26.} Therefore I fully believe that temporary believers are not regenerate, and consequently that temporary faith is not true faith. For:

<!-- B053 -->
No unregenerate people can be saved.
All temporary believers are unregenerate. Therefore:
No temporary believers can be saved. And again:
No temporary believers, that is, those who have temporary faith, can be saved.
All those who have true faith can be saved. Therefore:
None of those who have temporary faith have true faith.

<!-- B054 -->
And with this all Bertius's arrows lie in the ashes. {pa.57.58.59} As for the other discourses that Bertius further brings forward here, these have either already been touched on above, or they do not particularly concern our dispute.
