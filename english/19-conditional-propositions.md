Let us go on, then.

<!-- B002 -->
> Now those who have had no success by this route, says Bertius, take another. They say: {pag.49 li.37} II. No categorical or firm conclusion can be drawn from a conditional proposition. {pag.50.li.2. etc.} Take these words: “If the righteous man abandons his righteousness, he will die.” They deny that one can conclude from this: “The righteous man can abandon his righteousness.” But pray, in what school do these people argue such things, etc.?

<!-- B003 -->
**Answer.** When I answered Bertius’s arguments, I did not use this “evasion,” as he calls it. Nor did I undertake to defend everything others have said in defence of the truth, but only the truth itself, to the best of my ability. Still, I cannot let everything Bertius says here pass without comment. Even if everything he says is well founded, it can do the truth no harm. My reason for answering is that I think Bertius does a great injustice to the learned men he argues against. {Siet doch van dese uytlegginghe Vorstium tegens Pistorium part. 2. Pag. 64. &c} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M03">Marginal translation: On this interpretation, see Vorstius against Pistorius, part 2, p. 64, etc.</span> {Pag. 50. l. 14} Has anyone ever denied that a conditional proposition can be put in categorical form? Or that, in the language of dialectic, a conditional proposition contains several kinds of statements? No one. {Ibid. l. 35.}

<!-- B004 -->
Here is what they do say. First, not all the statements a conditional proposition contains are always true. Bertius himself admits as much. {Pag. 51. l. 27} Second, given a conditional proposition, one can infer the possibility of the consequent from the possibility of the antecedent. What they deny is that the conditional proposition itself can prove that the antecedent is possible. Thus they grant that from “If the righteous man abandons his righteousness, he will die,” one may quite properly conclude, “The righteous man will die provided that he abandons his righteousness.” But to conclude from the same words that the righteous man *can* abandon his righteousness is, they say, wholly absurd and contrary to all reason. And so it is.

<!-- B005 -->
In the very chapter where those words occur, the prophet Ezekiel says, “If the wicked man turns from his sins, he will live.” {Ezech. 33. 14} On Bertius’s reasoning, we would also have to conclude that all the wicked could come to repentance, even those who have now fallen away from God completely. Are they not wicked too? Indeed they are. Or does God not speak of all the wicked when he says, “If the wicked man turns, he will live,” just as he speaks of all the righteous when he says, “If the righteous man abandons his righteousness, he will die”? To deny that would be absurd. Yet Bertius says:

<!-- B006 -->
> Not all the wicked can come to repentance. Those who fall away completely cannot be renewed. Once salt has lost its saltiness, there is nothing with which it can be salted, etc. {Pag. 17. l. 15} {Pag. 50. li. 4} {Pag. 53 li 3.}

<!-- B007 -->
Is that true? And is the only conclusion we can draw with certainty from “If the wicked man turns, he will live” this: “All the wicked, whoever they are, will live provided that they turn,” and not “All the wicked can come to repentance”? Why, then, does Bertius rebuke and ridicule so many excellent men who say that from “If the righteous man abandons his righteousness, he will die,” one can indeed conclude, “The righteous man will die provided that he abandons his righteousness,” but not “The righteous man can abandon his righteousness”?

<!-- B008 -->
Consider any conditional proposition that promises or threatens something on condition that a person do something. Even if it does not establish that any particular person actually does it, does it nevertheless always establish that the person can do it? {Pag.50.l.23} Why should the promise “If the wicked man turns, he will live” not establish that all the wicked can come to repentance just as surely as the threat “If the righteous man abandons his righteousness, he will die” establishes that all the righteous can abandon their righteousness?

And if a person’s ability entails the possibility or contingency of the event, so that one must acknowledge that it is not impossible for salt to lose its saltiness, {Pag.51.l.30} why, then, will one not also have to acknowledge that it is not impossible for salt that has lost its saltiness to become salty again? {Siet ooc Le. 18. 5. Matt. 19. 17. resolveert de conditionele propositie so als Bertius hier leert oock: [unreadable] sult ghy te recht merckē dat Bertius groot ongelijck heeft.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M13">Marginal translation: See also Le. 18. 5. Matt. 19. 17. Analyse the conditional proposition as Bertius also teaches here: [unreadable] you will rightly see that Bertius is seriously mistaken.</span>

This is beyond my understanding. If our Bertius had not wished to introduce a new philosophy alongside his new theology, or if his zeal had not blinded him so thoroughly, he would undoubtedly not have argued as he does. What is right for him ought to be right for us too; and what is wrong for us could not be anything but wrong for him too.
