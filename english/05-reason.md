<a id="s05-b000"></a>

Let us proceed. Having shown that what Bertius teaches about the falling away of the saints conflicts with God’s Word and the Netherlandish Confession, together with the Heidelberg Catechism unanimously adopted in the Netherlandish churches, let us add one argument here to show that Bertius contradicts himself. I say one argument, for this could be demonstrated in many ways, as will become apparent at a suitable time. I use just this one argument here because it does more than forcefully overturn Bertius’s teaching by showing that he contradicts himself. It also overturns the teaching of his associates and so contradicts them too, in no uncertain terms.

<a id="s05-b001"></a>

Among the propositions that Bertius wants us to grant him, this is the last: “Perseverance in faith is a gift of God.” {pag. 9. lin. 5.} Indeed, he not only wants us to grant him this, but wants it to be held as an indisputable proposition. Therefore Bertius does not doubt it, but holds it as certain that perseverance is a gift of God. Perseverance, I say, and not merely the ability to persevere. The Remonstrants, with whom Bertius thus sides, say that this is not indisputable; rather, they say, perseverance is improperly called a gift. {Inde Collocatie pag. 302 lin. 35.} <span class="marginal-translation" id="s05-m02">[Marginal translation: In the Collocatie, p. 302, line 35.]</span> This is the first disagreement. And in this Bertius sides with us, whereas it appears from what Augustine writes in one passage that the Remonstrants side with the Pelagians. {Vande gave der volhardinghe cap. 22.} <span class="marginal-translation" id="s05-m03">[Marginal translation: On the Gift of Perseverance, chapter 22.]</span> This much can be said: Bertius’s proposition and ours agree well with God’s holy Word, as is clear from the testimony Bertius cites: 1. Corinth. 4.7.

<a id="s05-b002"></a>

> What do you have that you have not received? And if you have received it, why do you boast as though you had not received it?

<a id="s05-b003"></a>

1. Corinth. 15.10.

<a id="s05-b004"></a>

> By the grace of God I am what I am, and his grace in me has not been in vain.

<a id="s05-b005"></a>

Jaco. 1.17.

<a id="s05-b006"></a>

> Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming from the Father of lights, etc.

<a id="s05-b007"></a>

From all these passages it is clear that not only the ability to persevere, but perseverance itself, is a gift of God. For believers, such as Paul, not only can persevere, but have also persevered and still persevere daily. They have received this; they have it through the grace of God; this comes from the Father of lights. Furthermore, not everyone has this gift of actually persevering, but only those to whom God is pleased to give it. Joan. 13.17.

<a id="s05-b008"></a>

> If you know this, you are blessed if you do it. I do not speak of you all: I know whom I have chosen,

<a id="s05-b009"></a>

and cap. 15.16.

<a id="s05-b010"></a>

> I have appointed you to go and bear fruit, and for your fruit to remain.

<a id="s05-b011"></a>

Rom. 9.16.

<a id="s05-b012"></a>

> So it does not depend on anyone’s willing or running, but on God’s mercy.

<a id="s05-b013"></a>

The Apostle means that it does a person no good to have received the gift of willing, or of running either, unless God in his mercy also gives him the gift of remaining steadfast in willing and running. Philipp. 2.13.

<a id="s05-b014"></a>

> It is God who works in you both the willing and the accomplishing, according to his good pleasure.

<a id="s05-b015"></a>

God brings about the accomplishment as he pleases and in whom he pleases. He does all this not according to our worthiness but according to his good pleasure. Christ’s command to us to watch and pray also serves this purpose. Matth. 26.41. Bertius likewise points this out. {pag. 9. li. 13.} This prayer shows us in particular that we are wrong to say that only the ability to persevere comes from God, while perseverance comes from ourselves. Both come from God: the ability to persevere and perseverance itself. For God has granted all his true, regenerate children the ability to persevere, as Bertius demonstrates at great length and with many arguments. {Pag. 5. lin. 6} What, then, do they still pray for daily with such devotion, other than that God should also grant them the act itself, that is, perseverance? Augustine explains this very well in the words of the holy bishop and martyr Cyprian. {August. van de straffe en̄ genade cap. 6.} <span class="marginal-translation" id="s05-m06">[Marginal translation: Augustine, On Rebuke and Grace, chapter 6.]</span> Consider also what Augustine writes in another passage that bears directly on this argument: {Teghens Julian Pelag. lib. 5. c. 3.} <span class="marginal-translation" id="s05-m07">[Marginal translation: Against Julian Pelag., book 5, chapter 3.]</span>

<a id="s05-b016"></a>

> From among the elect, he says, even those who have lived most wickedly are brought to repentance, and this through God’s kindness. Through his patience they were not snatched out of life while they were still immersed in their wickedness.

<a id="s05-b017"></a>

And again in another passage: {Van straff. en̄ ghenade, cap. 17.} <span class="marginal-translation" id="s05-m08">[Marginal translation: On Rebuke and Grace, chapter 17.]</span>

<a id="s05-b018"></a>

> See, he says, how utterly untrue it is to say that someone’s persevering to the end of his life is not a gift of God. For God can cut short our life when he pleases, and if he does so before we come to fall away, he surely thereby causes us to remain steadfast to the end.

<a id="s05-b019"></a>

It also seems that the Wise Man wishes to teach us this same thing when he says: {Sap. 4.10}

<a id="s05-b020"></a>

> For he pleases God well and is dear to him, and is taken away from life among sinners. He is snatched away so that wickedness may not turn his understanding aside, nor false teaching deceive his soul. For evil examples lead astray and corrupt what is good in a person, and the desire that incites one to this perverts innocent hearts. For his soul pleases God; therefore God hastens to take him to himself out of this wicked life.

<a id="s05-b021"></a>

And indeed, daily experience also proves this abundantly. For we often see people die in the very act of sinning. Consequently, having no time to repent, they must perish. Many who live on after committing sin are often brought to repentance and then to salvation. Many also die in their youth before they suffer this or that harm or disgrace, or choose a way of life that might lead them into sin, apostasy, despair, and consequently damnation. Many seem to be spared until advanced old age so that either through sincere repentance they might at last still attain eternal life, or through certain atrocious sins be cast out of that same eternal life in which, so to speak, they already had one foot. Perhaps neither of these things would have happened if both groups had died twenty-five years earlier. If we consider all this carefully, along with whatever else might be said to the same purpose, it is plain that Bertius is right to call his proposition indisputable: it agrees in every way with God’s express Word, reason, and daily experience. But what is to be done next? If perseverance is a gift of God, where does that leave the Remonstrants with their “Perseverance is improperly called a gift”? And again, if not only the ability to persevere but perseverance itself is a gift of God, where does that leave Bertius with his whole book, and also with his predestination and all that follows from it? For we conclude from what has now been related as follows: if no one perseveres because he can persevere, but only because God grants him perseverance, and God does not grant this to everyone, but only to those whom he pleases, then it is not those who, strictly speaking, wish to be saved who are saved, but only those whom God wishes to save.

<a id="s05-b022"></a>

The first is true; therefore so is the second. Accordingly, perseverance and salvation are founded on God’s unchangeable election, and not the other way around: God’s election is not founded on perseverance. For why does God grant you perseverance, and why does he not grant me perseverance also, although he has granted me, just as he has you—and you, just as he has me—the ability to persevere? In short, Bertius is not in agreement here with his dear brethren, the Arminians; nor is he in agreement with himself. In any case, even if he should try to evade the point here, he opposes and contradicts that clear argument which we have now put forward from God’s Word, the testimony of Augustine and Cyprian, and experience itself. I leave aside the various other arguments that further confirm the truth, since many excellent teachers wrote them down and published them long ago.
