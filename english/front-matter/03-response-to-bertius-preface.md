# Reply to Bertius’s Preface to the Reader

> Since, a year ago, men came to me on behalf of the whole body, etc.

Bertius puts on a bold front here, but gives too brief an account of what happened. I must set it out more clearly and fully. {Pag. a 3. b.} This is how matters stood. As regent of the theological college in Leiden, Bertius had been entrusted with the teaching and governance of many fine young men preparing for the ministry. It fell to him, as it were, to shape them. Yet he had never served in the ministry of any church, nor had he ever been duly tested and examined in the doctrine he was to teach. The Synod of Dordrecht in 1608 was concerned for the congregations where his pupils might one day serve. It therefore sent deputies to urge him either to take his degree in theology under Dr Gomarus or at least to confer with them on doctrine. Either course would count as an examination. The Synod judged it dangerous, and a very bad precedent, to appoint a teacher in God’s Church, especially a teacher of its future ministers, before the Church had duly examined, tested and approved his doctrine. Already there were disturbing reports that Bertius held strange opinions.

Accordingly, on 12 February 1609, the deputies came to Bertius and spoke with him about the matter. He said it was inconvenient for him to take a degree in theology, but he was willing to confer with them. So began the discussion Bertius himself relates at greater length. But he leaves out the circumstances I have just described, {Pag.a 3.l.23} and so contrives to accuse these “questioners,” as he calls them, as if they had done something unbecoming to them or beyond what their conscience and office allowed. Indeed, he says outright that he would have been within his rights to turn them away. {Ibid l.24.}

Everyone knows the usual cry when a man’s own words or someone else’s accusation casts suspicion on him and the commissioners of the classis or synod want to examine him: “Tyranny! The Spanish Inquisition! They want to rule our consciences! These men trouble Israel and are enemies of our peace!” And whatever other affectionate names they have for them. Bertius means as much here, though he does not put it so bluntly. For {Ibid.l.4.} what right would he otherwise have had to turn away these worthy men who came on behalf of all, especially with such a commission?

Must not faithful ministers see to it that no one teaches or preaches under them or alongside them in the same well-ordered Reformed congregation unless they know him to be sound in doctrine and blameless in life? And how can they know this before they ask him and he frankly tells them the truth? Some are hostile to such questioners and their questions and think themselves entitled to turn them away. I cannot see how they can be in earnest {Actor 20 28 / 1.Tim.5.22.} about honouring God, caring for the welfare of his congregation, and warding off every kind of sect, confusion and godlessness. {pag.a 3.l.13. ende 20.}

> I have always praised a sincere and straightforward disposition, says Bertius himself, and thought that I ought to conceal nothing of what I had learned through Christ’s grace.

Therefore those who conceal anything of it do not praise a sincere and straightforward disposition. What honour, then, would it have brought Bertius if he had refused these questioners? And what honour can it bring others who refuse similar questioners?

> Since I judged that this opinion conflicted with the Holy Scriptures and God’s nature, etc. {pa a 3. fac.2. li.12.}

The contrary, I hope, will become evident.

> In the congregation, which investigates the truth … {Ibid.l.19.}

Investigating the truth, which is praiseworthy, is one thing; seeking the truth, which is ridiculous and ought not to take place in the Reformed congregation, is another. What need has anyone who has the truth to go looking for it? But those who have the truth and understand it only to some extent do well to investigate it more deeply and thoroughly, so that they may understand it better. Bertius meant to do this when he undertook to fathom his new opinion with a new diligence. {Pa.a 4.l.25}

But previously, when he still shared the view of our churches, he thought he could not investigate the truth without first calling our doctrine into question, asking whether it was true or not. Of the same opinion are all those who have lately taken it into their heads to present themselves as special lovers and investigators of the truth—as though all of us were investigators of lies.

But this is not how things ought to proceed in God’s congregation. If one may call one article of our teaching into question and ask whether a point of the Reformed religion is false, why should one not be allowed to ask whether all of them are false, and thus call the entire doctrine into question? And if that happens, what certainty is there in Christendom? When will one ever be allowed to stop investigating the truth in this way? Or is everything Bertius calls truth nothing but truth? And must one not then investigate that further too—that is, call it into question?

What, then, will be the outcome of all this, not investigating but seeking the truth? Eternal doubt and instability; a continual and unceasing questioning and searching for what cannot be found; a complete eradication of all religion; and an incurable desperation, or despair, in everyone’s heart.

Yet when Bertius’s followers, who wish the truth to be investigated in this way and would have people doubt whether this or that article of our teaching is true, instruct their hearers from the pulpit in other articles of our teaching, they speak of these as certain and infallible truth. It seems they cannot bear anyone to doubt these in the least, although they are of the same nature as the very articles they would have people doubt. Is that not openly making a mockery of one’s own words, or one’s own conscience, or God and his Word, and all honourable people?

If you hold our teaching to be true, do not seek truth, but investigate it so that you understand it more and more. If you doubt whether our teaching is true—indeed, if you say it is untrue—why do you not leave that teaching, and at the same time the church and pulpits in which it is taught? Our Christian civil authorities tolerate and allow so many different sects and sectarians in this country: why should they not tolerate you too, provided only that you leave us, since you do not wish, and are not able, to belong to us and be with us?

> The sayings of Scripture stood constantly before me, etc. {Pa.3. §a.2. l.26.}

A weak staff and a frail foundation on which Bertius has built his new view. Should anyone abandon a truth he once acknowledged so lightly, on so little reflection, before he properly understands the matter?

> I began, he says, to investigate more deeply and thoroughly what I had previously understood to some extent. {Pa.a.4.l.25}

Why did you not investigate first, Bertius, before declaring yourself so emphatically? Is that not reaching your decision first and only then looking for reasons to support it? I fear that you paid far too much attention to the times and to people. On 7 March 1608, in your letter to Jan Barentsz., these things were still abominations; and now, on 12 February 1609, they are truth. At least, you call them truth, although by your own account you understand it only in part. Should a sober-minded man, as you ought to be, act so? Nevertheless, it happened. Let us hear, then, what you build on. {pag.a 3.fa.a lin.27.} 1 John 2.2: Christ is the propitiation for the sins of the whole world. What does “the world” mean here? All the people in the world, one by one? Why not rather believers, as in 2 Corinthians 5.18–19: God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not imputing to them—that is, to the world—their sins? For it is only to believers that sins are not imputed. {Rom.3.25.} Along with others, Prosper of Aquitaine understands the aforesaid words of John in this way too, where he writes: {de voc. Gent. lib. 2.c.1.}

> God’s saving grace has appeared to ALL people, {Tit. 2.11.} and yet the ministers of this grace are hated by ALL people. {Matt. 10.22} And although those who hated were different from those who were oppressed by the persecutors’ hatred, nevertheless the name of ALL people is withheld from neither. Thus the portion consisting of the disobedient indeed suffers the loss of its salvation, but the dignity of BELIEVERS is nevertheless reckoned as a fullness. For the Apostle John says: Christ is the propitiation for all our sins, and not only for ours but for the sins of the whole world.

Thus the meaning would be: Christ is the propitiation for all our sins, and not only for ours but for those of the whole world—that is, for the sins of all believers. Or why not rather understand “the world” to mean the believing Gentiles? For it is quite probable that John wrote specifically to believing Jews, {Siet 1 Joan 2.7.} and wished to teach that Christ had become the propitiation not only for them but also for believing Gentiles. The rejection of the Jews, says Paul, is the reconciliation of the world—that is, of the Gentiles. {Rom.11.5.} And the aforementioned Prosper also says very aptly: {lib. 1.de voc. Gent.c.3}

> God’s people thus has its fullness; and although a large portion of humanity rejects or neglects the Saviour’s grace, nevertheless in the elect and foreknown, who have been set apart from the universality of all people, a particular universality is also established, so that it seems that from (or, out of) the whole WORLD the whole WORLD has been redeemed, and from (or, out of) ALL people ALL people have been received.

This is further shown there by many examples.

Why, then, I say, not interpret John’s word “world” in one of these two ways? Especially since it conflicts with God’s justice that he should fully punish in the damned their sins, which he has already fully punished in Christ Jesus, if Christ became a propitiation or propitiatory sacrifice for all the sins of all people. For in that case he surely bore everyone’s sins and suffered and paid for them.

Since God also knew from eternity that he would not choose to make his Son Christ known to many peoples, what purpose could he have had in nevertheless willing his Son’s precious blood to be shed for such people? If we may not preach Christ before dogs and swine, if we may not pray for those who sin unto death, {Matt.7.6 I.Joan.5.16 Joan.17.9} if Christ himself will not pray for the world, did he nevertheless love all these so much that he died for them and reconciled them with his Father? I do not believe it.

And why did Bertius not rather also turn his eyes to what is written in Matthew 26.28, John 10.11 and 11.51–52, Ephesians 5.25, and other such passages? It is certainly more than certain that ALL, and likewise the WORLD, often mean in Scripture a portion or kind of all, and of the world. Consequently, the argument built upon those little words alone is altogether insecure. Conversely, the argument drawn from the divine statements just cited must be altogether secure, since it is expressed and presented in proper and by no means ambiguous words.

But let this suffice here concerning Bertius’s first foundation. The second is Mark 16.16: Whoever believes will be saved. But how could Bertius use these words to answer the question put to him? {p.a 31.28.} That question was: To which people does God impart his saving grace?

For saving grace means not only eternal life—which we readily acknowledge with Bertius is imparted only to believers (I am speaking of adults)—but also faith and every other grace necessary for our salvation. Shall one then say that faith and all saving grace are imparted only to believers because Christ says, Whoever believes will be saved? Or should one rather say that salvation is imparted to believers, and faith to the elect?

But it made no difference to our Bertius what he said or why he said it, provided he might thereby be admitted to this new, highly privileged order. Those who examine Holy Scripture to investigate the truth, with simplicity and godly sincerity, as in God’s presence—as Bertius wishes we would—are certainly not accustomed to act like this. {pag.116.127} But let us proceed. The second question put to Bertius was this:

> In what way does God impart his saving grace? {pa. a 3. l.30}

To this he answered, it seems, that it takes place through a resistible power. {p.a 3 f.2.l.8.} The foundations of this answer were the following divine statements: Hebrews 3.8 and 12, and 4.2. {ibid lin.32.} Since these passages show that some can resist God’s calling, Bertius concluded that all people could indeed resist God’s calling. He did not so much as distinguish between the different ways in which God applies his grace, although that distinction demands careful attention here. In the book that follows I discuss this more fully as the occasion arises, along with Bertius’s answer to the third question and his grounds for it. I shall let the matter rest here.

> Not long afterwards I became aware, etc.

Here Bertius again accuses certain people whom he does not name. {p.a 4.l.11} I can well guess what wrong these people supposedly did. They will have said that Bertius was not sound in the teaching of the Reformed churches and did not agree with the other teachers of those churches; and that therefore he ought not to be allowed to remain the instructor of so many prospective preachers, lest he corrupt them too, to the great harm of all the congregations to which they might yet be called. Was that wrong of people who loved the truth and God’s Church? And was such concern not fitting for such people? Would Bertius himself not be entitled to see that no one in his college corrupted another? And if he could not otherwise prevent such harm, would he not be allowed to drive the deceiver out of his college? Well, then! Why should people not have been allowed to have such concerns about Bertius too, and in such a case deal with him as he deals with his pupils? Surely Bertius has not outgrown all oversight. Or may people indeed drive away and banish the old, faithful preachers who in Amsterdam abide by the doctrine they have preached for 20 or 30 years, and to preach which they were appointed, and treat them as villains, while they may not so much as give a stern look to Bertius, who has lightly fallen away from the accepted doctrine which he himself so shortly before acknowledged as good? This fellow [description unclear] thinks far too highly of himself.

> {pag a.4 f.2. l.23.} That this same principal article ought to be treated first, etc.

This strikes me as strange. For Bertius himself says {pa.b l.10.} that our doctrine of perseverance rests upon the doctrine of predestination as upon a foundation. Can one bring down a house better than by destroying its foundation? “Now I do not see,” he says, {pa.b 2.l.25} “how, if this principal article (unconditional election) lies overthrown, that absolute necessity of perseverance can be maintained; therefore those who retreat from this step can easily be pushed off the other.” And again: {p.b 1.13.} “I intend not only to attack but to overthrow the grounding of perseverance upon unconditional election.” Why, then, did he not rather first work to overthrow unconditional election, when he says he was ready to do so? {pag.46.li.7. Pa.11.l 9. p.40.l.6.} It seems he hoped to achieve it with demands and bluster, imagining that we were a pack of fools who would seek only to preserve the roof and walls while letting him dig away the foundation as he pleased. Dear God, how this failing plagues us!

> {p.a 4.f.2. l.28.} An offence punishable in the body …

How lightly they take it when they turn everything upside down, tear unity apart, grieve the godly, and put religion on display to be mocked by the whole world. And all this is to pass under the name of Netherlandish liberty! For Bertius writes in his dedicatory letter that among the other reasons which moved him to publish this book of his (he says, to dedicate this work to them; but this last reason cannot refer to that, as all sensible people can judge) there was also this: {pag. 2. l.a l.16.} {p.a 2.l.ull.}

> A certain desire and wish to preserve and hand down to posterity both the truth and also that LIBERTY which our forefathers acquired with so much sweat, blood, sleeplessness, toil and expense, as you well know.

As if we had waged war for so long so that everyone might openly not only hold whatever opinion about religion seemed best to him, but also speak, preach, write or put into rhyme whatever came into his confused, foolish and dull head! Indeed, so that even the teachers of one and the same publicly accepted doctrine might shout, cry out, teach and rail against one another, one saying yes and another no, one saying this way and another that, and thus throw religion to the winds.

Ungrateful children, who so shamefully misinterpret your forefathers’ good intention! Who among you all has ever before heard this godless and accursed licence called Netherlandish liberty? When they would not permit Caspar Coolhaes, Cornelis Wichertsz. Herberts and others what you think ought not now to be denied to you, where was this liberty then?

See how the times change. One must no longer ask what people have believed and taught until now, what people have hitherto held concerning liberty, or how they have been accustomed to call everything by its name. Instead one must ask Bertius and his companions what henceforth will be the best and most appropriate judgement on everything. For they have the Pope’s authority. Let them only take good care not to bring us back under the Roman Pope in the end.

> Those who are alarmed at the novelty of the title … {p.b l.4.}

Certainly, many had heard of the perseverance of the saints who had never heard apostasy, or the falling away of the saints, mentioned. Might they not now be startled at these unheard-of words? What lengths he goes to to find fault! But, Bertius, many also take alarm at this strange title, *Hymenaeus desertor*—that is, Hymenaeus the deserter or apostate—since you will not be able to prove from God’s Word (at least, that is my judgement) that Hymenaeus was truly born again, and that he never rose from his fall. What will you say now? Can you not bear our being human? {bl.60.ll.7.}

> As though this saying of Ezekiel were new …

This saying is not found in Ezekiel; Bertius merely calls his own gloss a saying of Ezekiel, as a courtesy. More will be said about this below.

> {pag.b 1. f.2. l.26.} To introduce a new heresy …

We do not say that either. Rather, it is the old heresy of Pelagius that Bertius brings out again, which was rejected and condemned by the ancient orthodox Church already 1200 years ago. This will become clearer in what follows.

> {p.b 2.l.17.} Indeed, our brothers themselves, being convicted in their conscience, etc.

Previously, people tried to persuade others that the entire disagreement concerned predestination alone. Thus there may have been some who held that the entire disagreement was not about predestination alone: were it only about predestination, they would not wish to make so much fuss about it. Our Bertius takes this as admitted, and now concludes from it: Therefore those who spoke in this way are convicted in their consciences that their doctrine of predestination is either false, or uncertain, or unnecessary. Is that true? Or did Bertius take pleasure in slandering those whom he nevertheless calls his brothers? That is certainly a fine brotherly deed! If that is how he treats his good friends and brothers, how might he treat his enemies?

> {p.b 2.l.34.} So that God, Scripture, reason, etc.

Boasting requires little skill and brings little honour; yet Bertius seems very pleased with himself for doing it throughout. But, as the saying goes, “Hear the other side.” Listen and see whether our account agrees with his. For what he says is only half the story.

> {p.b 2.f.2.l.6} This, at any rate, is more than certain, etc.

In appealing here so frequently and so boldly to antiquity, Bertius follows the old, common practice of heretics. For they are accustomed always to appeal to antiquity in order to deceive the simple and give their claims some plausibility and standing. The Africans appealed to Optatus; the Nestorians to the Council of Nicaea; the Donatists to Cyprian; Dioscorus to Cyril and Gregory Nazianzen. Indeed, at the Council of Chalcedon he expressly said: {Actio. 2,}

> I defend the teachings of the holy Fathers. I have their testimonies, not plucked from the air somewhere, but written in their own books. I am cast out together with the Fathers.

Likewise, did not Pelagius and his following also defend themselves with antiquity, appealing to Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine himself? {Prosp epist. ad Augustin,} Indeed, when certain sayings from Paul’s letter to the Romans were brought against them, did they not dare boldly to say that no teacher of the Church had ever understood them in that way? {Prosper ibid.} Let no one, then, be unsettled by Bertius’s words, but pay close attention to his reasons and proof.

> None of the Latin Fathers has ever, etc. {P. b 2. f. 2. l. 20.}

This is sheer untruth. For Augustine himself writes about this matter as follows: {In boeck bd hier goet te volghar.} {c. 20.}

> I am astonished, he says, that the Pelagians do not notice that, to say nothing of others, in many of my books which I wrote and published before the Pelagians began to be known—indeed, before I knew that any Pelagian heresy would ever arise—I wrote and spoke about God’s grace and mercy, and likewise about perseverance, as I do now.

And afterwards: {ibid. c. 23.}

> On this matter (predestination and perseverance) the Church has never been silent in its prayers since its beginning, although it has not always declared itself on the matter in such explicit words, because it was not provoked to do so by any opponent.

{c. 22. uit eyn be.} {Nota.} Which of the two will one now believe, Augustine or Bertius?

> Which nevertheless the Africans would not, etc. {P. b 3. l. 28}

No heresy ever began and reached completion all at once. If Pelagius himself did not attack the doctrine of perseverance and predestination, or did not do so openly, or at least not before the Councils of Carthage and Milevis, his pupils were nevertheless guilty of doing so. This is so clearly evident from the letters of Prosper and Hilary to Augustine, and from Augustine’s answer to those letters, that anyone who wishes to talk people out of it must resort to palpable lies.

I call Bertius himself as a witness.

> {p b 3 f.2.l.17} Just as we dare not despise them, etc.

Why, then, did Bertius dare despise them? Has he more power and courage than the Pope? But let us hear the Pope’s words in full.

> As for the deeper and more difficult aspects of the questions that arise, he says, those who opposed the heretics have treated them more fully. Just as we dare not despise them, neither need we uphold them.

(Bertius’s translator says “ought not”, which the Pope does not say.) Why?

> Because, to confess God’s grace—from whose operation and worthiness nothing at all may be taken away—we consider sufficient what the writings of the Apostolic See have taught in accordance with the foregoing rules.

Is this now to say that Augustine’s view of predestination and perseverance did not please Pope Celestine? {q. b 3d.29.} Certainly, if Pelagius did not err in these principal articles, as Bertius would like people to believe, then the Pope is not speaking of these articles here either. And if he is speaking of them, he expressly says that he dares not despise Augustine’s view of them—which he would indeed have had condemned if he had regarded it as unchristian.

Perhaps Celestine neither wished nor dared to approve Augustine’s view either, Bertius will say, and therefore left it undecided, so that people might accept or reject it as they wished. But that cannot be inferred from the words of Celestine quoted above. For at the Councils of Carthage and Milevis the question was not properly about predestination and perseverance, but about the grace of Jesus Christ, which Pelagius attacked. This question could quite well be settled without needing to bring predestination or perseverance into it. And that is all Celestine meant to say here. Meanwhile, however, the aforesaid councils and Celestine in his rules, as he calls them, in maintaining Christ’s grace against Pelagius, declared so plainly that not only the will and the ability to do good, but the good deed itself, are God’s gracious work in us, that I cannot understand how Bertius will defend his manner of persevering if he wishes to hold those councils and Celestine’s rules in esteem. Let him look at chapters 6, 7, 8, 9 and 12 of Celestine’s letter, volume 1 of the Councils, page 593 etc., and also the Second Council of Orange, canons 3, 6, 9, 15, 20 and 23.

> Indeed, not even in that of Trent. {pag 63 f.2. l.20.}

What does this mean? Are we, who have been cleansed of the leaven of the papacy, still to let the Council of Trent set our standard in matters of faith or religion? That is how we would have to begin, then, if the matter were still to end well. Listen, nonetheless, to what that council teaches:

> Whoever says, they say, that a person who has been born again and justified must believe with certainty that he is infallibly among the number of the elect, let him be accursed. {Sess.6.can.15}

And:

> Whoever says with absolute and infallible certainty that he will assuredly have that great gift of perseverance to the end, unless he has learned this through a special revelation, let him be accursed. {ibid. can.16.}

And:

> Whoever says that the grace of justification comes to no one except the elect alone, and that the rest who are called are indeed called, but do not obtain (this) grace because they have been ordained to evil, let him be accursed. {ibid. can.17.}

Can you not readily guess by now which way Bertius leans?

> That even today among the adherents of the Pope, etc. {pag.63.f.2. l.22.}

What Bertius means to say is this: “Whatever is done and tolerated under the papacy without breaking the unity of those churches may also be done in the Reformed churches. And I hope to be tolerated there too without breaking their unity.” Is that not a fine piece of work? Remember it well.

> This at least is certain, that Melanchthon and Luther, etc. {ibid.lin.26.}

Why should doctors and other learned men not be able and free to bear with one another? They may hold a distinct opinion on such profound articles and put it soberly, without conceit, to their peers—that is, to other learned men. This is especially so when they are still at work establishing religion and their aim is not only to investigate the truth but also to seek it, not to gather disciples for themselves and so ride roughshod over one another. But does that give people licence to conduct the dispute as they do today? Certainly Melanchthon never judged Luther’s view as Bertius here judges ours. {pa. 6 l.11. pa. 6 2.l.6. ende lui.14. pa.2.l.3. pag.83.li 20}

For he calls our doctrine of predestination a fabricated decree, an empty opinion, of which not one jot is to be found in Scripture, a crude and fabricated doctrine. And he calls our view of perseverance a false doctrine harmful to the whole Christian world, from which flow things that are false and harmful both to God’s honour and to good, honourable morals.

Thus it is impossible for Bertius to tolerate us in friendship and brotherhood with such doctrine of ours, if he has the slightest love for the edification of God’s Church. Why, then, does he recount the example of Luther and Melanchthon, which he neither wishes nor is able to follow? Or if Bertius is such a misguided lover of peace that he would tolerate us forever for peace’s sake, why did he not leave us in the good peace we had among ourselves before he and his followers threw it all into turmoil? {pag.6 3. f a. l.31.}

But perhaps Bertius hopes that, just as nearly all the churches of Germany abandoned Luther’s view and sided with Melanchthon, as he says, so all of us together will eventually side with him; and that in such a hope he might tolerate us for a time. But this is an altogether empty hope. Thank God, we do not stand so insecurely and unsteadily that Bertius’s reed staffs could knock us down. {Van’t goet der volh:rd. ca.20;}

Augustine tells us that when the Pelagians forced the issue, he defended predestination more diligently and at greater pains. I have no doubt it will be the same with us. We shall not seek the truth, as Bertius has done, but investigate that same truth with greater zeal and diligence so as to understand it more deeply than we have until now. That is one notable benefit I can see in the disputes that have arisen.

As for the churches of Germany which, as Bertius says, mostly abandoned Luther’s view and sided with Melanchthon, one may read what Leonard Hutter writes about this in his exposition of the Book of Concord, article 2, and also what the churches of the Palatinate relate in their Reminder concerning the Book of Concord, page 602.

> Indeed, that John Calvin himself, on more than one, etc.

Bertius {pag 23 sqq. lin. 34.} does Calvin an injustice. For in the passage cited, Calvin teaches the exact opposite of Bertius’s entire book, in the words immediately following what Bertius recounts here. Look up and read Calvin himself on John 15.9 and verse 6, at the end. From this you will easily be able to judge what a fine man Bertius is: he picks out a little word here and there that seems to serve his advantage, and deliberately leaves all the rest unreported. But this is the nature of error, says Augustine, {de Genes. con. Manich.} that it thinks everyone must dislike what it dislikes. Bertius does not wish to be a Calvinist himself, as people call them; therefore he wants to turn Calvin into a Bertian. I pass over his other needless boasts, the mockery and slander he adds in his preface, and the directions he gives anyone who wishes to answer his book. I hasten to the main subject.
