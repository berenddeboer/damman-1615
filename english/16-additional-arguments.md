<!-- B001 -->
“Let these arguments, which are all scriptural, now be added as the seventh proof,” says Bertius. {pag.41.l.20.}

<!-- B002 -->
> First: anyone whose sins have all been forgiven is truly justified. {Ioh. viii.34.}
> Yet such a person can fall away again for good and be condemned.

<!-- B003 -->
> Thus someone who was once truly justified can later fall away for good and be condemned.

<!-- B004 -->
Answer. I deny the second part of this argument. What Bertius adduces from Mat. 18.27. to prove it is wholly irrelevant. For, as Jerome very well says:

<!-- B005 -->
> Parables and the uncertain interpretation of riddles can never serve to establish the authority of doctrines. {over Matt 13.vers.33.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M03">Marginal translation: On Matt 13.vers.33.</span> That is: one neither can nor may establish any article of faith from parables and similitudes.

<!-- B006 -->
Yet, since all parables have their meanings, and Christ does not use such parables without reason, the same Jerome teaches us how to understand and interpret them:

<!-- B007 -->
> “It is not the beginnings but the end that should be sought,” he says. {over Matt. 22 vers.14.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M04">Marginal translation: On Matt. 22 vers.14.</span> That is: one should look not to the beginning but to the end.

<!-- B008 -->
He means that one must look to Christ’s purpose and intention in such parables. Christ commonly expresses this intention in a saying at the end of the parables, as may be seen there, namely in Matth.22.14., and in other places, as also in these cited words of Matth.18. For in vers.35. Christ says:

<!-- B009 -->
> My heavenly Father will also do this to you, unless each of you forgives his brother’s offence from the heart.

<!-- B010 -->
Christ’s purpose in this parable, then, is to teach that we must forgive if we are to be forgiven. The occasion that prompted the Lord to tell the parable, in verses 21 and 22, makes his purpose still clearer.

Accordingly, this parable must be applied and used only for that purpose; one may not apply parts of this or any other parable to some other purpose. Otherwise, if one wished to proceed as Bertius does here, one could also infer from verse 25 that God might punish the wife for her husband’s sins and the children for their fathers’ sins; and from verses 32 and 33, that someone truly justified could be cast into hell by God for one single sin. {Pag.16.l.2.} Yet Bertius does not concede this.

And from verse 34 one could infer that God can justly punish and exact payment for sins that he has once truly forgiven. For that wicked servant is not handed over to the tormentors merely to be punished for the mercilessness he showed his fellow servant: his master handed him over until he had paid the entire debt. {vers.34.} The entire debt? How was there still a debt when it had been entirely remitted? {vers.27. en 32.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M07">Marginal translation: Verses 27. and 32.</span> We could draw still more absurdities of this kind from the parable. And Bertius himself undoubtedly knows all this very well too. Is he then at liberty to use the parable this way?

<!-- B011 -->
> Second: anyone from whom the Devil has been cast out is truly justified. {Pag.42.l.8.}
>
> But such a person can, through his carelessness and inattention, fall into a worse state than before.
>
> Thus someone who was once truly justified can fall again into a worse state than before.

<!-- B012 -->
Answer. The first part of this syllogism is false. For, first, the Devil possesses some people bodily, and he is cast out bodily by Christ’s power. {Mat. 4.24. en cap.7.22. en cap.9.32.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M09">Marginal translation: Mat. 4.24. and cap.7.22. and cap.9.32.</span> There are many examples of this. But why call everyone who has received such a bodily benefit truly justified? Secondly, the Devil also possesses some people spiritually, {Act.19.13. 2. Tim 2 26.} and he is cast out spiritually through God’s word and the power of the Holy Spirit. But are all those from whom the Devil has thus been cast out truly justified? If so, all the scribes, Pharisees and ordinary Jews living in Christ’s time must have been truly justified. For, in applying this parable of his, which Bertius adduces here {pag.42. li.7.} to confirm his boast, Christ says: “So it will also be with this wicked generation.” {Mat.12.45} If Bertius has not seen this before, let him now put his spectacles on properly for once.

<!-- B013 -->
> Third: what can increase can also be perfected; and what can diminish can also perish and be annihilated. {Pag.42.l.14}
>
> Now faith can increase and diminish. {Ibid.lin.19.}
>
> Thus it can also come to nothing, unless one gets back on the right path in time.

<!-- B014 -->
Answer. I grant the whole of this. Only let Bertius prove that the truly regenerate fail to return to the right path in time. [It is unclear whether Bertius must prove that they fail to return or that they can fail.]

<!-- B015 -->
> Fourth: if righteousness is not abandoned, nor faith, nor holiness, there will be no deserters. {Ibid. lin.28}
>
> But there certainly are deserters.
>
> Thus righteousness is abandoned.

<!-- B016 -->
Answer. The first part of this argument is false. This is sufficiently apparent from what Bertius himself has taught elsewhere in his book. {Pa.48.li.36. en volghens} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M16">Marginal translation: Pa.48.li.36. and following.</span> We too have repeatedly pointed this out before, and shall demonstrate it further in our answer to the second absurdity that Bertius draws from our teaching.

<!-- B017 -->
> Fifth: if righteousness is not abandoned, no one can be erased from the book of life. {Pag.43.l.2.}
>
> But it certainly can happen that someone is erased from it.
>
> Thus righteousness can be abandoned.

<!-- B018 -->
Answer. There are two kinds of life: one temporal, the other eternal. God has a book for each, so to speak. That is, God knows all people now living and those still to be born. {Psa.139.16.} Likewise, God knows everyone who will be saved. {Luce 10.20.} All people, I say, each according to their condition, are as though written in a book before the Lord; or the Lord himself has written all people as though in a book. {Exod. 32.32} To be written in the book of temporal life is to live or to be going to live. [The opening phrase of this definition is unclear; the Dutch reads “to have counted.”] To be written in the book of eternal life is to be saved now or in the future. Those who have already died or are still to be born cannot at present be erased from the book of temporal life. And those who now enjoy salvation in heaven cannot now be erased from the book of eternal life either. Yet all these are still written in those books.

Therefore it is certain, in the first place, that not all those written in the book of life can be erased. The question concerns only those still living: can they be erased? As regards the book of temporal life, we acknowledge that all the ungodly can be erased from it, but not all of them will be. For to be erased from this book does not mean simply to die, but to be punished by God with death, as an example to others and a demonstration of God’s righteous wrath. God speaks of this in Deutron. 29.20.: “The Lord will remove his name from beneath heaven”—beneath heaven, not in heaven. {Luce 10.20.} And Moses, Exod.32.32.:

<!-- B019 -->
> Erase me from your book. But the Lord said: I will erase from my book the one who has sinned against me.

<!-- B020 -->
That is, the entire Israelite people, as is apparent from the preceding and following words, and especially from verses 9, 10 and 11 and those that follow. But was the entire Israelite people written in the book of eternal life? Or can someone be erased who has never truly been entered? Or does Moses speak of the book of eternal life, while God answers concerning the book of temporal life? Or may one here understand by God’s book—for the text does not say “the book of life”—the book of the covenant of grace, in which all Israel stood? Let Bertius say which he prefers; only let him prove his assertion with sound reasons. I still stand by what I have said: namely, that the book of temporal life is being discussed here, if indeed the book of life is being discussed at all. As for what David says in Psalm.69.29.:

<!-- B021 -->
> Erase them {Psal.69.29.} from the book of the living, so that they are not recorded with the righteous.

<!-- B022 -->
Here we must also understand “the living” to mean those who live on earth, so that the prophet’s meaning is this:

<!-- B023 -->
> O Lord, snatch these ungodly people from the earth in your anger, with such a death that they are not, after their death, reckoned and recorded among the righteous, as they might be if they came to die in peace.

<!-- B024 -->
One may nevertheless also understand “the living” here as those who, having been called to eternal life, will yet be brought to eternal life. Yet many ungodly people hide among them and are counted as though they too belonged to eternal life. It is as though he said:

<!-- B025 -->
> O Lord, cast such deceitful people out of your congregation, or let them be cast out, {Ezech.13.9. Galat. 5.12.} and do not let them be reckoned with the righteous.

<!-- B026 -->
For it is also worth noting that the prophet says: “Erase them, so that they are not recorded.” If they were recorded, how does he pray that they should not be recorded? And if they were not recorded, how does he pray that the Lord should erase them? Moreover, does God write any people in the book of life in time, or has he written that book from eternity? And does the prophet say, “Do not write them with the righteous”, or rather, “So that they are not recorded”? In this way he indicates {der Septuagint Pa. 53:11. 25 en voortaen} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M24">Marginal translation: Of the Septuagint. Pa. 53:11. 25 and following.</span> that these evil people were judged by human beings to belong to eternal life, but had not been written down for it by God, elected, or, as Bertius calls it, foreknown. Meanwhile he desires that God, who alone knows hearts, should make such fellows known, so that people should no longer be deceived by them. Augustine, writing on these words of the psalm, {August. over Psal. 69.29.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M25">Marginal translation: Augustine on Psal. 69.29.</span> speaks very aptly:

<!-- B027 -->
> Brothers, we must not take this as though God wrote someone in the book of life and then erased him again. If a man, Pilate, has said, “What I have written, I have written”, {Joan. 19.22.} would God write something and erase it again? How, then, are they erased when they were never entered in it? It is said with reference to their own hope: they thought their names were written there. What does “Let them be erased” mean? It means: make them know that they are not written there.

<!-- B028 -->
Thus Bertius again has the choice here of which interpretation he wishes to accept. He ought not to reject them all unless he can give reasons why none of them is acceptable, even though he has great latitude. In such a case, we shall also always gladly give reason its due.

Finally, as regards what is written in Apocal. 3.5., {Apocal. 3.5.} there is no doubt that the book of eternal life is spoken of there. But what does Bertius hope to prove from those words? How so? Because it says, “Whoever overcomes, I will not erase his name from the book of life”, does he mean to infer: “Therefore, I will erase from the book of life the name of whoever does not overcome”? {Pa. 62. l. 29.} Let that be so. But what more will follow? “No one can be erased,” he will say, “who has not previously been entered. Therefore, whoever does not overcome has previously been entered.” Entered? In the book of eternal life? By God? Before all eternity? And yet he is erased? How does that happen?

Did God not will from eternity, for example, that Paul should overcome and Demas be overcome? {Actor. 15.18. 2. Tim. 4.7.10.} Or that Paul should enter eternal life and Demas should not? Why, then, does he enter Demas in the book of eternal life? Or does God sit throughout the year writing and blotting in his book: now entering someone who submits himself to his obedience, and then scratching that same person out when he abandons his obedience? And if he nevertheless returns to God in heartfelt repentance, does God enter him once more, and then, if he departs from God a second time, erase him again?

It is sheer blasphemy to ascribe such ignorance and changeableness to God. {2.Tim 2.19} God knows, and has known from eternity, who are his. {Mat.25.34 en ca.20.23.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M31">Marginal translation: Mat.25.34 and ca.20.23.</span> He knows, and has known from eternity, who would enter salvation, whether he has ordained them to salvation by an absolute decree or has only foreknown them, as Bertius puts it. {Apoc.20.15. en ca.21.27.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M32">Marginal translation: Apoc.20.15. and ca.21.27.</span> These alone, if one wishes to speak truthfully, may be said to have been written by God in the book of eternal life; and none of them can ever be erased by God from that same book.

But God has revealed in his word, not which individual persons, but the kind of persons to whom he wishes to give salvation and will give it. {2.Cor.10.18} That is, he has revealed what kind he has written in the book of life. And it often happens that many, having received certain gifts of the Spirit, readily attribute salvation to themselves, or outwardly conduct themselves in such a way that others can suppose nothing of them but that they will be saved. In this respect, then, it can and may indeed be said that some who stand entered in the book of life will be erased from it. {Siet ooc Bertium pa.31. lin.30. ende volghens, Item pa.48 lin.26. ende voortaen. Item pa.53. lin.25 ende voortaen. Dan.5:6 etc Mat.13.12. 1.Cor.4.5. Matth.3.18. 1.Joh.2.19. 2.Tim.5.24} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M34">Marginal translation: See also Bertius, pa.31. lin.30. and following; likewise pa.48 lin.26. and onwards; likewise pa.53. lin.25 and onwards. Dan.5:6 etc Mat.13.12. 1.Cor.4.5. Matth.3.18. 1.Joh.2.19. 2.Tim.5.24.</span>

Written, I say, not by God, but by themselves or by others, judging by their present condition. [The Dutch also mentions eternity here; it is unclear how the phrase qualifies this contrast.] Thus they are erased not only on the last day, when the Lord will cast it all out and reveal the counsel of hearts, but they are also often erased in this life by those very people who had previously entered them: namely, when their hypocrisy and ungodliness come to light and they do not show by their deeds which spirit they are children of. [The wording is unclear: it may instead say that their deeds reveal whose children they are.] If this does not suit Bertius, let him tell us the reason why.

<!-- B029 -->
> Sixth: The righteous person can act according to the principles within him. {Pa.43.li.20}
>
> Now he has two principles: one of good, namely the Spirit; the other of evil, namely the flesh.
>
> Thus the righteous person can act according to the Spirit, and also according to the flesh.

<!-- B030 -->
Answer. I grant this. For who among us has ever taught that the righteous could not sin? “We all stumble in many things,” says St James. {1.Reg.8.46. Jacob.3.2.} But Bertius seeks to infer further from this that it can happen that they entirely extinguish the Spirit. {Pag.44 l.1.} That I deny, and it by no means follows from the preceding syllogism, unless he changed it in this way:

<!-- B031 -->
> All who have Spirit and flesh within them can, through the flesh, entirely extinguish the Spirit.
>
> All the righteous have the Spirit and the flesh within them.
>
> Thus the righteous can entirely extinguish the Spirit.

<!-- B032 -->
But who does not see here that the first part is false—or at least, that it is accepted as known and true, although our entire dispute concerns precisely this? And as regards the second part, although it is indeed the case that the righteous have flesh and Spirit within them, one must nevertheless also take due note that the righteous:

<!-- B033 -->
> are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit; {Roman.8.9} that they have crucified the flesh with its impulses and desires, {Galat 5 24.} and are led by the Spirit; {Rom.8.14.} that they have died to sin, {Rom.6.2.} and live the life they live in the flesh in the faith of the Son of God. {Galat.2 20.}

<!-- B034 -->
Thus the Spirit is far stronger than the flesh within them. And since, beholding the Lord’s glory with unveiled face, {2.Cor.3.18} they are also changed into that same image from glory to glory, daily growing in every respect in love in Christ, {Ephes.4.15.} we hold it certain—as indeed it is certain—that within them the flesh never can, nor ever will, gain the upper hand to such an extent {Ier.31.21.22} that it entirely extinguishes the Spirit. {1. Joan.4.4.}

<!-- B035 -->
> Seventh and last: if righteousness could not be abandoned, the righteous would necessarily have to persevere always, and consequently no one would deliberate about maintaining righteousness. All instruction, admonition, rebuke, encouragement to virtue and restraint from sins would also be in vain. {pag.44.l.14.}
>
> But these things are not taught and practised in vain.
>
> Thus righteousness can be abandoned.

<!-- B036 -->
Answer. This one syllogism contains three distinct syllogisms. For the sake of ordinary readers, we shall set them out and answer them separately. The first syllogism, then, is this:

<!-- B037 -->
> If righteousness could not be abandoned, the righteous would have to persevere NECESSARILY.
>
> But the righteous do not have to persevere NECESSARILY.
>
> Therefore righteousness can be abandoned.

<!-- B038 -->
Answer. What is said here in the second part is untrue. Indeed, we are wholly convinced that the righteous person perseveres NECESSARILY; but not in the sense that he is compelled to persevere. For regeneration does not impose natural compulsion on a person’s prior choice, as Bertius rightly says. {pag.44 li.2.} Rather, a righteous or regenerate person, being what he is—having, namely, received from God the will and ability to persevere—cannot do otherwise than persevere. A person does not always do what he is quite able to do, because he does not always wish to do it. A person also does not always do what he does wish to do, because he is not always able to do it. But what he is always both able and willing to do, he always does, and cannot omit to do it, given that he is both able and willing. Consequently, he does it NECESSARILY, yet without compulsion, for he also wishes to do it. But let us hear how Bertius proves his assertion. Here is his second fallacious argument.

<!-- B039 -->
> What someone deliberates about, he does not have to do NECESSARILY.
> The righteous person deliberates about perseverance.
> Therefore the righteous person does not have to persevere NECESSARILY.

<!-- B040 -->
Answer. The second premise is false. For all deliberation concerns only finding and obtaining the means that might serve to bring us to an end we have set before ourselves; but no one deliberates about the end itself, or about using the means already found. How, then, would the righteous deliberate about their perseverance? For they already know: 1. That they must persevere if they wish to be saved. 2. That they can persevere. 3. That they must watch, pray, and do other such things—those Bertius recounts—if they wish to persevere. {Pag.39.li.13.} 4. That they can do all these things.

Or does Bertius think that they must still deliberate whether they wish to do—and will do—these things that are necessary to their salvation and perseverance? That is altogether too absurd. For anyone who earnestly desires the end also desires to use all the necessary and possible means to reach that end. Nature itself teaches this.

The righteous do, of course, deliberate about whether they will perform a particular action. But that does not concern our dispute. Why? Do we teach that the righteous person must NECESSARILY persevere in good without any interruption—that is, without ever making any breach or omitting any good work? By no means. The righteous person can, as is also evident in practice, sometimes leave the good undone and do evil. Yet, despite this, since he always remains of a mind to persevere in good and not in evil, he afterwards considers such misconduct of his and guards himself all the more carefully in future against offending in that way again. To that end he uses whatever he knows he needs, with all the greater care and diligence, so that he may not only desire to persevere in good but also be equipped to do so. [The word qualifying his desire is unclear.] So much for the first proof of the preceding claim. Bertius now adds the second proof, and that is the third fallacious argument:

<!-- B041 -->
> Anyone who is daily admonished, counselled and instructed to persevere does not have to persevere NECESSARILY.
>
> The righteous person is daily admonished to persevere, etc.
> Thus the righteous person does not have to persevere NECESSARILY.

<!-- B042 -->
Answer. If someone must persevere by absolute necessity, it is in vain to admonish him to persevere: that is true. But the righteous do not have to persevere by absolute necessity. Therefore they are not admonished in vain. Our belief is this: God did not decree from eternity to give anyone salvation or perseverance absolutely, without means. Nor does he in fact give either without means: he gives them through means. Salvation comes through persevering in faith and good works; and perseverance through prayer, watchfulness and, not to enumerate everything, admonitions, as Bertius also acknowledges concerning this last point. {pag.40. &c.}

Accordingly, it is no more absurd to say, “THIS PERSON”—namely, the one God has elected, or the one to whom God has given the ability and the will to persevere—“must necessarily persevere, and yet he must be admonished daily to persevere”, than to say, “THIS PERSON”—who is elected, or, to speak more to Bertius’s taste, foreknown as one who will be saved—“must necessarily be saved, and yet he must believe”. Likewise, it is no more absurd than saying, “THIS PERSON”—who is elected or foreknown—“must necessarily come to faith, and yet he must hear God’s word”. For just as God gives eternal life through faith, and faith through hearing his holy word, so he also gives perseverance through admonitions and other such means. As for what Bertius says:

<!-- B043 -->
> Although admonitions help people to persevere, they nevertheless do not determine the will absolutely, and consequently they do not move it by an irresistible power either. Consequently, someone can despise them and depart from the living God through apostasy. {pag.113.l.21}

<!-- B044 -->
We acknowledge that to be true. For it is not the admonitions but the Spirit that determines the will of the regenerate (in Aristotle’s manner of speaking, one would say that practised virtue determines the will of the person who has virtue to set against it [possibly: “who has acquired virtue”]), just as the flesh determines the will of the unregenerate. And because there is Spirit and flesh in the regenerate, it happens that they often sin, listening to the flesh. Yet, since the Spirit within them is stronger than the flesh, and after the sin the Spirit still remains and continues to work within them, it also happens that, having fallen, they rise again: if not immediately, then some time later. God their Lord always faithfully helps them to rise in this way: now through admonitions, now through rebukes, now through fatherly visitations, now through their distress of spirit and the disclosure of the grace he showed them before, and now through other means that he is pleased to use. For since God, not desiring the sinner’s death but that he should turn and live, {Ezech.18. 30.etc.} often deals thus even with those who are unregenerate, he cannot do less for his dear regenerate children.

It is true that all these things fail to convert those unregenerate people; {Genes.6.3. 2.Cor.4.4.} but that is because they do not have God’s Spirit. It is also true that the regenerate are not always converted immediately through one means or another, for they still have flesh within them. But that, with God using all the customary means outwardly and the Holy Spirit applying all such means inwardly, a person—I say a regenerate person—could still, notwithstanding all this, remain in sin without coming to his senses and turning: I can find that nowhere in God’s word. Indeed, it plainly conflicts with the nature, so to speak, of a regenerate person, as has already been proved more fully above; it is unnecessary to repeat it here. {pag.44.127 en pa.45.13} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M54">Marginal translation: pag.44.127 and pa.45.13.</span>

In short, Bertius has nothing to support this last argument either. [The Dutch breaks off before saying what Bertius cannot do; “support” is inferred.] The argument rests solely on Aristotle’s rules and cannot set the standard for us in these divine, supernatural matters. Aristotle, being a heathen, without God and without Christ, spoke within the limits of natural understanding. {Ephes.2.12. 1.Cor.2.14. Colos.2.8.}
