<a id="source-block-000"></a>

# Proof from God’s Word that one who is truly born again cannot fall away from the faith, etc.

<a id="source-block-001"></a>

> Jerem. 31: 33. This is the covenant that I shall make with the house of Israel after this time, says the Lord: I will put my law in their hearts and write it in their minds, and they shall be my people; so will I be their God. And cap. 32: 39. I will give them one heart and one mind, so that they shall fear me all their days, and I will put my fear in their hearts, so that they do not depart from me.

<a id="source-block-002"></a>

From these words the following inference is drawn: All those to whom God gives a heart to serve him all their days, so that they never depart from him, cannot abandon their faith, holiness and righteousness either.

<a id="source-block-003"></a>

God gives such a heart to all true Israelites—that is, as Bertius says, {pag. 121. B.} to all true believers.

<a id="source-block-004"></a>

Therefore no true believers can abandon their faith, etc.

<a id="source-block-005"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection. {pag. 121. C.}

<a id="source-block-006"></a>

> We do not deny that God gives his Spirit in our hearts and is favourable and gracious to us to the end. But the question concerns the manner in which he does so: namely, whether he does it in such a way that we cannot fail in any way. For we must not conceive of a covenant in which God binds himself to us on such terms that he alone must do everything. For in every covenant there are as many acting parties as there are contracting parties, or parties dealing with each other. For example, in this covenant there are two parties: God and man. God graciously requires of us obedience in Christ and promises eternal life. Man asks for eternal life and promises obedience. What is promised, then, is given not to man but to believing man—that is, to the one who does what he is bound to do. And every covenant is conditional, whether or not the condition is expressed.

<a id="source-block-007"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-008"></a>

I. It is not of the essence of a covenant that there should be as many acting parties as contracting parties. This is clear from the covenant that God made with Noah and all creatures after the Flood, in which God alone promises to do something, but nothing is required of man. {Genes.6.18. Et cap.9.11.}

<a id="source-block-009"></a>

II. As for the covenant of works and that of the holy Gospel, I do not deny that they are conditional, and that there are as many acting parties in them as contracting parties:

<a id="source-block-010"></a>

> Whoever does this shall live, and cursed is the one who does not do it all. {Deut. 4.1. Et cap.27.26.}

<a id="source-block-011"></a>

> Likewise, whoever believes in the Son has eternal life, and whoever does not believe is condemned. {Joan.3.36.}

<a id="source-block-012"></a>

III. Nor do I deny that a person, considered in himself, falls short of the obedience and faith required of him, and that consequently he can indeed be excluded from what is promised, namely eternal life.

<a id="source-block-013"></a>

IV. But all this is irrelevant to our subject. For from the prophet’s words we advance these two points. First, in his new covenant God requires of man only that he should know him rightly and fear him all his days; thus, if man does this, God will certainly give him eternal life. Secondly, a person cannot fail to fear God all his days if God gives him a heart to do so. And since God promises that he will not merely offer true believers such a heart, but give it to them, so that they may not merely be able to fear him if they wish, but may actually fear him without departing from him, it follows that, since God cannot lie or fail to keep his promise, such a person cannot fail to fear God all his days either. This is what Bertius ought to have contradicted if he wished to accomplish anything here. For when we ask:

<a id="source-block-014"></a>

> If God gives a person his Holy Spirit, can the person not then fail?

<a id="source-block-015"></a>

we are simply doubting whether a person can do what God enables him to do.

<a id="source-block-016"></a>

> Matth.24.24: False Christs and false prophets shall arise and shall perform great signs and wonders, so that they would deceive even the elect, if it were possible.

<a id="source-block-017"></a>

From these words the following inference is drawn:

<a id="source-block-018"></a>

Those who cannot be deceived cannot abandon their faith either.

<a id="source-block-019"></a>

The elect cannot be deceived.

<a id="source-block-020"></a>

Therefore the elect cannot abandon their faith either.

<a id="source-block-021"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-022"></a>

> The Lord is not speaking of people who are elected absolutely, {pag.122.l.5.} for no such people are to be found anywhere, and neither Holy Scripture, nor God’s nature, nor any reason in the world can allow that there should be any. Furthermore, it can be understood of the blessed angels, who {ibide lin.34.} may in a special way be called God’s elect. And further still: if one wishes to understand these words of human beings, they must {ibid.lin.11.} first be taken of those to whom God has shown some special favour or benefit above others; and then they must {pag.123.l.2.} be resolved as follows: believers cannot easily be deceived. For that the elect—that is, those to whom God is especially favourable and whom he enlightens—can nevertheless be led astray has been proved by us above sufficiently, and more than sufficiently, etc.

<a id="source-block-023"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-024"></a>

Here Bertius is in great distress and scarcely knows into what hole he can crawl. {Pag.116. lin.24.} Yet I wonder how these things could have entered a Christian’s mind in opposition to such manifest truth. {2 Pag.122. lin.38.} For as to the first point, “The Lord is not speaking of people who are elected absolutely”: why not? Because there are no such people. Why are there no such people? From what is that evident? Bertius says it: therefore! And if I were then to say, “The Lord is speaking here of people elected absolutely, for there are no other elect”, what more would Bertius have said and proved than I?

Furthermore, “It can be understood of the angels”, he says. This is sheer childishness. The angels are in heaven; the false prophets are on earth, and they will perform signs on earth, not in heaven: to win over human beings, not to deceive angels. How, then, can this serve to prove the power of deception, as he says, if Christ merely meant that the angels could not be deceived by the false prophets, since, being in heaven, they will neither see the signs nor hear the teaching that the false prophets will produce on earth? Nevertheless, the angels are called elect: therefore! Why should one not be allowed to understand the angels here by “the elect”? That is what Bertius wishes to say. {Vergelijckt het 22.24. en 31. veerskens deses 24. capittels Matthei/met malcanderen/so sult ghij lichtelijck verstaen dat door uytvercorene hier gheen Engelen connen worden verstaen. T’gaet de Engelen sullen de uytvercorene vergaderen: Maer de Engelen en sullen haer selven niet vergaderen.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: Compare the 22.24. and 31. verses of this 24. chapter of Matthew with one another; then you will easily understand that “elect” here cannot mean angels. The angels shall gather the elect; but the angels shall not gather themselves.</em></span>

It is true that one reads once—and no more—of the elect angels. It is clear enough from this that some angels have been cast out; {1. Tim. 5.21.} but from what will it be clear that “elect”, used absolutely in this way, is to mean angels? Besides, we also read that Christ is called God’s elect. {Luc. 23.35.} Let us therefore rather, if Bertius pleases, understand the words in this way: “So that they would deceive even the elect, that is, Christ and his holy angels, if it were possible.” Or, to come still closer: since the text does not say “God’s elect”, but only “the elect”, and it is certain that the ungodly also have their elect, as Bertius admits, let us rather understand the words thus: “They would deceive the elect, that is, the greatest and most ungodly, if it were possible.” {pa. 122. l.28.} But this does not sound right. So Bertius proceeds further and says:

<a id="source-block-025"></a>

> If one wishes to understand these words of human beings, understand by them those to whom God is especially favourable.

<a id="source-block-026"></a>

But, dear sir, why is that? Because there are no absolute elect; and elsewhere in Scripture “elect” sometimes means such people, so why not here too? What do you think? Is that not conclusive? Therefore this much, at least, is certain: those to whom God is especially favourable cannot be deceived. And since God is more favourable to none among all the children of men here on earth than to the regenerate, his dear children, it would also have to be certain, according to Christ’s saying and Bertius’s explanation, that the regenerate cannot be deceived. That is what we would gladly have Bertius acknowledge with us.

But what hurry is he in? Just listen to this fine masterpiece: “One must resolve these words thus: believers cannot easily be deceived.” Not easily? Why do you add that? Previously, when we spoke of the angels, it was altogether impossible; now it is possible, but not easy. How does this come about? Because believers can be deceived, but the more a person is strengthened, the harder he is to deceive; and what happens with difficulty and seldom is called impossible. {pag. 123. l.7.} Thus Scripture must say whatever best pleases his mind, and we must believe (if we wish to do justice to their meaning) whatever they dare to say. If we too might use such an advantage, what difficulty would there be? When Bertius demonstrated in his fashion from Scripture that believers can be deceived, we would need only say, “This is not to be understood of true believers, for true believers cannot be deceived.” But would we then have acted honestly? {pag. 125. l.15} Apparently so, for “if we had intended to seek evasions”, says Bertius, “and creep under the hedge, we might also have said that Christ’s statement is conditional; but it was our duty to act honestly.” Therefore Bertius has undoubtedly acted honestly here.

But when we seek to govern ourselves by Bertius’s practice and offer an absurd or ill-considered gloss on a passage of Scripture as a fitting explanation, what is it then? Sheer childishness; trifles; speaking doubtfully and haltingly; an improper opinion; striking against the goad; mocking God; dealing without godly sincerity [possibly “with godly sincerity”], etc. {Pa. 101. l. 3.} {〃 104 l. 3.; 〃 109 l. 19; 〃 156 l. 28; 〃 117 l. 11; 〃 ll. 34} May we not say the same of Bertius’s many crooked leaps here, made more from desperation (though he will not have it called that) than to show his skill? Sheer childishness, trifles, etc. Indeed, unless he clothes his assertions with better reasons. But look at all the different speculations Bertius has had about these words, and how his thoughts have accused and excused him:

<a id="source-block-027"></a>

> If one wishes to understand Christ’s words of human beings, he says, this is the meaning: In the last days the power of deception will be great, so that it will come close to carrying away into error even those who are acceptable to God and whom ordinary people regard as pillars of the churches, etc. {pag. 123 §. 30}

<a id="source-block-028"></a>

If this is the meaning of Christ’s words, why have so many words been tortured here? 1. “It will come close”: therefore let it fall just a little short, and it is certain that it will not and cannot happen. 2. “Those who are acceptable to God and are regarded by ordinary people as pillars of the churches”: are these difficult to deceive? I am content for Bertius to say that they can easily be deceived. But what does all this contribute to our subject? We are speaking not of those regarded as good and believing, as was agreed above, but of those who actually and truly are so before God. For when he says “those who are acceptable to God”, this does not mean that they are believing, righteous and sanctified before God, but only that they have possessed some excellent gifts, like Saul, Judas and the like. It is of no help for Bertius to wish to say that all who are called elect have also been believing, holy and righteous, or at least to wish to prove that it must be understood so here. I scarcely know, or do not know at all, any means that could serve him in this.

In sum, the elect are the angels, or believing human beings, or those otherwise acceptable to God—even those regarded by ordinary people as pillars of the churches. “If it is possible” means: it is in no way possible; it is truly possible; it falls little short of happening. This is then proved from Scripture, from “samiaspe” [an unidentified source], from Beza, whom Bertius otherwise opposes in this dispute, from Aristotle, a pagan who had not heard of Christ, and I know not what else: so that it becomes quite clear that Bertius has had difficulty quieting his conscience when he wished to contradict the plain truth. And yet he still cannot leave it alone. Wishing to prove the absurdity of our interpretation of these words, he puts forward the following argument, which, he says, would have to be drawn from them according to our understanding:

<a id="source-block-029"></a>

> Those who cannot be deceived must guard themselves against being deceived. {Pag. 124. lin. 30.}
>
> The elect, by virtue of God’s decree, cannot be deceived.
>
> Therefore the elect must guard themselves against being deceived.

<a id="source-block-030"></a>

{Pag. 125. lin. 6.} “And who does not see”, he goes on to say, “that the major premise is unsound, and that the whole conclusion is far removed from Christ’s intention?”, etc. Answer: And who, in turn, does not see how badly Bertius goes astray here, confusing God’s decree or promise with its proper application by each individual? For it is a very different thing to say “The elect cannot be deceived” and to say “You, Peter, Paul, John, etc., cannot be deceived.” The first assertion is absolutely true, for God’s decree cannot fail. The second is not absolutely true, but must be understood with this condition: if you are elect.

But how shall they know whether they are elect? If they find that they believe, love God and their neighbours, do good and leave evil alone; in sum, if they find themselves disposed as regenerate people are. For although even those still sunk in unbelief and every kind of ungodliness can be called elect with respect to God’s omniscience, nevertheless no one can know that he is elect unless he has already actually been delivered from his unbelief and ungodliness. I say “actually”, for many often deceive themselves in this respect too, imagining that they are what they nevertheless are not. 1. Corin. 10. 12. 1. Tim. 6. 4. Gal. 6. 3. Ioan. 9 41. Apoc. 3. 17. Tit. 1. 16. Rom. 2. 3. 1. Corin. 3. 18. and cap. 8. 2. and cap. 14. 37.

Christ’s intention in these words, then, is to exhort to watchfulness and diligent, careful vigilance against being deceived. This exhortation concerns both the elect and the non-elect, just as both will also be tempted to apostasy by the false prophets. But victory is promised here to the elect alone: namely, that they will not—indeed, cannot—be deceived. This is not to make them careless, but to give them heart and courage through the certainty of victory. Thus he lets everyone firmly believe that the elect cannot be deceived, while meanwhile leaving everyone with this concern: whether he himself is certainly elect; and if so, that he should prove it true by resisting vigorously. As Peter says: {2. Petr. 1. 10} Therefore, brothers, be all the more diligent to make your calling and election sure.

<a id="source-block-031"></a>

> Joan 4.14. Whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall become in him a fountain of springing water unto eternal life.

<a id="source-block-032"></a>

From these words the following syllogism is drawn:

<a id="source-block-033"></a>

Anyone in whom the water he has drunk does not dry up, but becomes a fountain of springing water unto eternal life, must necessarily always be watered, and consequently cannot fail to bring forth good fruits.

<a id="source-block-034"></a>

In all those who drink of the water Christ gives, that same water becomes a fountain of springing water unto eternal life.

<a id="source-block-035"></a>

Therefore all who drink of the water Christ gives must necessarily always be watered and bear fruits.

<a id="source-block-036"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-037"></a>

Someone who has drunk once can cease drinking. Jerem. 2.13. {Pa.125 l.24}

<a id="source-block-038"></a>

> They forsake me, the living spring, and make themselves cisterns that hold no water.

<a id="source-block-039"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-040"></a>

This conflicts entirely with Christ’s words. For if someone who has drunk once can cease drinking, then someone who has drunk once can thirst again; likewise, the water he has drunk can be consumed and dry up in him. But Christ says:

<a id="source-block-041"></a>

> He shall not thirst again, and it shall become in him a fountain of springing water unto eternal life.

<a id="source-block-042"></a>

Mark this: a fountain; springing water; unto eternal life. By this Christ clearly meant to indicate that the nature of the water he would give was, first, to quench present thirst; and secondly, to prevent any further thirst from coming. In this respect it differs from the water Jacob drank, for whoever drinks that will thirst again. {Vers.13} That is, whoever drinks from Jacob’s well will indeed quench his thirst with it; but when he stops drinking, he will thirst again, because the water he has drunk does not remain in him but is consumed by the heat of the stomach. If that did not happen, or if someone drank continually, he would not thirst.

As for what is adduced from the prophet Jeremiah, it has no bearing on this matter. For forsaking the spring and ceasing to drink are very different things. Someone who has never drunk from the spring can forsake it, but no one can cease drinking except someone who has drunk. Moreover, someone who belongs only to the outward covenant of grace can forsake God, that is, this living spring [another reading calls him “his God”]; but no one can cease drinking unless he has also been in grace itself. And not all those who are in a people’s covenant of grace, says Bertius, are therefore immediately in grace itself. {Pag.19.N.4.}

<a id="source-block-043"></a>

> Johann. 6.35. {4.} I am that bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst.

<a id="source-block-044"></a>

From this we conclude as follows:

<a id="source-block-045"></a>

Anyone who cannot hunger or thirst because he has within him spiritual nourishment that abides for ever cannot die either.

<a id="source-block-046"></a>

No one who believes in Christ can hunger or thirst.

<a id="source-block-047"></a>

Therefore no one who believes in Christ can die.

<a id="source-block-048"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-049"></a>

{Psal. 25.13.} These words concern those who do their duty.

<a id="source-block-050"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-051"></a>

But what does Bertius mean here by our duty? Does he mean that we must hunger, thirst, eat and drink daily, and that this is our duty? That cannot be so. For otherwise, where would be the distinction that Christ makes here between himself, the true heavenly bread, and the manna of the Israelites of old, as can be seen from the text? Those who ate the manna, says Christ, hungered again, and so had to eat again each time, and at last they all died. But whoever eats this bread never hungers again, and therefore does not need to eat again either; nor does he ever die.

Or does he mean that we must keep within us this heavenly bread, this spiritual nourishment, which we have once taken into ourselves in faith? And that this is our duty, so that we shall neither hunger nor thirst as long as we do not cast out or vomit up that nourishment? This is indeed true, but it is altogether out of place here. For our whole dispute is whether this can happen: that is, whether such spiritual nourishment—which is Christ, the heavenly bread—can be cast out. To this we say no. For 1. If Christ can be cast out by those who have truly come to partake of him, those same people can hunger again. But they shall never hunger again, says Christ. 2. If Christ can be cast out, then he is not bread of eternal life, and those who have come to partake of him have not had within them a beginning of eternal life. For how can life that can come to an end be called eternal? But Christ is the bread of eternal life, and those who share in his goodness [the wording is unclear] receive a beginning of eternal life. Therefore Christ cannot be cast out.

<a id="source-block-052"></a>

> Joan. 6.37. All that my Father gives me shall come to me, and whoever comes to me I shall not cast out. {1.}

<a id="source-block-053"></a>

From this the following argument is made:

<a id="source-block-054"></a>

Anyone who, once received by Christ, is not afterwards cast out again cannot fall away from his faith.

<a id="source-block-055"></a>

All who have been given to Christ by the Father and believe in him are received by Christ and are not cast out again.

<a id="source-block-056"></a>

Therefore such people cannot fall away from their faith.

<a id="source-block-057"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-058"></a>

Christ will not cast out the one who comes; but what if the person himself draws back? {Pa. 73. l. 28}

<a id="source-block-059"></a>

> Joan. 17.12. Those whom you have given me I have kept, and none of them has been lost except the son of perdition.

<a id="source-block-060"></a>

Or if the Father cuts off the unworthy one?

<a id="source-block-061"></a>

> Joan. 15.2. Every vine branch in me that bears no fruit he takes away.

<a id="source-block-062"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-063"></a>

Since our present dispute is whether believers can draw back, our argument, which we adduce to prove our negative answer, is answered most irrelevantly with “What if the person himself draws back?” For that is the question: whether such a thing can happen. We say no. If Bertius wishes to oppose this, let him overturn our proof and not our conclusion. Our proof is this: no one is cast out unless he has first been inside. Therefore all who are given to Christ by the Father are received within. {Bertius stelt dat aengenomen worden van Christo, is een Tempel des H. Gheestes worden.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: Bertius states that to be received by Christ is to become a temple of the Holy Spirit.</em></span> But within what? Within the true church, within Christ’s body, within the number of those who shall be saved. Thus someone who is once within the true church, within Christ’s body, remains within it; he is not cast out.

<a id="source-block-064"></a>

> Outside shall be the dogs, sorcerers, fornicators, murderers, idolaters and all who love falsehood. {Pa. 35. li. 22.} {Apoc. 22.15.}

<a id="source-block-065"></a>

These, I say, shall not be received into Christ’s body, into the true church here on earth, nor into heaven after this life. And if anyone here, having been incorporated into the true church, drew back and became a dog, a sorcerer, a fornicator, etc., he would not remain inside, but would be cast out. In sum, no one is cast out except one who draws back. And just as one may therefore say, “This person is no dog, sorcerer or fornicator, etc., since he has been received and accepted by Christ”, so one may also say, “This person shall not become a dog or sorcerer, etc., since he will not be cast out.”

<a id="source-block-066"></a>

As for Judas the traitor, he was never given to Christ to enjoy salvation, but only to serve in the apostolic office. This is how even the Remonstrants understand the passage cited above, Joan. 17. Christ himself also makes this sufficiently known to us when he says: {Coll. pa. 10.} {Joan. 6.64.}

<a id="source-block-067"></a>

> There are some among you who do not believe. For Jesus knew from the beginning who they were that did not believe and who it was that would betray him. And he said: Therefore I have told you that no one can come to me unless it is given him by my Father. And further afterwards: One of you is a devil. He said this of Judas, son of Simon Iscariot. {Vers.70.}

<a id="source-block-068"></a>

From this one can readily gather that Judas never believed, never came to Christ, and was never given to Christ by the Father for salvation. I am not a little astonished that Bertius allows himself to be moved by such childish reasons to believe the contrary. {Pa.35 li.14.} For he writes of it thus:

<a id="source-block-069"></a>

> It seems that Judas believed at first, since, having left all his possessions, he was enrolled among the apostles; {Matth.10.} and the Lord prayed for him as well as for the others; {Luce 6.12.} and he was given to Christ by the Father; {Ioan.17.12.} and he confessed and preached Christ; {Matt.10.7.} and he was incorporated into Christ; {Ioan.15.2.} and the promise of greater glory in eternal life was also made to him. {Mat.19.28}

<a id="source-block-070"></a>

What? Must all those be called believers who leave their possessions, who confess and preach Christ, for whom Christ prays, or who have in some manner been given to or incorporated into Christ? Bertius would first have to prove that. As for the promise mentioned here, it does indeed serve to prove that Judas was promised eternal life, or great glory in eternal life, but not to prove that he believed, or that this was promised him on account of his faith. For when Peter says: {Vers.27.}

<a id="source-block-071"></a>

> Lord, we have left everything and followed you; what shall we receive?

<a id="source-block-072"></a>

and Christ answers him:

<a id="source-block-073"></a>

> You who have followed me shall, in the regeneration, etc.

<a id="source-block-074"></a>

it is apparent that this promise is made to them because they had become Christ’s apostles. But they had not become that through faith, as we become God’s children through faith. And this is all that concerns Bertius’s first objection.

<a id="source-block-075"></a>

As for the second: “What if the Father”, he says, “were to cut off the unworthy?” Joan 15.2. “Every branch”, etc. I answer that the grafted branches are of two kinds. {Pa.126.II.1.} Some are grafted in such a way that they also grow firmly into the trunk, so that they share in the sap and strength of the root. Some are grafted in such a way that they are only outwardly attached to the trunk, but have not grown into it; hence they wither too, being unable to obtain any moisture from the root. We certainly see this happening daily with trees, from which the comparison is taken here. Thus two kinds of branches are, as it were, grafted into Christ: some only outwardly, and these bring forth no good fruit; some also grow inwardly into Christ and partake of his Spirit and strength {Rom.6.5.} to bring forth good fruits. It was of this second kind that Christ spoke in the passage cited above, Johann 15.2.

<a id="source-block-076"></a>

“Every vine branch in me that bears no fruit he takes away”: that is the first kind, which is indeed attached to Christ but has not grown into him. For we must carefully observe that no branch can bear good fruit unless it enjoys the sap of the root on which it stands. Thus one of the two is necessary: either the grafted branch that bears no fruit has not properly grown onto and into the trunk; or the trunk has no good sap in it, or at least not enough to impart sufficiently to all the branches it bears. Consequently it would reflect to Christ’s disgrace if a branch, which of itself can bear no fruit, bore no fruit even when grafted into him. For that could arise from nothing else than that Christ did not supply it with nourishment, strength and ability to bear fruit. Now of the second kind Christ goes on to say there: “And every vine branch that bears fruit the Father cleanses, so that it may bear more fruit.”

<a id="source-block-077"></a>

This is a beautiful and comforting saying, from which the following argument from the text can be made:

<a id="source-block-078"></a>

Those who are cleansed by the Father so that they daily bear more and more fruit—I do not say “can bear”, but actually bear it—can never cease believing and doing good.

<a id="source-block-079"></a>

All who bear fruit—that is, all who have been incorporated into Christ and have grown into him—are cleansed by the Father so that they daily bear more fruits.

<a id="source-block-080"></a>

Therefore all who bear fruit—that is, all who have been incorporated into Christ—can never cease believing.

<a id="source-block-081"></a>

Thus Christ’s words in Joan. 15.2. not only do not overturn our conclusion and purpose, but could themselves especially confirm and strengthen it.

<a id="source-block-082"></a>

> Joan.6.39. This is the will of the Father who sent me: that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but should raise it up on the last day. {6.} {Siet August. int boec van de straffe en ghenade. c.9.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: See August. in the book On Rebuke and Grace. c.9.</em></span>

<a id="source-block-083"></a>

From this we conclude as follows:

<a id="source-block-084"></a>

Anyone whom Christ CANNOT lose—or destroy—CANNOT depart from the faith. For Christ can and shall destroy all who depart.

<a id="source-block-085"></a>

Of all those whom the Father has given to Christ—that is, those who {Mar. 16.} believe in him—Christ cannot destroy anyone. For Christ can do nothing other than what the Father wills.

<a id="source-block-086"></a>

Therefore, of all those whom the Father has given to Christ, no one can depart from the faith.

<a id="source-block-087"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-088"></a>

> All these things must be understood of those who remain steadfast to the end. Unbelievers are not touched upon here. {Mat. 24. 13.}

<a id="source-block-089"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-090"></a>

And our argument is not touched upon here by Bertius, much less overturned. He says: this must be understood of the steadfast. But why? Because Bertius says it? That does not follow. And what, then, must be understood of the steadfast? “All these things”, he says. Therefore all whom the Father gives to Christ are steadfast; all who see the Son and believe in him are steadfast; and consequently Christ will destroy none of them, for all these things must be understood of those who remain steadfast. Has Bertius not managed his business well now?

Or does he reformulate it thus: Christ will destroy nothing of all that the Father gives him, provided only that they remain steadfast? This fits much worse still. For then Christ would have come {Ber. 38.} not to do the will of the one who sent him, as he says, but would have had to come to do the will of human beings: that is, to save and not to destroy whatever wished to remain with Christ. Thus God would indeed wish that all whom he gives to Christ should be saved, but matters would nevertheless not proceed accordingly. Rather, as man wills, so Christ wills too: if man wishes to remain, Christ saves him; if man does not wish to remain, God must be patient, for Christ must then destroy him. Henceforth, then, matters depend not on God’s mercy, but on our willing and running. See Rom. 9. 16.

<a id="source-block-113"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-114"></a>

This is a very absurd thing to say. For believing, being in Christ’s hand and being a sheep of Christ are one and the same thing, just as not believing, not being in Christ’s hand and not being a sheep of Christ are one and the same thing. Thus, when one says, “Christ gives his sheep eternal life, and they are not seized out of Christ’s hand as long as they remain sheep, or if they remain sheep to the end”, it is as much as saying: no one remains a sheep of Christ if he remains a sheep of Christ; no one remains in the faith as long as he remains in the faith; no one is seized out of Christ’s hand as long as he is not seized out of Christ’s hand. This, I say, is very absurd. Christ meant to teach something very different when he says: {Vers. 28.}

<a id="source-block-115"></a>

> My sheep shall never perish.

<a id="source-block-116"></a>

For if a sheep can change into a goat, someone who is now a sheep can also be lost. But someone who is now a sheep can never be lost: therefore! And again: {ver. 28. ende 29}

<a id="source-block-117"></a>

> No one shall or can seize them out of my hand and out of my Father’s hand.

<a id="source-block-118"></a>

No one? Therefore not the devil either, nor sin; for sin is the only means by which the devil can kill us. The sheep themselves cannot become estranged either, nor desert from the Father’s hands to the enemy. For that cannot happen except through the devil’s deception and violence; but the Father, who has not only given Christ to us but given us to Christ, is greater and mightier than the devil—indeed, than all.

Now, as for what Bertius goes on to relate from the prophet Ezekiel, Cap. 34. 16., and the explanation he gives of it, all of it is irrelevant to our subject. {Pa. 127. li. 1.} For in the prophet “sheep” means not believers, but generally all those Israelites who were in God’s covenant, as can be seen from the entire text. We read the same in Matth. 10 6. and Capit. 15. 24. Joan. 11 17., and in other places. And it is an entirely different thing to speak of those who are merely in the covenant of grace and to speak of those who, besides this, are also in grace itself.

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-139"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-140"></a>

Hope puts to shame when what one hopes for does not follow or come about. If hope can be rejected, and is indeed rejected by some, then it also puts some to shame. But the apostle says: “Hope does not put to shame.” This cannot be understood as “if it is preserved”. For hope gives no one what he hopes for; rather, only when it ceases do we obtain what we hope for. It is true that many do not obtain what they hope for, and consequently it might seem that hope can […], or at least that hope does put someone to shame. But such hope, if one is even to call it that, is built on no firm foundation and is therefore an empty hope, as can be seen in Iob 8.11. Psal.112,10. Prov.10,28. Jerem.2,37., and elsewhere.

Indeed, many firmly hope, right to the end of their lives, that they will be saved, while meanwhile they are sunk, and remain sunk to the end, in unbelief and every kind of sin and shame. Shall we say that hope does not put such people to shame because it is preserved and not rejected? Far from it. The apostle speaks of the hope of believers who are righteous before God and who do not imagine it for themselves, but through sure belief and experience know that God loves them in Jesus Christ. He says that their hope does not put them to shame: that is, that what they hope for will not fail them.

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-147"></a>

> Very well, if we retain faith; but if we wound our conscience and are not restored through repentance, must we not necessarily suffer shipwreck in the faith? {pag.128.l.1.}

<a id="source-block-148"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-149"></a>

This is irrelevant to the matter, and it expressly contradicts the holy apostle’s meaning. With these words he wishes to comfort believers and teach that they shall glory and rejoice in their cross, not doubting that they will obtain a good outcome unto salvation. For, he says, if God loved you so much when you were his enemies, in sin and under wrath, that {Eph.2.4.5.} he gave you his only Son to die for you, and also gave you faith to receive that same Son, so that you should become God’s friends—indeed, God’s children—and be freed from sin and wrath: how should that same God not now love you so much, now that you are his children, free from sin and from his wrath, that he would keep you from wrath, that is, keep you from falling under wrath again?

Does a father love his wicked children? How much more, then, the good ones? Does God let rain fall upon the unrighteous? How much more upon the righteous? Does he receive us when we are not his? How shall he not keep us when we are his? If we were reconciled to God through Christ’s death when we were God’s enemies, {Rom. 5.10.} how shall we not much more be saved through Christ’s life now that we are reconciled? Thus one can clearly see that St Paul wishes believers to be perfectly sure concerning that about which Bertius wishes them to doubt. Consequently his interpretation does not at all agree with Paul’s words.

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-156"></a>

> First, these words do not serve at all to prove the [unclear qualification] decree to save certain particular persons absolutely. Rather, these words tend towards a quite different end: namely, to prove that believers ought patiently to bear the cross and every kind of adversity, as those who were ordained to it beforehand and have now also been called to it, etc. {pa.128 l 22, en pag.129. gheheel.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: pa.128 l 22, and the whole of pag.129.</em></span>

<a id="source-block-157"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-158"></a>

Bertius is playing the master a little too much for my liking here. “These words”, he says, “do not serve at all for the absolute decree to save anyone.” Why? Is everything true simply because Bertius says it? {sal wel waerheyt zijn om datter Bertius seyt / te meer moet het waerheyt zijn / om datter so veel anderen hebben gheseyt.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: If it is to be true because Bertius says it, it must be all the more true because so many others have said it.</em></span> Yet these words had already been used, on good grounds, to prove such an absolute decree before Bertius was born. And they were used by people whose trains Bertius would scarcely be worthy to carry after them, in respect of learning, godliness, constancy and many other excellent virtues. Nevertheless, they serve no such purpose at all—and that because it does not please Bertius. Therefore: patience!

But what if they served, at least, solely to show clearly the certainty of salvation and the stages of God’s dealings in restoring the human race: God’s foreknowledge, election, calling, justification and glorification? Many of Bertius’s good friends would readily have allowed this, {a Pag. 102. lin.12. en pa.98.l.1.} such as Melanchthon, {b inde ghemeene plaetsen/pa.490. 491. en 698.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: b In the Commonplaces, pa.490. 491. and 698.</em></span> Chemnitz, {c Tegẽ ’t Cõcilie van Trenten/ fol.172.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: c Against the Council of Trent, fol.172.</em></span> Hemmingsen, {d over dese woorden in questie.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: d On these words in question.</em></span> Arminius, {e disputatie vande roepinge ter salicheyt thes 8 En over ’t sevende capit. totten Romeynen.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: e Disputation on the calling to salvation, thes 8, and on the seventh chapter to the Romans.</em></span> and others like them. None of this may be allowed. These words must be understood of believers’ cross. But why? Bertius cannot understand it otherwise. Let us see, then, whether we can point it out to him a little more clearly.

First, just as calling follows God’s foreordination, and glorification follows justification, so justification also follows calling, as the text makes clear. But those called to the cross, Bertius says, God has justified long before; and it is also certain that not all those called to the cross are justified. {f Pag.129. lin.32.} Therefore there can be no discussion here of foreordination or calling to the cross. If you wish to say that many are also called to Christ who are nevertheless not justified, I answer from the text that such people are not called according to God’s purpose. {g vers 28}

Secondly, the cross is not necessary to salvation for all believers. All believers must indeed prepare themselves for the cross and resolve to bear it if God appoints it to them. But nowhere does Holy Scripture teach that God wished precisely all saints, all who shall be glorified, without exception, also to suffer—and that for Christ’s sake—before entering glory, as Bertius alleges here and as must necessarily follow from the gloss he places on these words. {Dwalh 2.1. Matth.16.24 pa.129.l.25. en volgens.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: The concluding “en volgens” means “and following”. The preceding uncertain abbreviation and reference strings are retained literally above.</em></span> For that would condemn all those who turn to God only at their last hour. Unless one were to understand by the cross and suffering the common hatred of the devil and the world against all believers; likewise, the mortification of the old man, and the like. For it is more than evident that not all those who are saved become martyrs. Thus carrying the cross cannot be the subject here either.

Rather, these words serve only to describe the person for whose good the cross will work, and the reason why this will happen. For all who love God the cross must work for the best, says the apostle. Who are those who love God? They are those whom God has called according to his purpose. But why shall the cross help them towards good? Because God foreknew them and took pleasure in them, so that he also ordained them beforehand to be conformed to the image of his Son. This image consists not only in suffering and glorification, but in knowledge, truth, holiness and righteousness. {Ephes. 4.24 Coloss. 3.10.} And all whom God has thus ordained beforehand from eternity, he has called—according to his purpose, that is—and those whom he has called he has justified through faith in Christ, {Rom. 10.17.} which faith is given through the calling; and those whom God has justified, he has also decreed to glorify.

Since God’s decree cannot fail or be overturned, believers who have been justified, who love God because he first loved them and forgave their sins, cannot doubt that they shall come to salvation. {1 Joan. 4.19} {Luc. 7.47.} Therefore they must be not only patient but also joyful in their cross. This is the true sense of the apostle’s words, and they have almost always been understood so. At least I know of no one who has proved, or tried to prove by any reasons, that they could not be understood in this way. As for Bertius, he says much against it, but assertion will not do: proof is what is required here. {Psal.116.1.2.} May God grant us to handle Holy Scripture in simplicity and godly sincerity, as before God’s face.

<a id="source-block-176"></a>

The apostle does not say, “God shall strengthen you so that you can be blameless”, but “so that you are blameless”—not merely for a time, but to the end and on the day of Jesus Christ, that is, on the last day. ## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-183"></a>

> We know the apostle’s words and believe them; but we also know that the Spirit can be grieved and quenched. When this happens, the pledge of life is lost together with eternal life. {pag. 131. ll. 7.}

<a id="source-block-184"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-185"></a>

1. We too know the apostle’s words and believe them. And we also know that, although the Spirit can be grieved in believers, he nevertheless cannot thereby be entirely expelled or quenched; and consequently the pledge of life cannot be lost together with eternal life. Bertius’s words have now been repaid with words. But is it not shameful that Bertius dismisses the matter so lightly, and yet wishes his bare assertion to be accepted as though it were a word of God or of the apostles?

<a id="source-block-186"></a>

2. But to see more clearly the firmness of our preceding conclusion from these words of St Paul, and more appropriately to rebuke the absurdity and unfounded audacity of Bertius in contradicting it, observe only that it is stated here that God has sealed believers with the Holy Spirit. For this teaches that God has marked believers as his own and distinguished them as such from all other people, in order to keep them and let them receive what he has promised only to his own. {Exod. 12.13.} Just as in Egypt the smiting angel passed over all the houses that were marked and sealed with the lamb’s blood; {Ezec. 9.4.6.} and in Jerusalem all who were marked with a sign were spared—namely all who sighed over the abominations committed there; {Apoc. 7.2.3.} and the four angels who had power to harm the earth and sea were forbidden to do any harm until God’s servants had been marked with the seal of the living God, so that they might thus remain unharmed: so God has marked and sealed his believers with his Holy Spirit, in order that, as his own, they should be and remain protected from all the devil’s destruction. {2. Tim. 2.18, 19.} That is the first point.

Further, observe that the Holy Spirit is at once the pledge of our inheritance: not a bare pledge, but arrhabo, an earnest or part-payment, that is, such a pledge as one gives as part of the whole that has been promised, to assure that the remainder will without doubt also follow. It is like promising to give someone a hundred guilders and giving him ten guilders in hand now as a pledge and firm proof that one intends to keep the contract and certainly pay the remaining money too. So God gives believers his Holy Spirit: that is, through the Spirit, such peace and joy in their conscience, such movements in their will, heart and understanding, that here in this world they feel within themselves the beginning of eternal life. {Rom. 8.24. Ephe. 2.5.6. 1. Joan. 3.2. Catechismus inde 18. vraghe.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: The final reference is to the Catechism, in the 18. question; the preceding references remain as printed above.</em></span> This is to assure them that God intends to let them receive that same eternal life in full, just as he once graciously promised it to them in Jesus Christ.

Consequently it is impossible that believers who find such workings of the Spirit within themselves should be excluded from eternal life. {Rom. 11.29. Jer. 32.39.40.} For God cannot take back his pledge, which is the Holy Spirit; and the Holy Spirit, who cannot be idle in God’s heirs, cannot be expelled by them either. Let this suffice here.

<a id="source-block-187"></a>

> Ephe. 4.30. Do not grieve God’s Holy Spirit, with whom you have been sealed unto the day of redemption.

<a id="source-block-188"></a>

From these words the same argument is drawn as from the immediately preceding words, Ephes. 1.13., for here too the sealing that takes place in believers through the Holy Spirit is spoken of.

<a id="source-block-189"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-190"></a>

> These words must be understood like the preceding ones. I wonder that the opponent adduces them. From them I conclude thus: {Pag. 131. lin. 11, 15, 16.} One who can be grieved by the wilful sins of those who once believed can also be driven out.
>
> The Holy Spirit can be grieved.
>
> Therefore he can also be driven out.

<a id="source-block-191"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-192"></a>

Since these words must be understood like the preceding ones, we too stand by what was said in explanation of the preceding words. We add only this: we must carefully observe that it says “unto a day of redemption”. Scripture speaks of two kinds of redemption: one that has taken place through Jesus Christ’s blood, namely the forgiveness of our sins; {Roma. 6 22. Ephes. 1.7.} and the other that will take place on the last day, when, sin and death having been swallowed up, Christ will present his congregation without spot or wrinkle before his Father, wipe all tears from their eyes and lead them into eternal life. {Luc. 21.28. Openb. 6.17.} The apostle speaks here of this final, complete redemption, teaching that the Holy Spirit serves not only to seal to believers that they have been freed from sin through Christ, but also to seal that they shall be brought to salvation through Christ.

Thus it is impossible that believers should not have been freed and delivered from sin, now that the Holy Spirit seals this to them; otherwise the Holy Spirit would have to seal and assure them of what is false or uncertain. So it is also impossible that believers should not be assured of their salvation—that is, that they will undoubtedly come to salvation—now that the same Holy Spirit assures them of this too. Otherwise the Holy Spirit would have to seal and assure them of what is false or uncertain; and that is a contradiction: namely, to make certain what is uncertain.

As for Bertius’s syllogism which he further constructs from these words, I deny its first part: namely, that one who can be grieved can be driven out. It would have been well worth the trouble if Bertius had sought to prove that with a word, for then, undoubtedly, he would have had some material for contradicting us. As it is, omitting all proof, he gives us all the more reason to disregard his assertion and allegation. I readily acknowledge that the Holy Spirit can be grieved, and not only through wilful sins, as Bertius seems to think, but also through sins committed when one is taken unawares. For the Holy Spirit grieves, and is grieved, over and through everything that is sin.

But Bertius in turn acknowledges that not every wilful sin by which the Holy Spirit is grieved also drives out the Holy Spirit, {Pag. 16. l. 23.} and that one can be restored to one’s former state after those same sins. {Ibid. n. 10.} It is true that he adds {Ibid. lin. 32.} that one can thereby easily fall into a general apostasy, when someone, neglecting repentance [the word is unclear], and despising the Holy Spirit’s admonitions and knockings, suppresses grace and does not cease wallowing in the mire of sins. But he does not prove that this has ever happened to a believing, regenerate person. For what he alleges there from Mat. 13. 12. and 2. Timoth. 3. 13. is irrelevant, unless by “evil people”, “deceivers” and “those who do not have” he wished to understand regenerate people—that is, light by darkness.

Consequently Bertius’s foregoing syllogism remains not only unproved but is also almost openly contradicted by himself. Thus our argument still stands firm and immovable; and it is astonishing that Bertius nevertheless dared to express astonishment at us. But it is better to have said something than to have sat mute, keeping silent.

> 1. Thessal. 5. 23. May the God of peace sanctify you wholly and perfectly, and may your perfect spirit, soul and body be kept blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. He who has called you is faithful, and he will also do it. {17.}

<a id="source-block-194"></a>

From this we conclude as follows:

<a id="source-block-195"></a>

Those who are wholly and perfectly sanctified, and whose perfect spirit, soul and body are kept blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, cannot fall away.

<a id="source-block-196"></a>

All believing, regenerate people are thus sanctified and kept by God.

<a id="source-block-197"></a>

Therefore the regenerate cannot fall away.

<a id="source-block-198"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-199"></a>

> From a wish one can infer no more than the possibility of the deed. God is truly faithful to do what he has promised—indeed, more than he has promised. But we too must keep our promise, for God, who is faithful, loves the faithful person. 2. Timoth. 2. 13. {pag. 131. l. 26}

<a id="source-block-200"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-201"></a>

The force of our argument rests not upon the holy apostle’s wish, but upon these words: “He will also do it.” And when God does what the apostle wishes, it is impossible that a person should not do what he has promised. I take Bertius’s own conscience as witness—indeed, as judge—in this matter.

<a id="source-block-202"></a>

> 1. Pet. 1. 3. Who according to his great mercy has begotten us anew unto a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, {18.} unto an incorruptible, undefilable and unfading inheritance, kept in the heavens for you, who are kept in God’s power through faith unto salvation, ready to be revealed in the last times.

<a id="source-block-203"></a>

From these words the following inference is drawn:

<a id="source-block-204"></a>

Those for whom an incorruptible inheritance is kept in the heavens cannot cease to believe. For this inheritance is laid away only for believers.

<a id="source-block-205"></a>

Such an inheritance is kept or laid away for all who are kept through faith in God’s power.

<a id="source-block-206"></a>

Therefore such people cannot cease to believe.

<a id="source-block-207"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-208"></a>

I do not see from what words of this saying the impossibility of apostasy in those who have once believed is inferred, etc. {Pa. 121.}

<a id="source-block-209"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-210"></a>

What use is a candle or spectacles if one will not see? For the apostle’s words are as clear as sunlight, and had Bertius’s eyes not been blinded by his passions, I think he could have seen with half an eye that the conclusion we have now put forward could be drawn from the words above. Other syllogisms could also be drawn from them. Among these, the one Bertius puts forward fits very well too: “Anyone who is kept in God’s power through faith unto salvation cannot cease to believe.” {Ibid. l. 10.}

For someone kept unto salvation is not kept unto damnation; and someone kept through faith unto salvation does not lose faith, nor can he. For no one is brought to salvation, nor kept unto it, through unbelief, but only through faith. If, then, someone kept unto salvation can cease to believe, someone kept unto salvation can also come to damnation, which is impossible. For how would it happen? Through man’s wilfulness? But man is not kept by his own strength, according to his own will, but in God’s power. Or would it happen through the devil’s power? But God, in whose power the believer is kept, is stronger than the devil. Thus in no way can it happen that someone whom God keeps unto salvation through faith should be destroyed and fall away from faith. {Inde cracht Godts wort de geloovige tot de saligheydt bewaert / ende over sulcks oock mede voor sulcken moetwil. Joan. 10. 29. Luc. 22. 32.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: In God’s power the believer is kept unto salvation, and consequently also from such wilfulness. Joan. 10. 29. Luc. 22. 32.</em></span>

<a id="source-block-211"></a>

Now all believers are kept in God’s power unto salvation through the faith they have received.

<a id="source-block-212"></a>

Therefore they cannot cease to believe either.

<a id="source-block-213"></a>

[The opening about Bertius and this syllogism is unclear.] Though, when properly considered, it is very powerful, I nevertheless disregard it. For it can happen, he says, that a believer abandons God’s keeping. This, I say, is what he says, and he says it simply, without adding a word of proof. Thus I too can content myself with simply denying his assertion, saying that it cannot happen that a believer abandons God’s keeping. I shall reserve the proof of my assertion until he has proved his. In any event, everyone knows that Bertius is not to be believed completely on his bare word in the matter of salvation. Let this too suffice here.

<a id="source-block-214"></a>

> 1. Joan. 3.9. Whoever is born of God commits no sin, for his seed abides in him, and he cannot sin because he is born of God. And 1. Joan. 5.18: Whoever is born of God keeps himself, and the evil one does not come near him. {19.}

<a id="source-block-215"></a>

From this we conclude as follows:

<a id="source-block-216"></a>

Someone who does not sin—indeed, cannot sin—but keeps himself so that the evil one does not even come near him cannot fall away from his faith. For in doing so he would sin.

<a id="source-block-217"></a>

No one who is born of God sins.

<a id="source-block-218"></a>

Therefore someone who is born of God cannot fall away from his faith.

<a id="source-block-219"></a>

{Pa. 135. li 37.} Or thus, as Bertius puts it, according to the apostle’s meaning, as he says:

<a id="source-block-220"></a>

Anyone in whom God’s seed abides commits no sin.

<a id="source-block-221"></a>

God’s seed abides in the one who is born of God.

<a id="source-block-222"></a>

Therefore the one who is born of God commits no sin.

<a id="source-block-223"></a>

And consequently someone who does not, or cannot, commit sin cannot be cast off by God either.

<a id="source-block-224"></a>

## Bertius’s Objection.

<a id="source-block-225"></a>

> In this manner of speaking, to commit sin means not to shun sin, but to commit it habitually and heedlessly as often as the occasion presents itself. Conversely, not to commit sin means to refrain from sin, to avoid and shun sin, to purify oneself, vers. 3., and to do righteousness, vers. 7. John thus indicates what the godly are accustomed to do, and what, in respect of their regeneration, they ought to do and can do by what has been granted them [the gift is not clearly named]. {Pa. 133. li. 23} {Joan. Inn. 36} But what if someone, returning to his former perverse ways [the wording here is unclear], were to cast out God’s seed and become a believer only for a time? This seed abides in him as long {Pa. 134. li 5.} {Joa. iii. 9.} as they nourish it; and as long as they nourish it, they do not sin; indeed, they shun sin. Thus these words must be taken as if one said: {Joa. iii. 23} He cannot sin if he acts according to his birth. A good tree cannot bear evil fruit—that is, if it remains good; but if it degenerates, it will bear evil fruits, etc.

<a id="source-block-226"></a>

## Answer.

<a id="source-block-227"></a>

Here again things are awry with Bertius: he knocks the apostle’s words over, contradicts his own words, and still it all amounts to nothing. First, I accept as agreed the explanation he gives of the expressions “to commit sin” and “not to commit sin”, and from that explanation conclude thus: the regenerate refrain from sin; they avoid sin; they do not commit sin habitually and heedlessly as often as the occasion presents itself—indeed, they cannot do so. They purify themselves; they do righteousness. Therefore the regenerate cannot, through the accumulation of many sins, come so far that through a general apostasy they cast off the Spirit of grace entirely. {Pag. 11. ll. 2.} Does this not run directly with Bertius’s whole work? [The wording may mean “against” rather than “with”.]

As for the apostle’s words, Bertius makes a long-winded discourse upon them, pag. 132. 133. 134. 135., of which we have read through the principal part. But they are only empty imaginings of his confused head, serving his advantage very well but not the explanation of Holy Scripture. For John certainly does not say, “The godly, or those born of God, ought not to sin”, or “can refrain from sinning”. Nor does he say, “God’s seed can abide in them”, or “They do not sin as long as God’s seed abides in them”. Rather, he says, “The one born of God commits no sin, because”—that is not “as long as”, but “because”—“God’s seed is and abides in him.” God’s seed is in the regenerate, says John; God’s seed abides in the regenerate; God’s seed is the cause of the regenerate not sinning—indeed, not being able to sin. And since this seed of God can be cast out only through sin, the regenerate cannot cast it out or become estranged from their birth, because this seed of God makes them not sin.

A good tree bears good fruits as long as there is good pith within it. But show me a tree in which such good pith will abide, and I shall boldly dare to say that this tree cannot degenerate. For being able to degenerate and always retaining good pith are incompatible things. So it is here too. In sum, John spoke these words simply and without qualification; Bertius will not accept them without qualification. That is how far the two differ from one another. {Pa. 134. ll. 23}

<a id="source-block-228"></a>

Thus far, then, we have spoken of those passages of Scripture which orthodox Reformed teachers generally adduce most aptly to prove the point already stated. Not that these are all of them, for many other passages, at least as bright and clear as those cited above, can be added. But since Bertius has contradicted only the aforesaid passages, whether because he disregarded the others or did not dare touch them, I too have wished to leave it at this: to put forward and explain only those same passages and answer Bertius’s objections and strange glosses.

I think, and hold it almost certain, that even if Bertius does not accept our reasons, he will nevertheless have to acknowledge that what we teach and believe concerning the perseverance of the saints is not fashioned from fantasies and dreams out of our own brain, but taken, on good grounds, from the prophetic Scripture. {Inde voorreden Pag. B 3. lin. 4.} <span class="marginal-translation"><em>Marginal note in English: In the preface, Pag. B 3. lin. 4.</em></span> Consequently he will not be so greatly astonished that there are people whose view of this perseverance differs from his.

<a id="source-block-229"></a>

Let us take the argument a step further. Bertius’s opinion conflicts with God’s Word, which is the infallible truth; ours agrees with it. His opinion also conflicts with the Netherlandish Confession and the Heidelberg Catechism. Although these writings were composed by people capable of error, they were nevertheless recognised and accepted long ago as summaries of Christian doctrine and truth. Thus the aforesaid Catechism is here in these United Provinces presented not only to children in the schools, but also publicly to the whole congregation in the churches, and is regularly explained there as well.

Bertius and his followers—whose position is […] (God grant that it never comes to that), and who would dare [or: would not dare] to cast the Catechism out of the church all at once and altogether, and thus refashion the whole Reformed religion on another model—would very much like to persuade the congregation that {Pag. 5. l. 19.} this point, concerning perseverance, has hitherto never been decided either in the confession of our churches or in the Heidelberg Catechism, and therefore that investigation into the truth of this same point ought to be left free.

But with God’s help I shall prove that Bertius deceives the simple, and that this book of his, and the doctrine contained in it, directly contradict the aforesaid writings, which speak of perseverance just as we do. While holding so important an office in the Reformed Church, Bertius ought not to have been permitted, without any compulsion, to show such public contempt for a doctrine, or a point of doctrine, that these same Reformed churches publicly accepted as true so long ago and have firmly maintained to this day, despite occasional resistance here and there from troublesome spirits.
