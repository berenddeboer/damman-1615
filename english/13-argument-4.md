# The fourth proof, from the proper fear of the saints

<!-- source-block:B002 -->

> Anyone whom fear of rejection keeps at his duty can fall from his state. Out of fear of rejection the saints keep themselves and others at their duty. Therefore the saints can fall from their standing. {Pag. 33. l. 6}

<!-- source-block:B003 -->

## Answer

<!-- source-block:B004 -->

Scripture calls two groups saints: those who are only in the covenant of grace and those who are in grace itself. Both are said to stand: the latter in grace, the former in the covenant of grace, as we have already proved. {Rom.5.2.en cap.11.20.} {1.Corin.15.1} {1.Petr.5.12}

We must also distinguish the kinds of grace in which these saints stand. One grace is common to all saints: forgiveness of sins through faith, and newness of life through the sanctifying Spirit. Another is given to individual saints in different measure: one has greater and stronger faith than another, and one is further advanced in holiness than another. {Phil.3.15.} I speak here of newborn infants, {1 Pet.2.2} children, young men, fathers, {1.Joan.2.12. 13.14.} the mature and the immature. Besides this, God gives one person a grace he does not give another, such as prophecy, teaching, ministry and the like. {Rom.12.6/7} {1.Cor.1.18.}

With these distinctions in mind, I grant Bertius his syllogism in the following sense. Those in the covenant of grace can fall out of that covenant. Those who once had the Holy Spirit’s full assurance of the forgiveness of their sins and of God’s kindness towards them in Christ can fall from that state: when they grieve the Holy Spirit by their many sins, they may for a time no longer feel that full assurance and consolation in their hearts. Those who ran zealously in the way of godliness can lose their zeal and stop running, and so fall from their former standing. And those to whom God has given not only true saving faith in Christ but also the grace to serve as teachers and ministers of his congregation can fall from that particular state and become unfit for the work of ministry.

All this we grant Bertius. But none of it touches our disagreement: can anyone fall from the grace of being a child of God? If this is the state Bertius means when he says that the saints can fall from their standing, I deny his second premise. The saints do not fear that they will be rejected from that state, for they know

<!-- source-block:B005 -->

> whom they have believed, and they are assured that he is able to keep what they have with him until that day. {2.Tim 1.12.}

<!-- source-block:B006 -->

> But then the fear of the saints is in vain, says Bertius, and the admonitions they give one another are useless. {Pag.33.l.8.}

<!-- source-block:B007 -->

Answer. Why should that follow? Can one not fear for some reason other than to avoid rejection? People fear water, fire, knives, falls and the like. Why? Only because these things might cost them their lives? A child fears his father, a wife her husband, a servant his master, a subject his ruler. Why? Is it only for fear of losing a father’s inheritance, life together with a husband, or life itself? That is absurd.

What, then, do they fear? For one thing, some people have reason to fear that they are not what they think they are. Many grievously deceive themselves: they imagine themselves God’s children when they are not, and confidently claim salvation for themselves {Joan9.41. Apoc.3.17. Ro.2.13.17. Matt.3.9.} although they have not the least share in it. So it was with the Corinthians, who, says Bertius, {pag.32.§.3.}

<!-- source-block:B008 -->

> because they had a covenant confirmed with Christ’s blood, various gifts of the Holy Spirit, and the sacraments or seals of the covenant, lived without a care, as though they already had their sheep on dry land, as the saying goes, and were beyond all danger. Hence the apostle rebuked them in these weighty words: Whoever thinks he stands, let him take care not to fall. {1.Cor.10.12.}

<!-- source-block:B009 -->

Secondly, there are those who now know for certain that they belong to Christ. They know this through the infallible testimony of the Holy Spirit, who works powerfully both within them and outwardly through them. {Ro.8.14.16. Gal.5.24.} They must fear to sin, lest they show ingratitude to God their generous Father, cause their neighbour to stumble, grieve the Holy Spirit, disturb their own peace of mind, or bring … and rods upon themselves. {1.Joan.3.21 Mat.26.75 2Cor.7.10. 2.Sa.12.14.} [The word paired with ‘rods’ is unclear.] Do you think these are not reasons enough for fear? Will you say that such fear can keep no one at his duty, and that people will still live carelessly if they know they cannot fall away from salvation?

My first answer is that no such godless carelessness can arise in God’s truly regenerate children. No dutiful child, honourable wife or loyal subject gives up living in fear simply because they know that they are not about to lose their inheritance at the hands of their father, their marriage at the hands of their husband, or their life at the hands of their ruler. God’s children have been taught better. {Ephes4.21} Did Hezekiah stop eating and drinking because he knew his life had been extended by fifteen years? {Esa.38.5.} Did Paul stop taking steps to keep everyone aboard ship together because he knew that none of them would perish? {Actor.27.34}

Secondly, although God’s children cannot lose eternal life, they can still lose some of the glory they would have enjoyed in eternal life had they not neglected God’s gifts to them. About this, too, they may rightly be concerned and afraid, for everyone is eager to better his condition.

<!-- source-block:B010 -->

> The sun has one glory, the moon another glory, and the stars another glory. {1.Cor.15.14}

<!-- source-block:B011 -->

{Daniel12.3} Paul will shine differently from the murderer who repented. Those who have done much good will shine more brightly than those who have done little or no good and repent at the end of their lives. For God crowns his gifts with glory: the more gifts, the greater the glory; the fewer gifts, the less the glory. All who believe in Jesus Christ, then, will have eternal life. Those who besides faith have received but few gifts from God will shine like stars in eternal life [the wording that relates faith to these few gifts is unclear]; those who have received many gifts will shine like the sun and moon. Those who have received much and neglected much but have nevertheless kept the faith will lose as much of their glory as they would have enjoyed had they done their duty better. In short, God’s holy, regenerate children have reason enough and more than enough to fear and tremble, though they need not fear losing their inheritance. But let us hear Bertius’s proof. He cites the following passages.

<!-- source-block:B012 -->

First, 1.Corinth.9.27. {pag.31.l.14.} Answer. Paul was both a Christian and a teacher of Christians. What this chapter says he did, he did not as a Christian but as a teacher of Christians, as anyone who reads the chapter carefully can see. Among the things the apostle did as a teacher of Christians was this:

<!-- source-block:B013 -->

> he brings his body into subjection and makes it serve, so that he who preaches to others may not himself become unfit in any way.

<!-- source-block:B014 -->

That is, he takes every care not to be found guilty of the very sins he preaches against when he tells others not to steal, commit fornication or do anything of the kind. {Rom.2.21.} Had he been found guilty, Paul would have been unfit to build others up any longer by his preaching. {1.Tim.3.7.}

<!-- source-block:B015 -->

> A teacher must be blameless; he must be an example to the flock. {1.Tim.3.2. 1.Petr.5.3. 1.Tim.4.12.}

<!-- source-block:B016 -->

But suppose Paul had become unfit for the office of preacher, or at least unable to build others up by preaching. Would God therefore have rejected him altogether? By no means. A public sin could have made Paul unfit in human eyes, but it does not cause God to reject a person. A man unfit to be a shepherd may still be accepted as a good sheep. {dit leert Bertius pag.16 lin.2.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M026">Marginal translation: Bertius teaches this, p. 16, line 2.</span> Since Paul well knew that he could sin and so become unfit for the office so solemnly entrusted to him, he brought his body into subjection and made it serve. Chrysostom, to whom Bertius appeals, {pag.10 §.1.23} explains the words as follows: {Ibid.lin.28.}

<!-- source-block:B017 -->

> If it is not enough for my salvation to have preached, taught, and won countless people, unless my works too are such that nothing can be said against them, much less will the things related above be enough for you.

<!-- source-block:B018 -->

But what then? Can it be that those whose works are open to criticism cannot be saved? God's word does not teach that, nor is that what Chrysostom means to teach. He speaks of Paul the apostle, not as an ordinary Christian but as a teacher of Christians. It could not be enough for Paul to conduct himself as an ordinary Christian unless he also conducted himself as a Christian teacher, blameless in his works. That is why Paul took such care. But did he also fear that God would exclude him from eternal salvation? Paul himself says no. {2.Tim.1.12.} So this saying does not prove the point at issue.

What Bertius brings forward here from Ignatius {pag.104 l.32} is entirely beside the point. Must we take the words of Holy Scripture in the sense in which Ignatius used them? What reason is there for that? And what evidence is there that Ignatius feared he would be condemned unless he became a martyr? I think Bertius is too ready to pass judgement here. The early Christians, Ignatius among them, were indeed eager for the honour and crown of martyrdom. But those who thought they could not be saved unless they became martyrs were regarded as heretics and called Circumcelliones. {Siet August. de Hæres.69. en lib.3.con. Cresconium Gram c.48.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M031">Marginal translation: See Augustine, On Heresies 69, and book 3 against Cresconius the grammarian, chapter 48.</span> Ignatius feared only that he might be hindered and shut out, not from salvation but from the honour of martyrdom. This is especially clear from his writing to the Ephesians and the Romans.

<!-- source-block:B019 -->

Second, Mat. 5.13. {2. pag.31.l.17.} Answer. We have already shown that these words cannot be applied to this matter in any way. See the answer to the fourth Scripture passage in Bertius's first argument.

<!-- source-block:B020 -->

Third, Joan. 15.5. {3. Ibid.lin.20} Answer. Rightly understood, as we have explained them, these words cannot help Bertius either. See the answer to his objection to our fifth argument, by which we proved the perseverance of the saints.

<!-- source-block:B021 -->

Fourth, Philip. 2.12. {4. Ibid.lin.23.} Answer. The apostle teaches only that believers must fear, and we acknowledge that to be true. But what must believers fear?

<!-- source-block:B022 -->

> They must fear, tremble and shudder before God's holy name, for such people alone seek his grace, and because they seek it, they obtain it.

<!-- source-block:B023 -->

These are Bertius's own words. {pag.109.l.8} Does that mean believers must fear being rejected from salvation? Or what does Bertius hope to prove against us with these words of Paul?

<!-- source-block:B024 -->

Fifth, 2. Timoth. 4.7. {5. pag.31.li.29} Answer. Here Bertius contradicts himself. Believers must fear rejection as long as they live, he says. Yet Paul was sure of his own outcome, Bertius also says. {ibid.lin.25.} How can the two agree? The apostle did speak these words near the end of his life. But to be near the end is not to be at the end. Even then he had not sailed past all the dangerous rocks. The worst still lay ahead: the cruel and shameful death he expected at the hands of Nero the tyrant. Yet Paul was sure of his outcome, says Bertius. He was sure that he would overcome all the suffering, all the shame, even the terror of the death still to be inflicted on him, and hold fast to the faith.

Must all those believers and saints who do not even face such hardship nevertheless live in constant fear of rejection? Before their end, even when they are near it, may they never be sure of their outcome? And if they may be sure near their end, why not at all times? Anything that has befallen them in the middle of life can plainly befall them near its end. Paul's example thus tells directly in our favour and against Bertius.

<!-- source-block:B025 -->

Sixth, 1. Corinth. 10. 1. etc. {6. pag.72.l.23. ibid.lin.3.} Answer. The apostle addresses this admonition and rebuke to the Corinthians, who, says Bertius,

<!-- source-block:B026 -->

> because they had a covenant confirmed with Christ's blood, and various gifts of the Holy Spirit, and the sacraments or seals of the covenant, lived without care.

<!-- source-block:B027 -->

But what follows from this? The apostle rebuked such carelessness and taught the Corinthians not to rely wrongly on the grace they had received, as though the covenant they were in had the power to bind God to them forever, even if they showed no obedience. Does it follow that all God's holy, regenerate children must fear being rejected from salvation, or that if they do not fear it they become careless? Let Bertius first prove that all who stand are regenerate, and that all who fall fall away from salvation. {vers.12.} I leave aside for now those who *think* they stand. It is of them that the apostle actually speaks here, and that is quite different from speaking of those who truly stand. As for Bertius's claim {pag.107.l.21} that

<!-- source-block:B028 -->

> the expression ‘to think one stands’ is used both of those who have received some benefit from God and of those who have not,

<!-- source-block:B029 -->

that may well be true. But how does Bertius prove that here it is used of people who have truly received an eternal benefit from God—people, I mean, who have received the sanctifying Spirit of Jesus Christ?

<!-- source-block:B030 -->

Our question concerns this passage of Scripture, not what the expression means elsewhere. Chrysostom, to whom Bertius appeals, {Pag.108.l.3} says only that Paul and the Corinthians could fall. Does that mean they could be condemned? Bertius goes on to enlist Marloratus, Calvin and Beza {pag.94.l.16} {pa.108.l.5.9} as though they agreed with him. In this he does not deal honestly. Not only do they openly contradict him throughout their books, but they expressly rebuke him and his teaching in the very passage he cites. For after Marloratus had pointed out, drawing on Brentius [the parenthesis here may mean “I think”],

<!-- source-block:B031 -->

> that this conclusion warns against the fault of carelessness, etc.,

<!-- source-block:B032 -->

as Bertius reports it, Marloratus adds these words from Calvin:

<!-- source-block:B033 -->

> He (the apostle) does not admonish or command them (the Corinthians) to doubt God's will with anxious hearts, or to tremble through uncertainty about their salvation, as the Papists dream, etc. {Siet Cal.op 1.Cor.10.12.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M044">Marginal translation: See Calvin on 1.Cor.10.12.</span>

<!-- source-block:B034 -->

And Beza writes on these same words of the apostle:

<!-- source-block:B035 -->

> Here again, he says, ‘thinking one stands’ means that vain and inflated confidence which rests not on God's promises but on a foolish opinion. For otherwise, only someone who rests with assurance on God's pledges, following Paul's example, Roman. 8. 38., is a believer. And this assurance causes no slackness, but produces a godly concern: namely, that godly fear and trembling by which perseverance in faith is nourished, etc.

<!-- source-block:B036 -->

It was certainly well worth the trouble to look into Calvin's and Beza's view, even if only to have a sample of Bertius's remarkable sincerity. And yet this man is still so shameless that, if one cannot understand Scripture as he wishes, he cries out:

<!-- source-block:B037 -->

> Is that searching Scripture with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth? {pa.107.li.9}

<!-- source-block:B038 -->

Seventh, Rom. 11. 19. {7. pa.32.li.37à} Answer. The apostle speaks of the Jews and the Gentiles as whole peoples, not of particular individuals among them. He says that the Jewish people stood in the covenant of grace but were broken off through unbelief, that is, cast out of the covenant. Of the Gentiles he says that they stand in that same covenant. He warns them to take greater care and to show greater faith and obedience towards God than the Jews did, lest they too be cast out of the covenant of grace.

That this is his meaning is clear: not all the Jews had been cast out of grace, {Ro.11.2.etc} although as a people they had been cast out of the covenant of grace made particularly with them. {siet Bertii pa.20.li.37. ende pag.21. gheheel.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M048">Marginal translation: See Bertius, p. 20, line 37, and the whole of p. 21.</span> Nor did all the Gentiles stand in grace, although as a people they had been received into the covenant of grace from which they were formerly estranged. {Ephes.2.12. 1.Petr.2.10} So this passage too is beside the point. Our dispute concerns those who stand in grace, not those who are merely in the covenant of grace. Those who stand in the covenant have reason to fear that they may be cast out of it, as the apostle teaches here. But those who stand in grace have no reason to fear being cast out of grace; at any rate the apostle teaches no such thing here. Bertius says: {pa.104.li.32}

<!-- source-block:B039 -->

> If God does not spare an apostate people, but breaks them off, how much less will he spare a single apostate?

<!-- source-block:B040 -->

That is quite true if the apostasy is of the same kind. If God punishes a whole people that falls away from the covenant, how much more will he punish an individual who falls away from the covenant. But that is not our question. Bertius would have to prove that the Jews were all in grace, or that those who were in grace were broken off. He would also have to prove that the Gentiles who had been received not only into the covenant of grace but into grace itself were warned to fear that they might once again … out of grace. [The verb is unclear.] If that is proved, I will grant that if God rejected a whole people from grace for their unbelief, he would surely not reject a single individual. [The wording is disputed: the other reading says that God would *all the more* reject or deprive the individual.] Otherwise it is child's play to say:

<!-- source-block:B041 -->

> If God casts a whole people out of the covenant of grace, all the more will he cast a single person out of grace.

<!-- source-block:B042 -->

The difference between the people being compared is too great. One true believer who stands in grace is a thousand times more certain of his salvation—even supposing some reasonable ground for doubt, when there is none—than ten thousand people who stand outside grace and are only in the covenant of grace. {Pa.104.l.10} Why, then, would Bertius …? [The verb is missing; the question appears to ask why Bertius stands aloof from this.]

<!-- source-block:B043 -->

Eighth, Ebr. 4. 1. {8. Pa.32.li.ultima.} Answer. Where Bertius writes ‘Let us then fear lest any of us’, the Greek text has ‘Let us then fear lest any of you’. A notable difference. {Ibid.lin.34} According to Bertius's wording, the apostle was afraid for himself. But according to the apostle's wording, the apostle was afraid only for the Hebrews, admonishing them also to be afraid for themselves. But what sort of people were these Hebrews? True believers? Bertius says yes. {Ibid. ll.35.} But since he merely says it without proving it, I do not particularly value his assertion. On the contrary, I say that those for whom the apostle is afraid here were not believers. And I prove this, first, from the fact that the apostle speaks here only of forsaking the promise of entering his rest, teaching that one must indeed fear for the Hebrews lest they forsake the promise made to them, but not lest they forsake the faith by which they had accepted these promises. Secondly, when he says

<!-- source-block:B044 -->

> the word they heard preached did not help them, since it was not mixed with faith,

<!-- source-block:B045 -->

what else can he mean but that the Hebrews ought to take care not only to hear the holy Gospel but also to believe it? They were to do so lest, merely hearing it without believing, they be deceived in the end by claiming salvation on the strength of promises that could not benefit them without true faith. I conclude, then, that these Hebrews did not yet believe.
