Now, to come to the Catechism, the first question is answered as follows. {14}

<a id="source-block-001"></a>

> That the Lord Christ so preserves me that, without the will of my heavenly Father, not a hair can fall from my head; indeed, that all things must serve my salvation. Therefore he also assures me of eternal life through his Holy Spirit and makes me heartily willing and ready to live for him from now on.

<a id="source-block-002"></a>

What could be said more clearly against Bertius’s claim? Yet Bertius has not seen this, or has not wanted to see it.

<a id="source-block-003"></a>

On question 28. {2}

<a id="source-block-004"></a>

> We know for certain that no creature will separate us from God’s love, since all creatures are so held in his hand that they can neither stir nor move against his will.

<a id="source-block-005"></a>

### Bertius’s objection.

<a id="source-block-006"></a>

> This is to be understood only of external things. For if it {Pa. 79. l. 24.} were also to be understood of sin, then sin would have to be a creature. That would be to join the Marcionites and Manichaeans in making God a cause of evil. {pa. 79. l. 13.}

<a id="source-block-007"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="source-block-008"></a>

By creatures, the Catechism means all external things, including the Devil, as is clear from the references in the margin. For Job 1.12. is cited there: “The Lord said to Satan, behold, let everything he has be in your hand, etc.” And chapter 2.6.: “The Lord said to Satan, behold, let him be in your hand, etc.” Now it is certainly the case that Satan cannot, strictly speaking, separate us from God’s love by any external things, such as wealth, poverty, persecutions and the like; nor does he seek to do so. Rather, he seeks through such things to bring us into sin, and through sin to separate us from God. “What will you wager,” says Satan to God, “that he will bless you to your face—that is, curse you?” {Job 1.11. en cap. 2.5.} And it is sin alone that separates us, and can separate us, from God, as God teaches {Esa. 59.2.} and Bertius too acknowledges. {pag. 79. l. 23} This does not mean that all sins actually separate us from God our Lord, as Bertius too expressly teaches. {pag 16. l. 10.} Rather, all sins, by their very ugliness, deserve to have God withdraw his love from us, as he justly might. More on this in due course. This, then, remains firmly established: the Catechism teaches that the Devil cannot separate believers from God’s love.

<a id="source-block-009"></a>

On question 53. {3.}

<a id="source-block-010"></a>

> I believe that the Holy Spirit has been given to me so that, through a sincere faith, he may make me a partaker of Christ and all his benefits, comfort me, and remain with me for ever.

<a id="source-block-011"></a>

### Bertius’s objection.

<a id="source-block-012"></a>

> This question sets out the purpose of God’s gifts and why the Holy Spirit is given to believers. But the question is this: can we not depart from that same purpose through unbelief? {pa. 120. l. 18.}

<a id="source-block-013"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="source-block-014"></a>

1. Bertius’s answer does nothing to help his case and is therefore beside the point. We acknowledge with Bertius that this matter sets out the purpose of God’s gifts and why the Holy Spirit is given to believers: namely, to make them partakers of Christ and all his benefits, to comfort them, and to remain with them, not for a time, but for ever; and thus also to bring them to salvation. For it is certain that salvation is not the least of the benefits that the Lord Christ has merited and obtained for us through his suffering.

2. But Bertius’s question is much more absurd still. He ought instead to have asked whether the Holy Spirit would leave his duty undone and neglect it, or be prevented from doing in believers that for which he has been given to them. For in the Catechism’s words quoted above, no mention is made of our duty, but only of the Holy Spirit’s duty. We are taught that we must believe that the Holy Spirit has been given to believers not merely to make it possible for them to believe and to become partakers of Christ and all his benefits—for then one might say that they could also fail to believe and fail to partake of Christ’s benefits—but that he has been given to them to make them actually believe and actually become partakers of Christ’s benefits, that is, the forgiveness of sins and eternal life. Since this, then, is God’s intention and aim in giving his Holy Spirit, no one can hinder him from attaining his aim.

<a id="source-block-015"></a>

On question 54. {4.}

<a id="source-block-016"></a>

> I believe that I am a living member of the holy universal Christian Church, and shall remain so for ever.

<a id="source-block-017"></a>

### Bertius’s objection.

<a id="source-block-018"></a>

> These words must be understood as referring only to the duty of a believer, for they teach what he is bound to promise and to do. But cannot someone who has faith lose it? And could not someone who has promised this depart and leave his promise unfulfilled? {pag. 5. l 3.} {pa. 120. l 24.}

<a id="source-block-019"></a>

### Answer.

<a id="source-block-020"></a>

Truly, that is great audacity and shamelessness! Read and reread question 54 above, with its answer, and see whether you can find a single word or letter there that speaks of promising. It speaks of faith, not of promising. It teaches that every believer must believe for certain—not promise—that he is a member of the Church. That is, he must believe that he is counted among the elect and will remain among them for ever. Now, if a believer can abandon his faith and depart, it can happen that he does not remain a living member of the Church either, and consequently that his faith becomes false. For someone who has believed that he will remain a member of the Church for ever has certainly believed falsely if he was not always a member. But the Catechism teaches no one to believe what is false, or what can become false. Nor does it teach anyone to believe for certain what is uncertain. It necessarily follows that a believer—at least according to the teaching of our Catechism—cannot renounce his faith, nor ever depart without returning. Bertius may well consider whether his conscience is sufficiently appeased by the tricks he uses here, so that he dares to say without blushing that he teaches nothing contrary to the Catechism.

I have long known that people have sought to accept the Catechism, or to have it accepted, only as far as its words are concerned, leaving everyone to understand and interpret those same words as he thought best for God’s honour and the common edification. But I have not yet seen or heard anyone who, beyond that, also took it upon himself to put one word in place of another: “promise” for “believe”, and then again “believe” for “promise”. Thus Bertius far surpasses all his associates in perversity. That certainly brings him little honour, and he injures and harms no one more than himself. If this is the freedom of which Bertius speaks so much, and which he prizes so highly, then anyone who can see a little beyond the end of his nose can easily perceive that no small mysteries or hidden things indeed lurk beneath this new reformation of the Reformation. Let that be said to you.
