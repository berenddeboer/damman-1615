<!-- source-block:B001 -->

Let us now proceed to the fifth proof.

<!-- source-block:B002 -->

# The fifth proof: causes that bring about apostasy in themselves or incidentally

<!-- source-block:B003 -->

> Whoever can sin against his conscience, become sluggish, despise the opportunities for grace offered to him, recoil from persecution, {P.38.ll.32.} be led astray by the lure of pleasure, choke the word through love of riches, give credence to deception, burn with envy and contempt towards the brethren, and finally neglect repentance, harden his heart and resist the Holy Spirit, can indeed abandon his former righteousness.

<!-- source-block:B004 -->

> Now someone who is righteous can do all this.

<!-- source-block:B005 -->

> Can the righteous person therefore also abandon his former righteousness?

<!-- source-block:B006 -->

## Answer

<!-- source-block:B007 -->

The second part of this syllogism is false. It is true that the righteous can do some of the things mentioned above, and do them for a time. But I deny that God’s Word says they can do them all. Let us hear Bertius’s further proof.

<!-- source-block:B008 -->

First: 1. Timoth. 1. 19. {1. Pa.33.li.5} Answer. What purpose does this serve? To prove the first part of the principal syllogism? That will not do, for not all those who suffer shipwreck in faith were righteous before. Consequently one cannot say either that all who suffer shipwreck in faith abandon their former righteousness. Does it serve, then, to prove the second part? That was unnecessary. For who, unless he sides with the Perfectionists, denies that even the most godly sin daily against their conscience—not deliberately and out of wilful wickedness, but nevertheless doing what they know in conscience, and are convinced, is evil? Yet, to tell the truth, such sins of the righteous could scarcely be called sins against conscience, at least in the sense in which Bertius takes it.

But suppose the righteous committed the sins of which Bertius speaks, and rejected a good conscience as Hymenæus did: would they thereby fall away from God altogether? Would they run on from one sin to another, and thus remain entirely without a good conscience? The Apostle does not teach that here; and St John expressly denies it, in 1. Joan. 3.9., according to Bertius’s own interpretation. {Bertius pa:137.l20. citeert hem ooc pa.23.li.23. ende28.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m003">[Marginal translation: Bertius, p. 137, line 20, cites him also on p. 23, lines 23 and 28.]</span> See also what we wrote above to explain these words of 1. Timoth. 1. 19., as also 1. Joan. 3 9.

As for what Bertius further adduces here from Rom. 1. 21., {Pa.33.li.24.} that has nothing whatever to do with the matter either. For there Paul is not discussing those who have been converted to Christ, as Bertius himself says. Why, then, are those words cited here? Because the truth of this saying also holds for those who are already converted. {Idm lin.23;} But why?

<!-- source-block:B009 -->

> For our duty bears the same relation to the grace that followed as it once bore to the first grace.

<!-- source-block:B010 -->

Here we have a masterly piece of work! Look at what he had to prove: that the righteous would sin deliberately against conscience. What he proves is that the unrighteous can do so. And to make it look better, it is merely added that what is said in this respect of the unrighteous must also be understood of the righteous. {Rom.1.21. en cap.2.14.} Bertius seems neither to recognise nor to draw any distinction between the remnants of God’s image in the natural man and the renewed, restored image of God in the regenerate. For our question is not whether both can do what they know to be evil and leave undone what they know to be good, but whether the regenerate can sin against conscience just as well and just as completely, and continue and remain stuck in that same sin, as the unregenerate. For Bertius’s argument must be framed in one of these two ways:

<!-- source-block:B011 -->

First:

<!-- source-block:B012 -->

> Whoever sins once against his conscience abandons his former righteousness completely.

<!-- source-block:B013 -->

> Some saints have on occasion sinned once against their conscience.

<!-- source-block:B014 -->

> Therefore some saints have abandoned their righteousness completely.

<!-- source-block:B015 -->

Here Bertius himself acknowledges {pag.23.l.28.} that the first part is false, and we acknowledge that the second is indeed true. For David, Peter and others sinned against their conscience and yet did not abandon their righteousness completely.

<!-- source-block:B016 -->

Or again:

<!-- source-block:B017 -->

> Whoever can sin against his conscience continually and to the very end (in the sense Bertius intends) can abandon his former righteousness completely. 

<!-- source-block:B018 -->

> Some saints can sin in this way continually and to the very end.

<!-- source-block:B019 -->

> Therefore some saints can abandon their righteousness completely.

<!-- source-block:B020 -->

Here the first part is true and the second false; and it is precisely the second that is at issue between us. If Bertius undertakes to prove this second part from the words he cites from Paul, he must necessarily concede that dead people and living people, darkness and light, whoever is in Christ and whoever is outside Christ, the regenerate and the unregenerate, nature and grace, have the same power and operation. {Eph.2,5.12. en cap.5,8. en ca4.19.20 1.Cor.2.14. Ho.8.5.&c,} That is all too monstrous.

<!-- source-block:B021 -->

A person is not born again in Christ on the condition on which the first man Adam was created in God’s image, namely that by a single transgression he should be deprived of the grace received. Therefore we believe that a regenerate person, even when sinning against his conscience, still retains so much grace, and still receives so much grace from God—for the Spirit inwardly, and God outwardly through admonitions, threats, fatherly chastisements and other means, call him to repentance and assure him of forgiveness, etc.—that he can rise again, indeed that he cannot but rise again. If Bertius had been able to prove clearly and certainly from God’s Word that this was untrue, he would not have needed to resort to such trifling devices.

<!-- source-block:B022 -->

Second: Matth. 13. 12. and 19., and chapter 12.43. {2. Pa.33.li.24.} Answer. Does this show that the saints, the regenerate, can become so sluggish that they can abandon their righteousness completely without awakening again? I cannot see it. For ‘whoever does not have’, and likewise ‘whoever hears the word and does not understand’: how can these be called regenerate? Hear Christ himself explaining his words:

<!-- source-block:B023 -->

> And his disciples came to him {Matth.13.10} and said to him: Why do you speak to them in a parable? He answered and said to them: {11.} Because it has been given to you to understand the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but it has not been given to these. For whoever has will be given more and will have abundance; {12.} but whoever does not have will be deprived even of what he has. Therefore I speak to them through parables, {13.} so that seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not hear, nor understand either, etc.

<!-- source-block:B024 -->

Who does not see that Christ expressly says that the disciples have and that the crowd does not have? {Vers.18. etc.} And this crowd was certainly not regenerate. But the disciples who had—could they also abandon what they had? Christ says: More will be given to you, and you will have abundance. For this reason he also speaks to them without a parable. He goes on:

<!-- source-block:B025 -->

> And he who is sown in the good soil {Vers.23.} is the one who hears that word and understands, and in the end bears fruit: one a hundred, another sixty, and another thirty.

<!-- source-block:B026 -->

Whoever hears the word and understands, says Christ, is the good soil. Therefore whoever hears the word and does not understand is not good soil. {Vers.19.} Is that same person nevertheless a regenerate Christian? Then people must be born again otherwise than through God’s Word, {1.Petr.1.23. Iacob.1.18.} or at least they must be born again through mere hearing, without faith. Both are false. What is adduced from Matth. 12.43. will be more suitably discussed in the answer to the second argument of Bertius’s seventh proof.

<!-- source-block:B027 -->

Third: Lucæ 13.24. 2.Corinth.6.1. Ephes. 3.12. Joan. 12.35. {3. Pa.34.li.6.} Answer. These passages are meant to show that the saints can always neglect every offered opportunity of grace. That they do not always accept all the grace offered the moment it is offered, we grant [the wording here is unclear]. Bertius, in turn, must acknowledge that in doing so one does not immediately abandon all one’s former righteousness, lose it and remain without it. But the passages are beside the point. For in Luke no saints are spoken of, unless one calls saints those who ate and drank in Christ’s presence and in whose streets Christ taught, {Vers26.} although they were workers of unrighteousness. {Vers27.} In John, too, no saints are spoken of. For when Christ says, ‘Believe in the light, so that you may become children of the light’, {Vers.36.} he indicates that these people did not yet believe and were not yet children of the light: therefore not saints or regenerate people either.

Writing to the Corinthians, Paul calls ‘the grace of God’ the proclamation of the reconciliation brought about between God and man through Christ’s death. {2.Cor.5.19.} This is clear from the immediately preceding chapter, as also from the words adduced themselves. Those who do not now let themselves be reconciled to God have received this grace of God in vain. {2.Cor.5.20.} Such people do not as yet believe in Christ: for, being justified by faith, we have peace with God {Rom.5.1.} and are reconciled in fact. Just as there were undoubtedly still many at Corinth who did not believe in Christ, although they heard the Gospel preached daily, so such people are also found among us today. And it is properly to such people that this admonition applies.

A good teacher must know how to divide God’s holy Word rightly, {2.Tim.2 15.} so that each may have the word set before him [the precise wording is unclear]. He must set the Law before the impenitent and the Gospel before the contrite; milk before children and solid food before the mature. {Heb.4.13.14} He must teach, reprove, correct, instruct and comfort; {2.Tim.3.16.} and everyone must take for his benefit what he needs most. {Rom15.4. 1.Petr.2.2.} The Apostle’s words to the Ephesians {Gal.1.23.} were discussed in the answer to the third scriptural passage in Bertius’s first proof: one may look there.

<!-- source-block:B028 -->

Fourth: Mat. 13.20. 1. Tim.4.10. and 16. {4. Pa.35.li.25.} Answer. It is certain that believers would recoil from persecution; but we deny that such fear would overcome believers to the point of deserting God altogether. For ‘in all these things we overcome through him who loved us’, {Roma8.37.} says the Apostle; all the more so since God moderates these things according to the strength he has graciously given us, as the same Apostle testifies and Peter also acknowledges. {1.Cor.10 13. pag.6.li.30}

What is now cited against this from Matthew is wholly beside the point. For stony soil is not good soil; and those who hear the word and receive it immediately with joy, and no longer have any root in themselves, but are unstable—would Bertius dare say that they were true believers, founded on the immovable rock Jesus Christ? {Matt.7.24. in cap.10.18} We do not call those who accept truth as truth ‘true believers’ (that is Bertius’s opinion), {[onleesbare verwijzing]} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m034">[Marginal translation: Only “pag.58 li.” can be made out.]</span> but those who have obtained forgiveness of sins through Christ. For it is certain that devils and impenitent people can indeed believe that Christ forgives the penitent their sins, which is certainly believing truth; but would they therefore either themselves obtain forgiveness of sins, or be entitled to be called true believers?

Paul does not confirm Bertius’s assertion either. {Cypr.317?} Demas and all the others mentioned there have deserted him, he says. But have they all deserted God, Christ, their former righteousness too? First it would have to be shown that all those who confessed him with their mouths had had Christ dwelling in their hearts through faith; and that, in failing now to confess Christ out of love, they had wholly denied and rejected him. Indeed Elijah knew no one who confessed God, as he says; yet God still knew fully seven thousand—a great multitude—who had not denied him. {1.re.19.14.18}

<!-- source-block:B029 -->

Fifth: 2 Pet.2.15.Hos 4.11. Spr.19.2.Luc.21.34. {5. Pag.34.li.34} Answer. I acknowledge that wine and women can make the wise foolish, as Hosea and Sirach teach; also that the hearts of even the most godly can be weighed down by gluttony, drunkenness and the cares of this life, as Christ indicates. But that the saints could be overcome so as to serve pleasures, {2.Petr.2.19.} I deny. For:

<!-- source-block:B030 -->

> Everything born of God overcomes the world, that is, the desire of the eyes, the desire of the flesh, and the pride of life. {1.Joan.5.4. en cap.2.16.}

<!-- source-block:B031 -->

As for what is objected from Peter, it is frivolous. For by those who had truly escaped those who live in error, and also by those who had escaped the world’s defilements through the knowledge of Christ, {2.pe.2.18.20} Peter does not mean truly regenerate people, as was proved in the answer to the eighth scriptural passage of Bertius’s second proof. We shall consider more closely the example of Solomon and Absalom {Pag.35.li.2.} cited here if Bertius proves that they were both regenerate.

<!-- source-block:B032 -->

Sixth: Matth. 13.22. 1.Timot.6.10. {6. Ibid.lin.3} Answer. Although the regenerate sometimes let untimely worldly care hinder them in their prayers and other works of love towards God and their neighbour, nevertheless they are never so greatly and wholly taken up with such care as to suffocate and stifle God’s Word and faith in Christ. For the regenerate do not live in avarice, {Coloss.3.5.7} and those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires. {Galat.5.24.}

It is true that many who hear the word choke it within themselves through the deceitfulness of riches, as Christ teaches. Some, too, in their desire for money, have strayed from faith (that is, from the doctrine of faith which they had outwardly accepted and confessed), as Paul teaches. But what does that have to do with our dispute? Or are all who hear God’s Word to be regarded as regenerate, even supposing that Christ compares them to bad soil? {Matt.13. 22.23. Ebreor6.8.} Or should at least all those who have some faith, confess it and preach it be called regenerate? It is for Bertius to prove that.

And what is the traitor Judas doing here too? {Pag.35.l.14} ‘It seems that he believed at first’, says Bertius. How so? Because, leaving all his possessions, he was enrolled among the Apostles. Matth. 10. But from the dignity of an office one neither can nor may judge the dignity of the person who holds that office. And if Judas, being then a devil, {Ioan.6.70} was allowed to exercise the apostolic office, why could he not be called to the apostolic office while being a devil?

‘The Lord’, Bertius says further, ‘also prayed for Judas as well as for the others’, Luc. 6.12. The Evangelist says simply that the Lord prayed. From this Bertius concludes: {Luc.6.13.} therefore he prayed for others, and among others also for the Apostles, who had not yet been chosen at that time, and among the Apostles also for Judas. Therefore Judas was a believer. [The wording of this sarcastic question is unclear.] Christ prays for those who crucify him. Were they all believers too, then? {Luc.23.34.} He prays for those who would believe through the Apostles’ preaching. {Ioan.17.20} Were they believers too, then, before they believed? Then it could also very well be that Judas was a believer before he believed. For Christ, even before he began to preach, prayed when he was baptised, {Luc.3.21.} doubtless for Judas as well as for the others. Therefore Judas was a believer, just as the others were.

Further still: ‘Judas confessed and preached Christ’, {Matt.10.7.} says Bertius; therefore Judas was a believer. It is as if one said:

<!-- source-block:B033 -->

> All who confess and preach Christ are true believers and have forgiveness of their sins. Many or some hypocrites confess and preach Christ. Therefore many or some hypocrites are true believers and have forgiveness of their sins.

<!-- source-block:B034 -->

If that is so, why does Bertius say that hypocrites have never had faith? {pag.49.l.4.} Are they believers without faith? Or is Bertius trying to put something over on the unsuspecting here?

Further: Judas was incorporated into Christ, {Ioan.15.2.} says Bertius; therefore he was a believer. Now Bertius is acting without deceit, for he resorts to open lies! Was Judas incorporated into Christ? And is that written in John, chapter 15.2? At least be ashamed of being so shameless. Christ addresses the eleven there, after Judas had long since gone out, {Ioan.13.30.} and he says: Every vine branch that bears no fruit in me the Father takes away. Is there a word, a syllable, a letter, a little stroke, about Judas here, directly or indirectly? Or does Bertius think that all who perish have first been in Christ? Or does he presuppose that Judas was a believer and consequently incorporated into Christ, in order then to prove and conclude that Judas was a believer? Or does he think he has the wit to take in the whole world, as though people either could not see what he was doing or ought to be content to be put off with lies? Or does he fear that people would not quite believe that it was his duty to act honestly {dat pag.125.l.19} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m056">[Marginal translation: “that”, p. 125, line 19.]</span>, unless he had presented so striking an example of his honesty?

But however that may be, Judas must have been a believer, says Bertius: for he was given to Christ by the Father. {Joan.17.12.} And that is true, but given only for the apostolic office. He goes on to argue that since

<!-- source-block:B035 -->

> all that the Father gives to Christ comes to him, {Joan.6.37.} and he does not cast out whoever comes to him, it follows that Judas too was at first a temple of the Holy Spirit.

<!-- source-block:B036 -->

That too would be true if Judas had been given to Christ for the enjoyment of salvation. Do look at and read what we wrote to explain the fifth scriptural passage that we adduced to prove the perseverance of the saints. ‘And what more?’ he says finally: ‘He was also promised greater glory in eternal life.’ {pag.35.l.23. Mat.19.28} But how does it then come about that Judas must miss eternal life and this greater glory in eternal life? Surely it was promised to him in particular, and absolutely, without any expressed condition requiring something to be done in future; rather, it is promised as a recompense for the good already done. Can such a thing still fail to come about? It would impugn Christ’s honour to say that he would promise something in such a way that would not be fulfilled.

Therefore I side with those who say {Siet Augus de civit.Dei lib.21.cap.1.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m060">[Marginal translation: See Augustine, The City of God, book 21, chapter 1.]</span> that Christ is not speaking of the respective persons of the Apostles but of their estate or office, not promising to each of them, individually, what would happen to him, but indicating only what reward their office would receive. Each had to obtain salvation through faith; and those who, having faith and leaving everything, preached the Gospel would enjoy such glory in eternal life, etc.

Thus no particular promise is made here to Judas, nor to the other Apostles either, as Bertius seems to think. And suppose such a particular promise were made to them: it is made not because they had believed, but because they had followed him. Consequently one could still not prove from this that Judas believed, but only that Judas followed Christ, that he left his possessions, that he became an Apostle. One may not add that Judas followed Christ out of love, to attain salvation, {1.cor.13.l23 Joan.6.26. en cap.12.6. Philipp.1.18.} because the truth was in Christ, not with the body alone but also with a believing heart.

<!-- source-block:B037 -->

Seventh: Matth. 24.24. 2. Corinth. 11.3. 2. Thessal. 2.10. {7. pa.36.li23.} Answer. The power of deception is great, and the faith of many is corrupted daily by the Devil’s cunning. Nevertheless God’s firm foundation stands and has this seal: ‘The Lord knows those who are his’, {2.Tim.2.18 en19.} as the Apostle says, meaning that God’s children would not be led astray. For they have their senses trained in distinguishing good and evil; {Ebre.4.14.} they have received the spirit of understanding and discrimination; {1.cor.2.12.15} they are no longer children. {Ephes4 14.}

<!-- source-block:B038 -->

> They are of God; they know God, and consequently they hear only God’s servants. Whoever is not of God does not hear God’s servants; by this we know the Spirit of truth and the spirit of error. {1.Joan.4.6.} Christ’s sheep hear his voice and follow him, but they will not follow a stranger; rather, they will flee from him, for they do not know strangers’ voices. {Joa.10.5.27.}

<!-- source-block:B039 -->

And Christ also wishes to teach us this when he says: If it were possible, they would lead the elect astray. {Mat.24.24} ‘If it were possible’ means ‘it is not possible’, as we proved more fully above in our second argument against Bertius. What is now objected from Paul’s words to the Corinthians is therefore wholly frivolous. For were all those at Corinth fully regenerate, spiritual? {Ebr.4.14. 1.Corint.3.3} Or does the Apostle say that they can all be led astray, not only on incidental points of religion but on its principal articles and the very foundation of salvation? Does he say that even if they thought better of it, thoroughly tested the spirits and searched Scripture to see whether it said the same, {Acto.17.11.} they could never recognise the deception? Indeed, none of these things is taught here. Those who let themselves be drawn away from Christ in this way would have to possess a very slight and unstable knowledge of Christ. {Esa.54.13. Jerem.31.33 Johes.6.45. 2 cap.4.20}

The passage to the Thessalonians speaks of people who did not receive the love of the truth and did not believe the truth. {vers.10.en12} Were these, then, believing, regenerate children of God?

<!-- source-block:B040 -->

Eighth: 1. Corinth. 15.33. Galat. 5.9. Spreeck. 13.1. Psalm 1.1. Matth. 24.12. {8. Ibid.lin.38.} Answer. It is true that evil speech corrupts good conduct, just as grease stains those who touch it. Thus it can indeed happen that even the most godly, through the many bad examples they see and the evil example set by the ungodly among whom they live and with whom they must often associate, let themselves be led somewhat aside from the Lord’s way. But that they should stand or sit in the way of the ungodly—that is, remain there to the end—I firmly believe is not possible. For:

<!-- source-block:B041 -->

> They are crucified to the world, and the world is crucified to them; {Galat.6.14.} they have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires; {Galat.5.24.} they have put off, in respect of their former manner of life, the old man who is corrupted through the desires of error; {Ephes.4.22.} they have been set free from the service of sin and have become servants of righteousness; {Rom.6.20} they cannot sin, {1.Joan.3.9.} that is, cannot make a habit of sinning. They live, yet it is not they who live, but Christ who lives in them; and the life they now live in this flesh they live in the faith of the Son of God, who loved them and gave himself for them, {Galat.2.20} and who purified their hearts through faith {Act.15.9.} and cleansed their consciences through his blood from deadly works. {Ebræ.9.14.}

<!-- source-block:B042 -->

Could such people not only stumble (which they certainly can), but also remain lying where they fall? Could they fall back into an Epicurean carelessness and complete slavery to the flesh, the world, sin and impurity? Bertius does not prove this with the words he adduces. For Paul, writing to the Corinthians, addresses people who either did not know God {1.Cor.15.34.} or, in some cases, did not believe that there is a resurrection of the dead. {ibid.vers.12.} Indeed, to say that each and every member is meant when the Apostle addresses a congregation generally is so absurd that I know very well that Bertius himself will not be willing to concede it. Who, then, is meant here? I have said it. If Bertius understands it otherwise, let him bring good proof: we shall believe him.

In Galatians the subject is the doctrine of the Gospel, which was, as it were, being soured by the addition of the ceremonial Law. What has that to do with this matter? What does Sirach say against us, or David in the first Psalm that can help Bertius? Christ’s words in Matthew have been explained above. As for Jeroboam’s example, {1.Reg.12.28} although it caused Israel—that is, many in Israel—to sin, there were still many in Israel who did not follow his example. This is well known.

<!-- source-block:B043 -->

Ninth: Roman.11.18. {9. pag.37.li.8.} Answer. We have indicated the Apostle’s meaning in this saying above; it is unnecessary to relate it again.

<!-- source-block:B044 -->

Tenth: Deuteron.29.18. {10. Ibid.lin.17} Answer. If it is shown that the regenerate bless themselves in their hearts and say, ‘It goes well with me, since I walk as my heart thinks fit’, then Bertius will have gained something.

<!-- source-block:B045 -->

Eleventh: Apoc.2.5. {11. Ibid lin.19.} Answer. The teacher of the congregation at Ephesus {Johannes spreeckt eygentlijc den Leeraer aen maer onder mitsgaders di de gansche gemeynte oock mede aen.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m089">[Marginal translation: John specifically addresses the teacher, but in doing so he also addresses the whole congregation.]</span> had retained true faith and genuine Christian love, but had abandoned the first zeal and sincerity of love. Therefore he is not admonished to do good works as such, but to do his former works: that is, to do what he was now doing, but was not doing as he had done it before. And what, then, if he did not do his former works? ‘I shall remove your lampstand from its place’, says Christ. Does that now mean: ‘I shall cast you away from my presence for eternity’? It is true that those who grow sluggish in love do very wrong. But as for those who keep faith and love—love, I say, although not the first flame of love—to the end—[the clause about their salvation is broken; it contains a negative, but what is denied is unclear]. Whether Bertius would dare to do so, since he has far more freedom than I, I cannot say for certain. But I should like to see better proof of that.

<!-- source-block:B046 -->

Twelfth: Psalm 95.8. Ebrev. 3.13. {12. Ibid.lin.23.} Answer. I have explained these words already. Repeating the same point so often would take too long and weary the reader.

<!-- source-block:B047 -->

Thirteenth: Act. 7.51. 1. Thess. 5.19. Ephes. 4.30. {13. ibid.lin.29.} Answer. I have repeatedly acknowledged that the regenerate can grieve the Holy Spirit; Bertius, for his part, acknowledges that when the Holy Spirit is grieved, he is not thereby immediately expelled. {pag.16.l.3.} But that the regenerate could extinguish the Holy and sanctifying Spirit we cannot acknowledge; nor is it taught by Paul to the Thessalonians, as we proved above when explaining those same words. As for the saying of the holy martyr Stephen, it is truly making fools of honest people to try to persuade them that saints, believers, regenerate people are spoken of there. For how so? Are the regenerate, then, stubborn and uncircumcised in heart? Or are they circumcised with the circumcision that takes place without hands? {Coloss.2 11.}

<!-- source-block:B048 -->

Fourteenth: Proverb. 30.8. Deuter. 8.12. and chapter 32.15. 2. Paral. 12.1. Ezech. 16.49. Psal. 73.6. {14. Pag.37.l.37} Answer. Why does Bertius place prosperity among the incidental causes, but not adversity? Because he chooses to; and indeed he is free to do so. But what will prosperity now accomplish? Will it cause God’s children, the truly regenerate, to forget and abandon God their Lord? Then Sodom, Jeshurun, Rehoboam, all Judah and Israel must have shared in the Spirit of regeneration. They must have been righteous, holy, believing people; otherwise how does everything Bertius relates here fit the matter we are discussing? Read the words and judge for yourself.

As for Solomon, or, to speak correctly, Agur (for these are not Solomon’s words), {Salomon poechst te samen. Pro.30.8.9.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m095">[Marginal translation: Solomon brought these sayings together [the verb is unclear]. Pro.30.8.9.]</span> it is true that he fears that through riches he might exalt himself against God. But what sort of man was this Agur? Let Bertius tell us that first. And suppose he was the holiest man of that time: what else can one infer from these words of his than that, knowing the frailty of his heart, he desired moderation rather than excess? May a child of God not do that? Does not God always retain his freedom to govern us powerfully by his grace as our condition changes according to his fatherly will, for better or worse, for joy or sorrow, for suffering or rejoicing? [The opening of the question is broken; its wording is uncertain.] ‘God will not let you be tempted beyond your ability’, {1Cor.10.13.} says the Apostle. Why does this not hold just as well in prosperity as in adversity? And why may a child of God, knowing that his flesh is weak, {Mat.26.41} not fear prosperity just as well as adversity? And adversity just as much as prosperity? Or must one immediately conclude from such fear that someone sufficiently strengthened by God according to the requirements of the circumstances, with the Spirit, can nevertheless as easily be hindered by the one or the other as he, not yet having received such strength of the Spirit, suspects of himself, in his present condition, that it could happen? That is contrary to all reason.

Lastly: Galat. 5.13. 1.Corinth.8.9. Rom.14.15. 1.Corin. 8.11. {15. Pro.38.1.21} Answer. It would not be enough to show that Christian freedom can be abused in some way. Bertius would also have to show that it can be used as licence for the flesh, to the point of finally abandoning Christ and godliness. He would have to show that the regenerate could know so little of the limits of Christian freedom, or act so completely and persistently against their conscience, that they would rather cause a great multitude to stumble and bring them to ruin than give up something they could easily and in good conscience forgo, and were in duty bound to forgo.

Nowhere does Scripture teach that this has ever happened or even can happen. The very nature of a regenerate person requires far greater knowledge, love, discernment and conscience. Thus necessarily anyone who misbehaves in this way cannot be regenerate, or anyone who is regenerate cannot misbehave in this way.

As for the proof Bertius advances here, it does not contradict us. For, in Galatians, what does the Apostle mean by ‘flesh’? That which fights against the Spirit, such as all the sins called works or fruits of the flesh? {Galat.5.19} Then we must acknowledge that believers can sin. But can or must they therefore always remain lying in sin? For that is precisely our disagreement. Paul does not speak of that here. And the Apostle John, according to Bertius’s own interpretation, says No. {1.Joan.3.9.}

Writing to the Corinthians, Paul teaches that those who abuse their freedom have knowledge but no love, {1.cor.8.1/2/3 10.} or at least that while they act in this way they do not walk according to love, {Rom.14 15} as is also taught in Romans. We acknowledge that this can happen to true believers. For what are all the sins into which God’s children often fall {Jacob.3.2. 1.Joan.5.17} but transgressions against love of God and neighbour? {Matth.22 37.etc.} But can they always continue in such sins? If they fail to walk in love today, can they keep walking that way tomorrow and the day after, all the way to the end of their lives, without thinking better of it and turning back? The Apostle does not say that; and we have now shown at length and in detail that God’s Word teaches quite the contrary. Thus this too is entirely beside our point.
