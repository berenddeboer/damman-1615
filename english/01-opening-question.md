# On the Perseverance of the Saints

<a id="source-block-01"></a>

Some regard election to salvation as absolute, others as conditional. Accordingly, the perseverance of believers can be regarded either as a necessary consequence of absolute election or as a condition and prerequisite of conditional election. Bertius himself grants this. {Pag. 1. § 3.} But he alleges that the perseverance that follows absolute election is a mere imagining, and that absolute election itself is an invented doctrine, endorsed by no council and followed only by certain individuals. He claims, on the contrary, that perseverance as a condition of election flows from the Law and the holy Gospel, and that not only Holy Scripture but also reason and experience attest to it. {Pag. 1. & 2.} This is untrue. As will become plain, Bertius proves it in this work neither by Scripture nor by any sound argument.

Bertius would have done well to state more clearly and precisely at the outset the chief point on which we disagree. He does say, “The subject, or person about whom the question is asked, is a holy, believing, righteous, regenerate person.” {Pag. 3. li. 10.} But he knew well that if the matter under dispute admits of more than one sense, we must say in which sense we take it. Such words as *holy*, *believing*, *righteous* and *regenerate* have different meanings both in Holy Scripture and in the writings of biblical scholars. No one among the Reformed has ever claimed that no one can fall away who is in some sense holy, believing, righteous or regenerate, or at least is called and regarded as such. For all who are in God’s covenant and in a divine ministry, and who are only beginning to devote themselves to holiness, are called holy. {a Exo. 19.6. Num. 16.3. 1. Cor. 7.14. Origenes in Numer. Homil 10.}

<a id="source-block-02"></a>

Those who have some knowledge of God and of Christ—indeed, all who have merely been baptized—are called believers. {b Joh. 2.23. 24 25. Jacobi 2.19. Mat. 13.20. Joan. 12.42. Ambro. lib 1. de Sacrament. c. 1.} A person who does what is right and good in civil life, even without saving knowledge of Christ, is called righteous. {c Mat. 9.13. Luc. 16.15. Rom. 5.7. Phil. 3.6.9.} Such were Aristides and several others among the pagans. Those baptized in Christ’s name are called regenerate. {d Augustin. Ambros. en meer andere} <span class="marginal-translation">[Augustine, Ambrose and several others.]</span> “All who join in the profession of the Christian faith and are seen to live uprightly, we call children and members of God’s household, fellow citizens of the saints, and chosen disciples of Christ,” say Augustine {e De corrept. & grat. c. 9.} and Ambrose. {f In c. 2. ad Ephes. circa finem. & lib. de Noe. & arca. cap. 4.} Bertius himself also writes thus: {g Pagi. 53. lin. 24.}

<a id="source-block-03"></a>

> But did Paul know so certainly that they were in the Spirit that there was no room for doubt? Oh no! For Paul did not know the hiding places of the heart. Why, then, does he speak so? Because they had professed Christ; and it is reasonable to give credence to everyone who testifies thus about himself. The Apostles often judge likewise, and teach us that we too must judge in this way. 2. Corint. 10.7. If anyone trusts within himself that he belongs to Christ, let him in turn consider for himself that, just as he belongs to Christ, we too belong to Christ. Philip. 1.7. It is right for me to think this of you all; and cap. 4.3. Whose names are written in the book of life. Hebr. 6.9. We trust, beloved, that things are better with you. 1. Petr. 5.12. Through Silvanus, your faithful brother, as I judge. Apocalips. 3.1. You have the name of being alive. Likewise in other places.

<a id="source-block-04"></a>

All Reformed teachers, then, readily acknowledge that people of this kind, who bear these names and whom charity regards as such, can indeed fall away and abandon their faith, righteousness and holiness. Bertius should have given this serious thought at the outset of his book, so that he himself might rightly understand what he was setting out to write about and present it to others in good faith. He could have left aside such questions as whether to call his book *On the Perseverance of the Saints* or *On the Withdrawal of the Saints*; {Pag. 3. li. 16.} whether we may say that a person can *lose* faith or must say that he can *abandon* it; {Pag. 10. li. 4.} and others like them. These questions are quite unnecessary and do nothing to settle the main issue. Indeed, they suggest only that he wished to strike a blow at all those eminent teachers of the Church, ancient and modern, who frequently use the very expression he rejects. “Reject questions that are foolish and teach nothing,” says the Apostle. {2. Tim. 2.23}

<a id="source-block-05"></a>

But perhaps Bertius will think it enough that he says the person about whom the question is asked is a believing, righteous, holy, regenerate person—not someone who is so called, but someone who is such in truth. {Pag. 3. li. 12.} My answer is that this is not enough. We still do not know who truly is a believing, righteous, holy, regenerate person. And how shall we be convinced, when Holy Scripture speaks of true believing, righteous, holy, regenerate people, that it means those who are such in truth and not those who are regarded as such? Yet Bertius seems at last to have reconsidered and tried to make this point clearer. He writes:

<a id="source-block-06"></a>

> The person who abandons faith is not a genuine believer; for a believer, properly speaking, is one who keeps his word to the end and fulfils the conditions, etc. {Pag. 59. li. 7}

<a id="source-block-07"></a>

But what do you think? Does this not agree nicely with his question, “Can a genuine believer abandon his faith?” And with his answer: “Yes!” {Pag. 58. li. 5} Therefore I still say that Bertius, who wishes to be so wise, ought to have considered all this better at the outset of his dispute, so that he could have set plainly before our eyes both the person about whom the question is asked and what is asked about that person. He forgot to do so. Let us therefore take the trouble to examine the matter more fully for the ordinary reader.

Bertius gives this person four names: believing, righteous, holy, regenerate. We wish to give him only one name and call him (to cause the least trouble on both sides) Regenerate. Yet we do so with this proviso: since Holy Scripture and orthodox scriptural scholars, when speaking of perseverance, often ascribe it to believers, or to the saints, or to the righteous, we too shall use these names when appropriate. But by them we always understand (as they also do) those who are not merely believing, or holy, or righteous, but who are regenerate. For all the regenerate are believing, holy and righteous; but not all who are believing, or holy, or righteous are regenerate, as may be seen in the angels, and likewise Adam, Eve, etc. The person about whom the question is asked, then, is a human being who is regenerate in truth.

But whom shall we regard as regenerate in truth? We are speaking here not of a regeneration that has begun, but of one that is complete in all its parts. Answer: not the person who hears the Law, does by nature what it requires, shows the work of the Law written in his heart, while his conscience bears witness with it and his thoughts accuse and excuse one another; {Ro. 2.13.14. 15.} relies on the Law and boasts of God, knowing his will and discerning what is good, being instructed in the Law; {17. 18.} and is confident that he is a guide of the blind, a light for those who are in darkness, having the form of knowledge and truth through the Law. {19. 20.}

Nor is everyone regenerate who says “Lord, Lord” and casts out devils in the name of Jesus Christ; {Matt. 7.21. 22.} who has all faith, so that he even moves mountains; {1. Cor. 13. 2.} who knows that he is a sinner and is also grieved over sin, sighing, labouring and wearying himself under the burden of his sins (for Christ calls such people to himself, and this calling precedes regeneration); {Mat. 11. 28.} who knows that he is blind, poor, naked and wretched (for Christ invites such a person also to come to him); {Apoc. 3. 18.} who has been enlightened and has tasted the heavenly gifts, and has partaken of the Holy Spirit, and has tasted the good word of God and the powers of the world to come {Heb. 6. 4. &c.} (for regeneration does not exist without genuine faith and godliness); who has escaped the defilement of the world through the knowledge of the Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ {2. Pet. 2. 20.} (for here there is no mention of faith). None of those who have only these characteristics is truly and fully regenerate; consequently, the question here is not whether such people can abandon their knowledge, holiness and righteousness, for we grant that.

The truly regenerate, by contrast, are those who have put off the old man with his works and have put on the new, who is renewed unto knowledge after the likeness of the one who created him; {Col. 3. 9. 10.} who have received the Spirit of wisdom and revelation through the knowledge of him, enlightening the eyes of their understanding; {Ephes. 1. 18.} who have put off the old man belonging to their former conduct, who is corrupted through deceitful desires, and have been renewed again in the spirit of the mind, and have put on the new man, created after God unto truth, holiness and righteousness; {Ephes. 4. 22.} who, beholding with unveiled face the glory of the Lord as in a mirror, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Lord’s Spirit. {2. Cor. 3. 18.}

They have died to sin. {Rom. 6. 2.} Their old man has been crucified with Christ so that the body of sin may be brought to nothing and they may no longer serve sin. {6} They are justified from sin, {7.} and live to God in Christ Jesus our Lord. {11.} They are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, and the Spirit of Christ dwells in them. {Rom. 8. 9.} They are led by the Spirit of Christ and through the Spirit put to death the works of the flesh. They have been crucified with Christ and no longer live themselves, but Christ lives in them. The life they live in the flesh they live by faith in the Son of God. {Gal. 2. 19. 20.} They have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires. {C. 5. 24.} God works in them both to will and to accomplish according to his good pleasure. {Phil. 2. 13.}

In sum, a truly regenerate person is one whose understanding has been cleansed of all the damnable darkness and vanity of the world and enlightened by true saving knowledge and faith; whose affections have been put to death and, from the dominion or [unreadable] of sins, [unreadable], and kindled with new desires that accord with the divine nature, and thus made fit for a new life; {2 Pet. 1.4.} whose will conforms to God’s will; who has power through the help of the Holy Spirit not only to fight against the world, sin and the devil, but also to overcome them; indeed, who also actually fights and overcomes, doing not the evil that pleases the flesh, but the good that is acceptable to God. He does so not perfectly, but according to the measure of faith and of Christ’s gift; not continually and without interruption (for the regenerate too stumble daily in many things), {Jac. 3.2. 1. Reg. 8.46.} but generally and for the most part. It is about such people alone that we ask: can they abandon their knowledge, holiness and righteousness? {13.} {14.}

<a id="source-block-08"></a>

We now understand who the person in question is. We must also be clear about what is being asked about that person. Bertius writes:

<a id="source-block-09"></a>

> The occurrence (or accidental attribute) in question is falling away, denying the faith, abandoning one’s first righteousness, not persevering, not standing firm. {Pag. 3. li. 13}

<a id="source-block-10"></a>

And afterwards:

<a id="source-block-11"></a>

> The question is this: cannot a holy person, gradually changing his will, at last cast away all the weapons of faith? Etc. {Pa. 10. li 32.}

<a id="source-block-12"></a>

There is something to be said about this too, for Bertius has left out the most important point. Whether he did so deliberately or out of ignorance, I cannot tell. From what he says in various places in his book, he seems to think we believe the following: whether a believer prays or not, keeps watch or not, receives admonition or not—whatever he does—he still cannot abandon his faith. Nor can he even deserve by his sins to have God withdraw his faith and other heavenly goods, wholly or in part, forever or for a time. And all this solely by virtue of an absolute and precisely determined predestination to salvation!

But he falsely attributes this view to us. Name one preacher of the Reformed religion who ever taught and preached this. Show me anyone who holds such an absolute predestination that God has determined to bring certain people to eternal life even without any means at all. As for the man who, according to Bertius, dared to write these words: {Pa. 70. li. 18.}

<a id="source-block-13"></a>

> It is enough that we have acknowledged the riches and glory of God, the Lamb who takes away the sins of the world. Sin will not draw us away from that, even if we were to fornicate or kill a thousand times in one day.

<a id="source-block-14"></a>

I can say neither good nor bad of it. I would first have to know the man and see how his argument ran. Perhaps he did not mean anything as bad as the words suggest. Much depends on a fair interpretation. However that may be, Bertius knows very well that our churches as a body neither speak nor think this way about the matter. It is therefore unjust to lay at their door, as a reproach, what a private individual may have done wrong, even taking his words at their worst. In light of all this, the question in dispute should have been put as follows:

<a id="source-block-15"></a>

> Every day, through his Word and Spirit, God earnestly admonishes and powerfully strengthens a truly regenerate person to do his duty. Can such a person become so defiled and fall so far that he despises God’s admonitions and the working of the Holy Spirit, neglects his duty, changes his will—indeed, his very self—entirely, and at last casts away all the weapons of faith, casts off both the Spirit of grace and the new man entirely, and thus deprives himself of all heavenly goods?

<a id="source-block-16"></a>

That is the first point. As for the further question whether one *can* abandon faith, notice the curious play on that little word *can*. Some will indeed acknowledge that the truly regenerate will certainly retain their faith, holiness and righteousness—indeed, that everything God has decreed will certainly take place—yet they maintain that everything can still happen otherwise. God’s decree, they say, concerns only what will happen, not what can or cannot happen. But these are all perverse arguments of people who have corrupt minds and are deprived of the truth. {1. Ti. 6.5.} We wish to abide by the teaching that accords with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and with the teaching that is according to godliness; consequently, we say and believe from the heart:

I. That the truly regenerate, since they have been elected to salvation, cannot fall away from faith; for God’s decree not only will not change, but cannot change either.

II. That the truly regenerate, even apart from election (that is, with regard to regeneration itself; for otherwise we know of no truly regenerate people outside election), cannot fall away completely, provided only they do what is required of them—that is, provided only they keep watch, pray, work out their salvation, stand in faith, are strong, conduct themselves as men, etc. {Mat. 26.41. Phil. 2.12. 1. Cor. 16.13.}

III. That these same regenerate people cannot fail to do what is required of them, provided only God does for them what he has promised them—that is, provided only he gives them a heart and a mind to fear him all the days of their lives; {Jer. 32.39.} gives them more of his grace each day; {Mat. 13.12.} cleanses them so that they bear more fruit; {Joan. 15.2.} and preserves them in such a way that no one, not even Satan himself, snatches them out of his hands, etc. {ende cap. 10. 28/29.} And since God will assuredly do what he has promised, the regenerate will then also without fail do what is [unreadable] to them, and consequently not only can, but also will, certainly persevere.

I take this to be the view of all the teachers of the Reformed Church from the time of Christ to our own, including those still living. Bertius should have weighed all this carefully at the outset of his work, so that he might keep more closely to our position and better do what honour and conscience required of him in this matter. He seems to have misunderstood our view, or the ground on which we stand. Such a misunderstanding is bound to give rise to serious error. For an introduction it is enough that we now know whom the question concerns and what is being asked about that person. Let us then proceed in God’s name to prove forcefully that the answer we have already given to the question proposed accords with God’s holy Word, with the Netherlandish Confession, with the Heidelberg Catechism accepted in the Netherlandish churches, with the ancient teachers of the Christian Church, and also with all the foremost of those who have accepted and professed the Reformed religion in these and other provinces and kingdoms. We shall not present everything we could bring forward, but only a little—as much as we think will satisfy a sincere Christian and lover of the truth and set his mind at rest.
