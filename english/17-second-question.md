# The second question of this book

<!-- B002 -->
Bertius fares not a hair better with the second question than he did with the first. He has not understood—or at least has not wished to understand—the position of our Church, which he once approved and now attacks daily. He first states the question at issue in these words: {Pag.47.l.12}

<!-- B003 -->
> Is the righteousness that a righteous person abandons also true righteousness?

<!-- B004 -->
He adds: {Ibid. lin.24}

<!-- B005 -->
> Some allow that this can happen for a time and in some respects, yet deny that it can happen entirely and finally (as they put it). Their chief reason is simply that the elect must absolutely be saved and the reprobate absolutely condemned.

<!-- B006 -->
Bertius acknowledges here that we—for it is of us he speaks—allow that *true* righteousness can be abandoned. That is, the righteousness a righteous person abandons for a time is indeed true righteousness. What, then, is the point of the question? Or does he mean abandoning it entirely and finally? Then he ought to have given at least some hint of it, so that everyone could see he was asking precisely what had been asked and answered in the first part: can a righteous person abandon his righteousness entirely and finally?

Whoever is righteous has righteousness, and whoever has righteousness is righteous: truly righteous if his righteousness is true, righteous only in appearance if it is feigned. If a truly righteous person abandons his righteousness, then, what he abandons is true righteousness. That is beyond dispute. But does he also abandon it entirely and finally? That was the first question. We have already discussed it at length; there is no point in raising it again here.

Bertius also says that the chief reason the truly righteous do not abandon their righteousness entirely and finally is simply that the elect must absolutely be saved. {Ibid. lin.27} That is a slander of his own invention, as we have repeatedly shown and proved in treating the first question. Anyone who wishes to contradict another ought to present faithfully not only the other’s words but their proper sense, rather than substitute his own gloss and distort their meaning. {Mat.26.61.} That is how honest people act when in good conscience they seek only the truth.

In almost all the arguments Bertius goes on to offer as proof of this second point, {Pag 60.61 en 62.} he acts as though the question were whether grace, God, Jehovah, he who is in heaven, the Holy Spirit, righteousness, the way of righteousness, holiness and other such things are genuine goods or feigned ones. A foolish question indeed! What teacher of the Reformed Church ever doubted that God, his grace, his Holy Spirit and all the other things just mentioned are genuine goods? We expressly say that the true God, true righteousness, true holiness and the like are genuine goods. But is everything called God the true God? Is everything called righteousness and holiness true righteousness and true holiness? Of all who abandon God and fall away from their righteousness and holiness, have all truly loved, possessed and followed God, righteousness and holiness? To say nothing of others, the answer is plainly no. [The opening words are unclear; Damman may be pointing to hypocrites as his example.] Bertius now states the question a third time: {In de tytel sijns boecks nu. 10.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M06">Marginal translation: In the title of his book, no. 10.</span>

<!-- B007 -->
> Was the righteousness that is abandoned true righteousness?

<!-- B008 -->
He adds: {Pag. 47. l. 14}

<!-- B009 -->
> True righteousness is a virtue of the person converted and justified by God in Christ, who shows God and his neighbour the love he owes them and renders the services that follow from love.

<!-- B010 -->
Later he says: {Pag 58. l. 10}

<!-- B011 -->
> The *truth* of faith depends on the truth and goodness of its object, the mark on which faith fixes its eyes. Since that object is Christ with his merits and promises, there is no reason it should not have to be called true faith, even if it lasts only a day.

<!-- B012 -->
And again: {Ibid. li. 31.}

<!-- B013 -->
> The truth of righteousness consists in living according to the law God has given.

<!-- B014 -->
But this too is an ill-framed question. Look: no one is justified in Christ unless he has true faith, Bertius will say. All who look to Christ with his merits and promises have true faith, he says; and if such believers live according to God’s law, showing God and their neighbour the love they owe them, they have true righteousness. {Dit alles sal uyt het naevolgẽde best connen verstaẽ worden} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M10">Marginal translation: All this can best be understood from what follows.</span> This true faith and this true righteousness can be abandoned. That, says Bertius, is proved by all the same arguments “by which we proved the first point.” {Pag. 60. li. 5}

But why all this trouble? Whom is Bertius opposing? The teachers of the Reformed Church? Do they say that true faith and true righteousness *as Bertius describes them* cannot be abandoned? Away, away with such deceit! First learn to understand what we say before you set out to refute it. But notice how subtly Bertius proceeds, and what a grasp he has of this subject in which he involves himself and with which he takes such pains!

<!-- B015 -->
> Faith, he says, is either true or false and feigned; and the truth of faith depends on the truth of its object. {Pag. 57. l. 22}

<!-- B016 -->
Very well. On what, then, does the falsity of faith depend? {Pag. 58. l. 10} On the falsity of its object, Bertius will surely say. But what makes faith feigned? Is it that the object is feigned? [Damman appears to reply that even someone out of his mind would scarcely say so.] Or is it rather the person who feigns—the one who outwardly professes a faith he does not inwardly have? {Pag.49.l.4} Bertius himself, I think, will admit that it is. Here, then, is the first fault, a gross and ugly one, though Bertius imagines that all would be well if only people spoke as he does. {Pag.57.l.21} He goes further:

<!-- B017 -->
> God, he says, declares that person righteous and believing who lives according to his nature and according to the law that has been given. {Pag.58.l.20}

<!-- B018 -->
What? Did the Pharisees not read according to the law God had given? And Paul before his conversion—was he not blameless according to the law? {Phil. 3.6.} The angels, Adam, Eve and the Jews of whom God speaks through Ezekiel—did they not do what God commanded them? {Ezech. 33.12} And were they therefore truly righteous? Then the Pharisees, Paul before his conversion, the angels, Adam and Eve before the fall, and those Jews all believed in Christ! For true righteousness, says Bertius, is a virtue of the person converted and justified by God in Christ. {Pa.47.l.14.}

Where, then, do we put Scipio, Aristides and other pagans esteemed and praised as exceptionally righteous men? Were they truly righteous or not? Pay attention here, Master Adolph: this is oil for your lamp. If Aristides was truly righteous, he was also a true believer. And since all who truly believe are certainly saved if they persevere in true faith, it follows that Aristides was certainly saved: on that reasoning he did, after all, remain steadfast to the end in true righteousness, and therefore in true faith. {Adolph Wona: op den 19. Novem: des kercke tot Alckmaer meent dat de Heydenē sullen salich wordē. Siet sijn tegenrestantie ēē de verantwoordinghe Pag 130. ē 137} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M20">Marginal translation: Adolph (surname unclear), of the church at Alckmaer, thinks that pagans will be saved. The note also mentions 19 November, but its connection to the name is unclear. See his counter-remonstrance and the defence, pages 130 and 137.</span>

Do you say no to that? Then righteousness is not a virtue peculiar to true believers. Or, if true righteousness is a virtue peculiar to true believers, Aristides was not truly righteous. Consequently the truth of righteousness does not depend on the truth of its object: a person cannot be called truly righteous simply because he does what God has commanded in his law. Even the philosophers distinguish a virtuous act from an act of virtue. They call an act virtuous if it accords with right reason. An act of virtue not only accords with right reason but is done gladly, from the heart, without difficulty and with constancy. {De Heydenē die de wet niet en hebben doen van natueren het inhoudt des wets Roma. 2.14.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M21">Marginal translation: Those pagans who do not have the law do by nature what the law contains. Roma. 2.14.</span> Why, then, should we Christians look only at the outward act and disregard the disposition and purpose of the person who does it?

Again, when Bertius speaks of feigned righteousness, or righteousness in appearance, what does he mean by feigned righteousness? {Pag.49.l.9} Living according to a feigned law? That would be foolish. Hypocrites, too, live according to the true law of God. But they do not truly live by that law: what they seek from their works is not what they ought to seek. {mat.6.1. etc,} That is why they are called righteous only in appearance, not truly righteous. Their righteousness, though it outwardly conforms to God’s law, is feigned, not true. {Mat.23.28.}

<!-- B019 -->
Bertius continues:

<!-- B020 -->
> Properly speaking, he says, a genuine believer is one who keeps his word to the end and fulfils the conditions. {Pa.59.li.9.}

<!-- B021 -->
And later:

<!-- B022 -->
> A believer is one who keeps the faith. {Ibid. li 25.}

<!-- B023 -->
And again:

<!-- B024 -->
> Look: those who believe are already disciples. Yet it is said that if they persevere, they will then be disciples indeed. {Ibid. li.2.}

<!-- B025 -->
What else follows but that those who did not persevere to the end were indeed believers and disciples, yet were neither true disciples nor genuine believers? A believer takes his name from faith, and a righteous person from righteousness. Must not the kind of faith and righteousness a person has correspond to the kind of believer or righteous person he is? If he is a genuine believer, he has genuine faith; if he is not a genuine believer, he has no genuine faith. (Hypocrites are not believers at all; they only appear to be, and therefore have no faith.)

In short, Bertius says that the person who does not persevere in faith was indeed a believer before he fell away, but not a genuine believer. Then, I say, he had no genuine faith. Just as no one without faith can be called a believer, so no one without genuine faith can be called a genuine believer. Genuine faith is true faith, and true faith is genuine faith. Whoever lacked genuine faith therefore lacked true faith. It follows that what is abandoned is not true faith.

But would Bertius really believe that? Did he not see that he was contradicting himself? Or did he think himself free to say and write whatever came into his head, and expect the whole world to take it for pure truth and say Amen? It does him little credit. If Bertius were the man he makes himself out to be, one would think he ought to have taken a little more care with his arguments. But since he has been free to exercise his wisdom in this part too, we must be content with that.

To show both that what we have already said is true and that this whole dispute of Bertius’s is beside the point, I shall set out briefly and plainly what the teachers of the Reformed Churches hold on the matter. We teach that true believers cannot abandon their faith altogether; or, what comes to the same thing, that true faith cannot be abandoned altogether. When Scripture speaks of believers who have abandoned their faith, we grant that those people were not true believers, or that theirs was not true faith. By true faith we mean:

<!-- B026 -->
> not only a sure knowledge or understanding (these are the words of our Catechism) by which I hold as true all that God has revealed to us through his word, but also a sure trust, worked in my heart by the Holy Spirit through the Gospel, that God has granted forgiveness of sins, eternal righteousness and salvation not only to others but also to me, out of sheer grace, solely for the sake of Christ’s merits. {vraghe 21.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M28">Marginal translation: Question 21.</span>

<!-- B027 -->
We teach, then, that three things go together in true faith: first, a firm grasp and knowledge of the truth; second, assent to the truth; third, heartfelt trust. Where any one of the three is lacking, there can be no true faith. Bare knowledge of the truth cannot be called faith. Assent to a known truth is often called faith, and may even go by the name of true faith in Bertius’s account. But it cannot in fact be called saving faith, {pag.58 l.18.} for the devil also believes in this way and can never be saved. {Jacobi.2.19.} This, I think, is beyond dispute among all right-minded Christians. We come now to the third thing: trust. What is trust? Nothing more, says Bertius,

<!-- B028 -->
> than desire resting in the good it has grasped in thought. {pag.56. l.37}

<!-- B029 -->
Very masterly! Yet I think it better to say:

<!-- B030 -->
> Trust is the heart at rest because a person knows for certain, on infallible grounds, that for Christ’s sake God has granted forgiveness of sins, eternal righteousness and salvation not only to others but also to him.

<!-- B031 -->
First, I speak of the heart at rest because I think ordinary people can understand that more readily than if one spoke of desire at rest. Besides, trust belongs not to desire but to the heart, just as knowledge belongs to the understanding and assent to the will. {Rom.10.10; Ephes.1.18} I add that this rest arises from certain knowledge founded on infallible grounds. Some people assure themselves of God’s favour and eternal life without any solid grounds—indeed, often against all reason—though they are far from both and will remain so eternally. {Jere.7 9/10. Mich.3.11. Mat.7.21. [unreadable]am.1.24. ia. [unreadable] 27. Ap. c.3:17.} They have built their hopes on sand; no wonder they fall away so readily. {Matth.7.26.} True believers, by contrast, build their house on a rock so firm, and stand so firmly on it, that even the gates of hell cannot overpower them. {Matth.7.24 en cap.16.18}

As for true righteousness, we distinguish between righteousness in the regenerate person and righteousness in that person’s works. True righteousness in the person is a gift of God by which the regenerate earnestly and sincerely will to give God what is God’s and their neighbour what is their neighbour’s. It is of this righteousness that we speak when we say that true righteousness can never be abandoned. {Habituum justitiae nunquam amittitur, etsi, actꝰ injustum pluries committunt.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M36">Marginal translation: The sense appears to be: the disposition to righteousness is never lost, although they repeatedly commit unjust acts.</span> As for true righteousness in works, or truly righteous works, we define them, following the teaching of the Catechism, as:

<!-- B032 -->
> All that, and only that, which is done from true faith, according to God’s law, solely for his honour. {Vrage 91.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M37">Marginal translation: Question 91.</span>

<!-- B033 -->
This righteousness can indeed be abandoned for a time. True believers can do wrong. [Damman adds a qualification about what they sometimes do; it is unclear whether he says they sometimes do wrong or qualifies this with a negative or a limit of time.] But they do not persist in wrongdoing. {Jacob 3.2. I. Joan. 1.9.} Even while doing wrong they retain the gift just described and are rightly called righteous even then. {I Joan. 3.7.} No one can judge with certainty whether this righteousness is genuine except God and the person who practises it. {I Cor. 2.11.} For when I do something God has commanded, who can know whether I do it from faith and solely with a view to God’s honour? {Matth. 6.1. Mich. 6.6.7. Galat. 5.4.}

<!-- B034 -->
In short, as we see it, the question in this second part ought to be put like this: Did those who are taught to abandon their faith ever truly know the holy Gospel? 2. Did they receive it with full assent? 3. Were they assured on infallible grounds that their sins were forgiven and that they would be eternally saved?

Likewise, were those who are said to abandon their righteousness ever true believers? And when they performed works in accordance with the law God had given, did they do them from true faith, solely for God’s honour?

To both questions, put in this way, we answer plainly and with one voice: No. If Bertius wished to accomplish anything, he ought to have proved the contrary instead of fencing with his own shadow—unless it mattered nothing to him whom he opposed, so long as he could show the whole world that in matters of doctrine he would rather agree with the Jesuits than with the Reformed Churches in this country. For Doctor Vorstius himself, though perhaps he too saw no farther than the end of his nose at the time, counts this among the harmful errors of the Jesuits:

<!-- B035 -->
> True faith and true righteousness are not peculiar to the elect. What is more, they can be, and commonly are, abandoned entirely and finally, even by those who once truly possessed them. {Bellar m lib- 3 de Iustifi c. p. 14} {Indic. errorũ Eccles. Rom. pag. 119. l. 4}

<!-- B036 -->
What do you think? Is that not precisely what Bertius teaches? Doctor Vorstius once called it a harmful error and openly opposed it in several of his books. On the very point we are now disputing with Bertius, he taught and held just what we teach and hold. {Dat dogma} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M44">Marginal translation: That doctrine.</span> {merckt het [unreadable] tegens Pistorium, uit 2 pag.64.65.66.102.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M45">Marginal translation: Note [unreadable] against Pistorium, from 2, pages 64.65.66.102.</span>

But look how times change! What were harmful errors are now called apostolic teachings. Those who upheld and promoted such errors were once expelled from all the Reformed Churches; now it is those who rebuke and contradict the errors who are driven out of those same churches. [The second verb is unclear; the contrast suggests expulsion, but it may refer more generally to persecution.] Wherever people can or may, they oppress those who speak against these errors. Now is that not rightly called a reformation? Is that not giving doctrine an altogether different shape, shaping the shoe on a new last? You men, seize your opportunity! The fishing is good in troubled waters. Take this for certain:

<!-- B037 -->
> Every kingdom divided against itself is laid waste, and every city or house divided against itself will not stand long. {Mat.12.25.}
