But let us proceed.

<!-- source-block:B002 -->

## The second proof, from the testimony of Scripture

<!-- source-block:B003 -->

> What happened to the blessed angels and to Adam and Eve, who were endowed with true righteousness and holiness, can certainly happen. So too can what Scripture recounts, laments and rebukes in others, and foretells will happen. {pag.26.l.20}
>
> Now Scripture testifies that the righteous have abandoned their righteousness. Its witnesses are the angels, Adam, Eve and various others, in whose cases it recounts, laments, rebukes and foretells such a thing.
>
> Therefore a righteous person can abandon his righteousness.

<!-- source-block:B004 -->

## Answer

<!-- source-block:B005 -->

The second premise of this syllogism is entirely false. Nowhere does Scripture testify that anyone justified through faith in Christ ever abandoned the righteousness he had in Christ. Nor does it say that he abandoned righteousness in the other sense: the virtue by which a person converted and justified by God in Jesus Christ {pag. 4. l. 4} shows God and his neighbour the love he owes them and renders the services that follow from love. As we have often said, our dispute concerns only such a person and such righteousness. But let us hear what proof Bertius offers for his second premise.

<!-- source-block:B006 -->

{1. pag. 26. l. 23} **First: 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude, verse 6.** The example of the angels has no bearing here. Bertius himself says that the person at issue in this dispute is a holy, righteous, believing, regenerate human being. {Pag. 3. l. 10.} Our question, I say, concerns human beings, not angels. What use, then, is the example of the angels? Can everything that happened to angels happen to human beings? Or can everything that happened to human beings happen to angels? Neither is true. After a particular fall, says Bertius, human beings can be restored to their former condition; {Pag. 16. l. 10} the angels will never be restored to theirs. For a single fall or sin God cast the angels off altogether and deprived them of their holiness. Yet for a single fall God does not cast human beings off altogether or deprive them of their holiness, as Bertius himself acknowledges. {Ibid. lin. 2.}

<!-- source-block:B007 -->

{2. Pag. 26. l. 31.} **Second: 2 Corinthians 11:3.** Adam and Eve were holy and created in God’s image, yet the serpent enticed them and led them astray, bringing them to a fall, indeed to destruction.

**Answer.** As everyone knows, Adam and Eve were not believing, righteous, regenerate people in the sense already explained. By a single fall they deprived themselves and their descendants of the divine gifts they had received at creation. In this too they are quite unlike the regenerate. As we have said, the regenerate do not by a single fall deprive themselves of all the gifts they received in regeneration. Nor are they punished for sins they committed in the loins of their fathers, as Bertius likewise indicates. {Pag. 26. l. 2}

Adam and Eve were left entirely to themselves. Of the regenerate, by contrast, Scripture testifies that God’s power keeps them through faith for salvation. {1. Petr. 1. 5.} That is, they are not left to themselves. {Siet oock August. de Corro & gra c 12 daer hy seer fraey van dese sake spreeckt.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M010">Marginal translation: See also Augustine, Corro & gra, ch. 12, where he speaks very well on this matter.</span> Can one then conclude, as Bertius does here, that whatever befell Adam and Eve can likewise befall the regenerate?[^befall] That is absurd. One may argue in this way from like to like, but not from like to unlike.

<!-- source-block:B008 -->

{3. pag. 26. l. 35. en pa. 27. l. 1.} **Third: 1 Samuel 9:2 and 15:11.** Saul was choice and good, yet he turned away from following God.

<!-- source-block:B009 -->

**Answer.** Saul was choice and good in body or stature, as the text expressly says. {1. Sam. 9. 2.} But what of his mind? Did he believe in Jesus Christ? Was he justified? Was he regenerate? Scripture nowhere says so. Cyprian does say that Saul walked in the ways of the Lord. {Als Bertius verhaelt/ pa. 27. li. 12.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M013">Marginal translation: As Bertius relates, p. 27, line 12.</span> But does that mean God justified him in Christ?

Bertius insists on the Hebrew text, ostensibly to show how well he knows Hebrew, though what he actually shows is that he does not understand it. Here “choice and good” refers only to Saul’s appearance, as our translation puts it.[^saul] What follows in the text makes this clear enough, as I have said. The word *Tob*, meaning “good,” is often used this way elsewhere: Genesis 6:2 and “1. 4. 1. 5. and 24.”; Exodus 2:2; Deuteronomy 8:12; Song of Songs 4:10; Isaiah 5:9; and elsewhere. As for *Bachur*, meaning “choice,” our translation rightly gives “young.” That is the word’s proper meaning, although it derives from a word meaning “select” or “choose.” Everyone with some knowledge of Hebrew knows and understands this. Bertius can offer no argument, then, to prove that Saul may be counted among the regenerate. What bearing does Scripture’s account of Saul have on our question?

<!-- source-block:B010 -->

**Fourth: 2 Timothy 2:17.** **Answer.** Scripture does not show that Hymenæus and Philetus were believing, righteous, regenerate Christians. {4. Pa. 27. li 16} They did confess and preach the truth, and their outward profession may have led others to count them among the righteous and believing. Yet Bertius frankly acknowledges that people of this sort do not truly possess faith. {Pag. 49 I. a ende volgh.} How, then, can he prove anything better of Hymenæus and Philetus? See our answer to the thirteenth Scripture passage in his first proof.

<!-- source-block:B011 -->

**Fifth: 2 Timothy 4:10.** **Answer.** Demas abandoned Paul. {5. Pa. 27. li. 17.} But did he also abandon faith? Then let Bertius prove that he had it. All those in Asia likewise turned away from Paul, {2. Tim. 1. 15. en cap. 4. 16.} and at his first defence not one of those in Rome who professed the Christian name stood by him. They all abandoned him. Were they all believing, justified, regenerate Christians? Or can someone abandon Paul and the teaching of the faith without ever having had faith himself? Bertius’s own admission has now proved that he can.

<!-- source-block:B012 -->

**Sixth: Acts 6:5 and Revelation 2:6.** Nicolaus went from deacon to heretic. {6.} {Jud. [unreadable reference] 19.} {Oft dese Nicolaus te Antiochien een ketter zy gheworden / is by de oude kercken leeraers ontkent.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M020">Marginal translation: The ancient teachers of the church deny that this Nicolaus became a heretic at Antioch. The wording may instead mean that they considered it uncertain.</span>

**Answer.** Our question is not whether a deacon, or even a bishop, can become a heretic. It is whether someone justified in Christ can become unrighteous, a believer an unbeliever, a child of God a child of the devil. Or are all deacons believing, righteous, regenerate people? They certainly ought to be. But was Nicolaus such a man? He was a newcomer from Antioch, says the text, and nothing more. {1. Tim. 3. 8 etc} If Bertius knows more, we shall gladly hear it. {Act. 6. 3.} {Act. 6. 5.} {Act. 6. 3.} People commonly cite the apostles’ words:

<!-- source-block:B013 -->

> Look among yourselves for seven men of good reputation, full of the Holy Spirit and wisdom.

<!-- source-block:B014 -->

But it does not follow that the seven, Nicolaus among them, actually were such men. One might as well say that everyone Moses set over Israel was a reasonable, God-fearing, truthful man who hated covetousness, because Jethro advised him to choose such men. {Exod. 18. 21.} Did Moses know the people’s hearts? Or did the congregation know the minds of all who had a good reputation? If so, there could have been no hypocrites.

<!-- source-block:B015 -->

> Seventh, Solomon, who was so dear to God, allowed himself to become so estranged from God that even those who most strongly oppose our view doubt his salvation. {7. Ibid. lin. 31.}

<!-- source-block:B016 -->

**Answer.** If Bertius wishes to act in good conscience, as he knows so well how to exhort us to do, he must tell us not what others doubt, but what he himself is certain of, and prove it to us from God’s holy Word. Or does Bertius himself doubt Solomon’s salvation? Perhaps. But what follows from his doubt? Nothing certain or beyond doubt. Anyone who doubts Solomon’s salvation also doubts his damnation and so leaves the matter open. Love nevertheless inclines him to count Solomon among the saved, as he may, since God does not expressly condemn him, rather than boldly cast him into hell in defiance of love.

Yet Bertius seems more inclined to believe that Solomon is damned than that he is saved. Merely believes it?[^merely] Indeed, that alone is what he believes, and he believes it firmly. For those most strongly opposed to Bertius’s view doubt Solomon’s salvation; surely, then, {Pa. 27. li. 37.} those who most strongly support it ought to consider themselves certain that Solomon is lost. Some maintain {Ibid. lin. 21.} that those who fell and were not raised up again were reprobate and, conversely, that those who fell and were raised up again were elect. Such an assurance, says Bertius, is overturned by the example of Solomon, {Ibid. lin. 31.} who, though elect, fell and was not raised up again.

In sum: Solomon is damned, and yet Solomon was elect, for that is the word Bertius uses here. {Ibid. N. 27.} It follows that not only the regenerate, whom our question properly concerns, but even the elect can be damned. That is to say, the eternal purpose God formed in Christ Jesus can be overturned and changed. {Eph. 3. 11.} {Jac. 1. 17.} [Exclamation unclear.]

I think, then, that Bertius himself will deny that Solomon, supposing him damned, was ever elected by God. But how will Solomon’s example serve the point Bertius means to prove? [The wording of the reply is unclear.] But if Solomon may not be called elect, let us at least say that he was regenerate and that, since Solomon is damned, some regenerate people can be damned. I grant this, provided it is proved both that Solomon is damned and that he was regenerate. I know that many declare outright that Solomon is damned, and that few, if any, doubt that he was regenerate. But by what arguments will Bertius convince us that both things are true? I should like to hear them. Not that I wish to maintain that Solomon was not regenerate: I have already acknowledged the contrary plainly enough. But since Bertius means to establish so much by Solomon’s example, he has good reason to take the trouble, just once, to prove both points to us clearly and irrefutably.

<!-- source-block:B017 -->

{8. Pa. 28. li. 15.} **Eighth: 2 Peter 2:20.** **Answer.** The apostle Peter is not speaking here of regenerate people. How can someone who does not believe in Christ be called regenerate? Yet there is no mention of faith here. Indeed, Peter compares these people to pigs, {Vers. 22.} which can be washed clean on the outside yet always retain their natural inclination to filth and every kind of uncleanness. That comparison shows plainly enough that they had no faith. For it is through faith that God cleanses hearts, {Act. 15. 9.} and Christ’s blood, received through faith, cleanses consciences from deadly works. {Hebr. 9. 14.}

The text says that they knew Christ and the way of righteousness. {2. Petr. 2. 20. 21.} That knowledge was good, but not enough to justify them before God and save them.[^justify] For it is not the person who knows what is good who is saved, but the one who does it. {Rom. 2. 13.} {Iacob 1. 22.} {Joan 13. 17.} And since these people knew what was good and did not do it, their judgment will be all the heavier, as the apostle says. {Luc. 12. 47.} {Iacob. 4. 17.} {Joan. 15. 22. etc.}

Notice also what the text does not say. It does not say that they turned away from Christ, from faith or from their righteousness. It says only that they turned away from the holy commandment, or the holy teaching given them. {Vers. 21.} Nor does it say that they had received that holy teaching with faith. Even if it did say so, that would still not prove that they had been regenerate Christians. How, then, can that be proved now?

The apostle compares them to dogs that return to their own vomit. {Vers. 22.} Can one conclude from this that they had been [unreadable] in heart before they returned to the defilements of the world from which they had escaped? Is there any more warrant for that conclusion than for saying {Pro. 26. 11.} that a fool who repeats his folly is wise before he repeats it? For Solomon says:

<!-- source-block:B018 -->

> Just as a dog eats again what it has vomited, so does the fool commit his folly again.

<!-- source-block:B019 -->

A fool is always a fool, though he does not always commit folly. When he keeps silent, even a fool is thought wise and understanding. {Pro. 17. 28.} So too these people were always unclean, though they did not always wallow in uncleanness. Indeed, people even thought them clean because of their former association with those who were clean.[^among]

<!-- source-block:B020 -->

> But since such apostates, {Siet eñ leest de deghe igene Bertius schryft. pag. 48. l. 36 en pa. 49. geheel.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M048">Marginal translation: See and read carefully what Bertius writes on p. 48, line 36, and the whole of p. 49.</span> says Bertius, are compared to the fallen angels in verse 4, did the angels’ original condition also consist merely in bare knowledge and outward discipline?

<!-- source-block:B021 -->

**Answer.** Peter does not compare the condition of the one group with that of the other. {Pa. 117. l. 4.} He compares their sins and punishments:

<!-- source-block:B022 -->

> There will be false teachers, he says, and many will follow their corruption. {Vers. 1. 2.} Their condemnation does not delay. For if God did not spare the angels when they sinned, or the old world, {4.} and condemned the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah, {5. 6.}

<!-- source-block:B023 -->

then such people too, when they sin, will not be spared but condemned. Were the angels, the old world, Sodom and Gomorrah all in the same condition? Even children would see the absurdity. Yet these things are plain and clear, says Bertius: the words are not ours, {Pa. 117. li 9.} but the apostle’s, indeed God’s. See what a high opinion this man has of his stinking glosses! Let us not kick against the goad; God will not let himself be mocked. Bertius preaches well here, but does he consistently practise what he preaches?

<!-- source-block:B024 -->

> May God grant that we conduct ourselves with simplicity and godly sincerity, as in God’s presence. {Pa. 116. l. 27 Voorreden Ier 3. 14. ende Pa. 125 lin. 19.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M054">Marginal translation: Preface.</span> Amen.

<!-- source-block:B025 -->

As if Bertius were so godly and holy! He boasts often enough of his sincerity and candour, whether the occasion calls for it or not. But saying it does not make it so.

<!-- source-block:B026 -->

{9. Pag. 28. li. 7} **Ninth: Isaiah 1:21.** **Answer.** Suppose that worship, piety and righteousness have grown and flourished among the inhabitants of a city or province. Can they decline over time, first in part and then altogether? That is not our question. Who could or would deny it? Our question is whether one and the same regenerate person can abandon his former righteousness altogether. Of this Isaiah says not a word, unless one maintains that all Jerusalem’s leaders had been pious, believing, righteous, regenerate people, or that the very people who had been such were now fornicators and murderers. If Bertius can prove this, he stands on firm ground. We deny both claims.

<!-- source-block:B027 -->

**Tenth: Isaiah 30:1 and Jeremiah 2:13.** **Answer.** {10. Pa.28.li.10.} Not all who are called God’s children and God’s people may be called believers or regenerate people, as Bertius himself acknowledges. {Pag.19 l.4.} See our answer to the fifth Scripture passage in the first proof. The words cited here therefore do not help our opponent.

<!-- source-block:B028 -->

**Eleventh: 2 Peter 2:1.** **Answer.** {11. Pa.28.li.15.} We explained these words in our answer to the 17th Scripture passage in the first proof. See there.

<!-- source-block:B029 -->

**Twelfth: 1 Timothy 4:1.** **Answer.** {12. Ibid.lin.19.} See my answer to the sixth Scripture passage in the first proof.

<!-- source-block:B030 -->

**Thirteenth: Acts 20:30.** **Answer.** {13. Ibid.lin.22.} The false teachers will indeed speak perverse things in order to draw the disciples away. But will the disciples therefore let themselves be drawn away? Or does the text say that it will be possible to draw them away? Suppose they could be drawn away: that, one might say, is why the apostle warns them. Are the disciples, then, the believers or the regenerate who were at Ephesus? Scripture testifies otherwise: {Joa.6.64.66}

<!-- source-block:B031 -->

> They went out from us, but they were not of us, etc. {1.Joa.2.19.}

<!-- source-block:B032 -->

So much, then, for Bertius’s second proof.
