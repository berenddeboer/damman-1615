<!-- source-block:B001 -->

We turn to the third.

<!-- source-block:B002 -->

## The third proof, from the nature of the end proposed on condition

<!-- source-block:B003 -->

> If a man can fall short of his end in such a way that God no longer regards his former righteousness, and the man is rejected and falls into death of both soul and body and into ruin from which there is no recovery, then he can finally abandon his righteousness. {pa.30. l. 38}

<!-- source-block:B004 -->

> The righteous man can fall short of his end, since it is set before him only conditionally, not absolutely. Indeed, he can fall so far short of it that God no longer regards his former righteousness, etc.

<!-- source-block:B005 -->

> Therefore the righteous man can finally abandon his righteousness.

<!-- source-block:B006 -->

## Answer

<!-- source-block:B007 -->

The second premise is entirely false. Nowhere does Scripture teach that a person justified in Christ can fall short of his end in such a way that God no longer regards his former righteousness, that he is rejected and falls into death, etc. The reasons Bertius gives to prove his claim are so weak, so beside the point and so frivolous that I am not only astonished but thoroughly ashamed for his sake. Just listen to what he says.

<!-- source-block:B008 -->

{Pa. 29. li. 8.} First:

<!-- source-block:B009 -->

> It is not the nature of God's covenant that he wishes to love and save a person without regard to anything, but rather as one who obeys, believes and is righteous.

<!-- source-block:B010 -->

**Answer.** I grant this:

<!-- source-block:B011 -->

> For whoever believes {Mar. 16.16.} will be saved; whoever does not believe is condemned. {Ebre. 5.9.} Christ is the cause of eternal salvation to all who obey. Those who do not obey the Gospel will suffer punishment. {2. Tes 1.8.9.} {Matt. 7.21.} Not everyone who says “Lord, Lord” will obtain the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my heavenly Father.

<!-- source-block:B012 -->

There are similar teachings in many other places. {Pag. 29. l. 12} {Ezech 17..15.} But what does Bertius mean to prove by this?

<!-- source-block:B013 -->

> When obedience ceases, he says, God's obligation also ceases. And the obedience of the righteous can undoubtedly cease. Therefore God's obligation to them can also cease, so that he does not give them the salvation he promised.

<!-- source-block:B014 -->

This is mere wind. For the Lord God not only promises salvation to those who obey; he also promises:

<!-- source-block:B015 -->

> I will give them {Jere. 32.39. ende 40.} one heart and one mind, so that they will fear me all their lives. I will put the fear of me in their hearts, so that they do not depart from me.

<!-- source-block:B016 -->

Can anyone hesitate to fear God, obey him and walk in his ways when God does what he promises here? Or can God fail to do what he so expressly promises he will do? It is impossible. But how, then, is this a conditional covenant? If God does it all, what shall we do, and what must we do? **Answer.** We shall and must obey, fear God and not depart from him. Man, we are speaking of a new covenant, a covenant of grace, not the old covenant, the covenant of works. {Jerem 31.31 ende 32.} This has been shown several times already.

<!-- source-block:B017 -->

{2.} **Second: 1. Corinth. 15.2.** **Answer.** “Gospel” here means the teaching of the Gospel, as the rest of the passage makes clear. {Ibid. lin. 17} {Vers. 3. etc.} The word is often used this way: see Mar. 16 15., Galat. 1.6.7. and elsewhere. {Vers. 1.2.} The Corinthians, then, had accepted the teaching of the Gospel. They took their stand on that teaching and still held to it. If they kept it unaltered, just as Paul had preached it to them, they would be saved through it. {Vers. 11.} “Unless,” says the Apostle, “you have believed in vain”—namely, in believing that you would be saved through this teaching and that what I preached to you was true. [The wording of the first clause about salvation is unclear.] {Vers. 12. 33. 34 ende 58.} For in this chapter the Apostle warns the Corinthians not to accept any teachers who preach otherwise than he had preached. His general argument runs as follows:

<!-- source-block:B018 -->

> Whoever has believed that the teaching I preach is the teaching of truth and salvation must not accept any teaching that contradicts mine. You Corinthians have believed this. Therefore.

<!-- source-block:B019 -->

And further:

<!-- source-block:B020 -->

> Whoever abandons the teaching he accepted has believed in vain; or, whoever does not abandon the teaching he accepted has not believed in vain. (This is how the conditional proposition “if you have not believed in vain” must be analysed.) You Corinthians either abandon the teaching you accepted or you do not. Therefore you have either believed in vain or not believed in vain.

<!-- source-block:B021 -->

If you now accept as true a teaching that contradicts mine, you believed in vain that my teaching was true. If you now accept for salvation a teaching that contradicts mine, you also believed in vain that mine was the teaching of salvation. It is one thing to fall away from the Gospel teaching one has accepted, quite another to fall away from applying the promises of the Gospel to oneself in particular.

<!-- source-block:B022 -->

**Third: Rom. 2.7.** **Answer.** These words do prove {3.} {Ibid. ll. 20.} the first premise of Bertius's syllogism, which is not in dispute. But it was the second premise that needed proof: that some who are justified in Christ could fail to seek incorruptibility by persevering in good works. The Apostle does not say that, so this passage is beside the point.

<!-- source-block:B023 -->

**Fourth: Ebre. 3.6.7.** From this Bertius constructs the following syllogism. {4.} {Ibid. lin. 3.} {Pa. 1. 11. 17.}

<!-- source-block:B024 -->

> Whoever can harden his heart, is warned not to do so and is urged instead to hold on to confidence and hope to the end, can finally fall away. The righteous, the holy, the believing can harden their hearts and are warned not to do so, etc. Therefore.

<!-- source-block:B025 -->

**Answer.** 1. The first part of this argument is false. The whole church—that is, all who are holy and regenerate, not only those among the Hebrews but also those scattered throughout the world—are warned, or must be warned, not to harden their hearts, etc. It would follow that all the regenerate must be capable of finally falling away. That amounts to saying that Christ could be without a church here on earth. Would Bertius really dare believe and maintain that, especially now that Christ has promised

<!-- source-block:B026 -->

> that he wishes to remain with us {Mat. 28.19.} and will do so every day until the end of the world;

<!-- source-block:B027 -->

and again:

<!-- source-block:B028 -->

> {Ioan.14.16.} I will ask the Father, and he will give you a Comforter to remain with you for ever?

<!-- source-block:B029 -->

2. Nor do the Apostle's words prove the second part of the argument. Consider those who have some confidence in God and Christ, and those who boast of their hope of salvation. {Mat.7.21. en ca.25.11. Luce 13.25. Rom.2.17. Apo.3.17.} {Siet ooc Bertium pa.31. lin.30. inde volghens.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M025">Marginal translation: See also Bertius, p. 31, line 30 and following.</span> Not all of them, I say, are righteous, holy believers. Nor does a warning not to harden one's heart declare that the person warned already has a softened, believing, holy heart. A person hardens his heart as much by refusing to accept faith and holiness as by refusing to hold on to them. Here the Apostle urges the Hebrews more to do the former—to accept faith—than to do the latter. For not all the Hebrews yet knew Christ well, and so not all had yet fully accepted him. {Prov.19.9.} Bertius says that these Hebrews are urged to hold on to their hope and confidence to the end. One can draw that conclusion from these words only by inference. Even so, it does not prove with certainty that the Hebrews were holy, believing and righteous, and would be saved if they held on to the end to the hope and confidence they had and boasted of. For, as Bertius himself teaches elsewhere:

<!-- source-block:B030 -->

> {pag.48.l.36} God alone knows hearts, and he alone can judge hypocrisy. That is why, on the strength of their outward profession, we often count hypocrites among the righteous and believing. {Psal.78.36.} So it was with the Israelites: they lied to God with their tongues, but their hearts were not steadfast towards him.

<!-- source-block:B031 -->

{Pa.49.li.33 ende pa.33. lin 25.} And again:

<!-- source-block:B032 -->

> Paul did not know beyond all doubt that the Romans were in the Spirit and not in the flesh, for he did not know the secrets of the heart. He speaks as he does because they had professed Christ, and it is reasonable to believe anyone who testifies to this about himself.

<!-- source-block:B033 -->

Take these words in the light most favourable to Bertius. All they could prove is that someone presumed to be inwardly what he outwardly professes {Siet Bertii pa 49.l.12} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="M030">Marginal translation: See Bertius, p. 49, line 12.</span> can fall away from confidence and hope—not the confidence and hope he truly has, but those he seems to have. What has that to do with our question? If the Hebrews had a proper confidence and a well-founded hope of salvation, it was certain that they were God's house and would be saved if they kept that confidence and hope to the end. But does it follow that they *had* a proper confidence and a well-founded hope? Yes, says the Apostle, if you hold on to the end to the hope and confidence that, on the strength of your outward profession, I believe you have, then you are God's house, or Christ's. Therefore, if you do not hold on to them, you are not God's house or Christ's. To put it more briefly still against Bertius, though he pays it no heed: could anyone speak more clearly than the Apostle? {pag.111.l.26}

<!-- source-block:B034 -->

**Fifth: 2.Chron.15.2. and Psal.1.19.** **Answer.** This has no bearing on the matter. Not a word is said here about believers or their end, which is eternal salvation. What God promises here are bodily blessings for his people in general if they obey him. {5. pag.29.l 24 ende 29.} Even if one wished to draw an analogy from bodily blessings and speak in the same way of spiritual blessings, there are several respects in which the two differ. The application still fails: it would have to show that some who are justified in Christ have forsaken God and refused to hear or obey him. [The wording about forsaking God and refusing obedience is indistinct.] That is our chief point of dispute, yet there is not the slightest mention of it here.

<!-- source-block:B035 -->

**Sixth: Ezech.33.13.** **Answer.** Those who live according to God's law are righteous and are called righteous. It is of them that Ezekiel speaks. But we are speaking of those justified in Christ. They never rely on their own righteousness, but only on the righteousness of Jesus Christ, of which they are never stripped. {6. ibid. lin.35. Ezech.18.5. etc. Phil.3 9.} If Bertius will not believe this, he is free to prove the contrary.

<!-- source-block:B036 -->

**Seventh: Ebreē.10.38.** **Answer.** We have already explained this passage and shown that it does not bear on the question. {7. Pag.30.l.4} See the 16th Scripture passage in Bertius's first proof.

<!-- source-block:B037 -->

**Eighth: 2.Timoth.2.12. and Luc.9.26.** **Answer.** Not all who know God and profess Christ are believers or righteous. That is beyond dispute. {8. Ibid.lin.5.6} Those who deny God and are ashamed to profess Christ any longer do not, then, abandon their faith or righteousness. So this too has no bearing on the matter.

<!-- source-block:B038 -->

**Ninth: Ezech.33.18.** **Answer.** This is beside the point unless someone first shows on sound grounds that by “the righteous” the Prophet means those justified in Christ. {9. ibid. lin.9.} We think no one will ever be able to show that.

<!-- source-block:B039 -->

**Tenth: Joan.15.2. and 6.** **Answer.** We have, we trust, set out the true sense of these words of Christ in our reply to Bertius's objection to our fifth argument, in which we proved the perseverance of believers. {10. ibid. l 11.12.} Look there and read it.

<!-- source-block:B040 -->

**Eleventh: Roman.8.12.** **Answer.** This could help Bertius if all the Romans had been in the Spirit. But Bertius will surely stop short of saying that or proving it. The Apostle himself does, it is true, {11. ibid.lin.15.} seem to say so. But if Bertius stands by his own interpretation {Rem.8.8.} of these words, I hope he himself will acknowledge that the words he cites {pag.53.l.25.} do not bear on our subject.

<!-- source-block:B041 -->

**Twelfth: Syrach. 16.36.** **Answer.** Bertius is so hard pressed {12.} that, unable to defend his misdeeds {pag.30.li.18} from the divine, canonical Scriptures, he must call on the Apocrypha for help. Yet it is wasted labour. For not only those who have faith in their hearts but also those who profess it only outwardly can fall away from faith. We have said this several times and shown it clearly both from God's word and from Bertius's own book. This too, then, is frivolous.

<!-- source-block:B042 -->

**Lastly: Ebreos.6.4.** **Answer.** This passage mentions neither faith in Christ nor love. Could anyone who has neither faith nor love really be called justified or regenerate? {13. Ibid.lin.21.} Look also at the 7th or 8th verse, where the Apostle draws an analogy between good and bad soil:

<!-- source-block:B043 -->

> Soil, he says, that drinks in the rain that often falls on it and yet produces thorns and thistles deserves rejection and is close to being cursed.

<!-- source-block:B044 -->

By bad soil he means those who hear God's word, and hear it often, but are none the better for it. If we apply the analogy to the Apostle's subject, as clearly we must, bad soil means those who have been enlightened, have tasted the heavenly gift, have shared in the Holy Spirit and have tasted the good word of God and the powers of the world to come, yet either forget what they have learned or have not made the progress they ought to have made. This is the sum of it:

<!-- source-block:B045 -->

> I had much to tell you Hebrews about Christ, but it would be too hard {Ebræ.5.11} for you to understand. For although you have long heard God's word {12.} and learned from it the foundations of the Christian religion, you have not yet trained your faculties to distinguish good from evil. You are still stuck at the beginnings; indeed, you have gone backwards in your learning, which ought not {14.} to have happened. For if, after being enlightened to this extent and {Cap.6.4.} having learned so much, you now return to your old error, {5.} abandoning the truth of which you are convinced in your conscience, it is impossible for any further grace from God {6.} to be shown to you. But though {9.} I speak this way, I trust that this will not happen to you. For God is not so unjust as to forget your {10.} work. I desire each of you {11.} to show the same diligence to the end, {12.} so that you do not grow sluggish, etc.

<!-- source-block:B046 -->

This is the true meaning of the Apostle's words. It is clear from them that those Hebrews who cannot properly grasp and understand what the Apostle writes about Christ in this epistle did not yet believe it fully and beyond all doubt. Just as there can be no faith without knowledge and understanding, so faith must be in proportion to knowledge and understanding: perfect knowledge, perfect faith; imperfect knowledge, imperfect faith. Both kinds were undoubtedly found among the Hebrews. Surely, I think, they were not all regenerate or all unregenerate. They did not all need milk, nor did they all need solid food. Let us distinguish between them, then, and not apply strictly to each and every Hebrew what is said to the Hebrews simply and in general. The words of D. Junius, to whom Bertius refers us, would be far too long {pag. 115. l. 14} to repeat here. We have already seen what he thinks on this subject. Still, anyone who cares to take the trouble to read D. Junius on this 3rd chapter, 6th verse, will find that Bertius is quite wrong in his handling of the matter. In the meantime Bertius might give some serious thought to how he could prove, on sound grounds, that all who have been enlightened, tasted the heavenly gift, shared in the Holy Spirit, and tasted the good word of God and the powers of the world to come—that all such people, I say, are on that account alone regenerate, believing, holy and righteous. For it is such people that our dispute concerns. And with that we take our leave of the third proof.
