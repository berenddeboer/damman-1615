<!-- source-block:b000 -->
{pag. 24. l. 11.} “Let us return, then, to our subject,” says Bertius, “and prove what we have proposed, as follows.”

<!-- source-block:b001 -->
> {pag. 26. l. 6.} Whoever can fall away, turn away from righteousness, fall from grace, depart from the living God, become salt that has lost its savour, draw back, fall away from faith, fall from his own steadfastness, refuse him who is from heaven, quench the Spirit, suffer shipwreck in faith, cast away his confidence, withdraw himself, deny the Lord, become dross, degenerate into a wild vine, fall from heaven—such a person can abandon his former righteousness.
>
> Now someone who is righteous can do all these things together. Therefore.

<!-- source-block:b002 -->
# Answer

<!-- source-block:b003 -->
To understand this properly, we must remember what has been related above: first, that this dispute concerns a righteousness which, as Bertius teaches, {pa. 47. li. 14} is a virtue of a person who has been converted and justified by God in Jesus Christ. Such a person shows God and his neighbour the love he owes them and renders the services that follow from love.

Secondly, it is not denied, and consequently need not be proved, that a righteous person can for a time abandon his former righteousness, so that he is not as pure and upright in good works as he was before. The only question requiring proof is whether a righteous person can abandon his righteousness for ever. With this established, I say that both propositions in Bertius's syllogism are false, and consequently his conclusion is void.

As for the first proposition: consider all who do righteous works; 2. those to whom grace is offered; 3. those who believe God; 4. those who are good salt; 5. those who cling to God; 6. those who have or profess some faith; 7. those who are steadfast in the doctrine of truth, or at least think themselves steadfast; 8. those who hear, accept and acknowledge God's Word; 9. those who are in Christ's doctrine; 10. those who have some love; 11. those who have some gifts of the Spirit; 12. those who have the boldness to suffer something for the truth; 13. those who begin in the Spirit; 14. those who profess God; 15. those who acknowledge the Lord. Not all these, I say, are converted and justified in Jesus Christ. Consequently someone can indeed cease to do good or righteous works, fall away from God, be excluded from the grace offered, depart from God, become salt without savour, etc., without thereby abandoning the righteousness of which we are speaking here. That is the first point.

<!-- source-block:b004 -->
As for the second proposition, that a person justified in Christ can completely and for ever turn away from his righteousness, fall from grace, depart from the living God, etc.: this too is untrue, and Bertius does not prove it in any way.

<!-- source-block:b005 -->
As for what he cites to this end from Ezechiel 33.18., I answer that not all who are called righteous are justified in Christ; for one who acts rightly and well is also righteous. Thus being godless and becoming godless do not necessarily mean falling out of Christ and faith, but sinning and transgressing God's covenant, as these Jews against whom Ezekiel speaks had transgressed God's covenant. Indeed, many have become godless, without our therefore being entitled to say that they have all abandoned their faith in Christ. It is also said of many that they became godless, although they had never been true believers. Otherwise one would have to say that all the Jews carried away into Babylonian captivity, every single one, had previously been true believers; indeed, that no one could become godless unless he had first been a true believer. {Pa.24.l.16.} {Gene.18.23. Ezech.18.5. Haba.c.1.13. Dan.11.32. Ezech.5.6. Nehemi 9.33. Dan.9.5.15}

<!-- source-block:b006 -->
Moreover, by the life promised to the righteous we must not always understand eternal life. We can also understand it as temporal life, that is, temporal blessing and prosperity. {Deutr.5 33. ende ca 8.1. ende c.16.20 ende c.30 6. 1.16 19. ende c 32.39} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m006">Marginal reference: Deutr.5 33., and chapter 8.1., and c.16.20, and c.30 6. 1.16 19., and c 32.39.</span> Likewise, by the death threatened against the godless we must often understand only temporal punishments and adversity. Not that the godless do not also deserve eternal death through their sins; rather, I say that God does not always threaten the godless with eternal death alone. Thus, when he promises to deliver them from death if they repent, he does not always mean eternal death by it either.

And what is turning from one's godlessness? Becoming a believer in Christ? Then all the Ninevites must have become believers in Christ. {Jonae 3.10.} Indeed, since the Israelites—for they were in God's covenant—are said to have repented, one must suppose that they all became true believers and regenerate people; and that no one is or can be freed from outward punishments, or obtain forgiveness of any sins, except true believers alone. {1. Sa. 7. 3 4. Neh e. 9. 28. En in veel der Richteren dwi ls. Num. 14 20. 35. 1. Reg. 14 13. Ps c.21.29.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m008">Marginal note and references: 1. Sa. 7. 3 4.; Neh e. 9. 28.; and often in Judges; Num. 14 20. 35.; 1. Reg. 14 13.; Ps c.21.29.</span>

But all this is far too absurd. We therefore say, first, that the words of Ezekiel cited above can certainly be understood of people other than the regenerate. Secondly, those same words, in sensu primo—that is, according to the prophet's first and proper intention—must be understood of others, at least of more people than the regenerate.

Consider the chapter itself. Nowhere does it mention Christ, faith or those justified in Christ. It speaks instead of an outward sword and the temporal death that follows it. {Ezech. 33.2. 4.} The Israelites were already under that sentence of death, over which they disputed earnestly with God: {17.20.} they were captives in Babylon because of their sins. The prophet means to teach that they could get out of it again (that is, become alive) {10.} if they repented. All these are outward things which true believers and unbelievers have in common. But it is also evident from the prophet's description of these righteous people, these converted godless people, when he says:

<!-- source-block:b007 -->
> When I say to the godless man that he shall die, and he turns from his sin and does what is right and good, {Vers.14.} so that the godless man returns the pledge and repays what he has stolen, and walks according to the word of life, doing nothing evil, then he shall live and not die. {15.} And none of all the sins he has committed shall be remembered; for he now does what is good and right, and therefore he shall live. {16.}

<!-- source-block:b008 -->
This is surely a righteousness of the law, not of the gospel. And if all such righteous people are to live and obtain forgiveness of their former sins, precisely because they have become righteous in this way—that is, because they now do righteousness—then we may not understand life as eternal life, nor forgiveness of sins as a forgiveness that brings salvation. {Eze. 18.24 Iobus. 2.4. Galat. 2.16. Ro. 3.24.25. Phil. 3.9.} For eternal life and the forgiveness of sins that brings salvation are obtained only through Christ's righteousness, not through righteousness that one practises oneself. {Colos. 1.14.} It is true that believers also demonstrate their faith through such works of righteousness. {Iacob 2.18. 2.pet.1.5. &c} Nevertheless, not all who do such works are therefore believers, although they are indeed righteous in this sense; and yet here life is promised to everyone who does such works.

<!-- source-block:b009 -->
> The prophet here sets the Jews' sins before them—these are D. Junius's words, where he speaks of chapter 33 and some of the chapters that follow—and further exhorts them to repent and then trust firmly that they would yet be delivered in the end. {In argumento Ezechielis.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m018">Marginal title: In the summary of Ezekiel.</span>

<!-- source-block:b010 -->
Lodovicus Lavaterus, an eminent minister at Zurich, also understands it in this way; {Præfac. in Ezechielem.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m019">Marginal title: Preface to Ezekiel.</span> and apparently so does the very learned Peter Martyr. For he says that chapter 18 of Ezekiel properly speaks of the punishments of this life; {In Iudic.11. initio.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m020">Marginal reference: On Judges 11, at the beginning.</span> and elsewhere he says that chapters 18 and 33 of Ezekiel deal with the same subject. {In 1.Sam. 2. 25.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m021">Marginal reference: On 1.Sam. 2. 25.</span> Thus here in chapter 33 too, it must properly be the punishments of this life that are under discussion. Other distinguished men hold the same opinion, of whom I now give no further account for brevity's sake. It is enough for me to have shown that not only the circumstances of the text but also some learned men testify that these words of the prophet, cited above, do not serve Bertius's advantage or purpose. For from them one could conclude only—if indeed even that could be concluded—that someone who now does good or righteousness can fall into doing evil; and we readily acknowledge that this is true.

<!-- source-block:b011 -->
Next he puts forward Galat.5.4. {2. Pa.24.li.19}

**Answer.** Whoever wishes to be justified through the law has fallen from grace: not from grace in which he has come to share, but from the grace offered to him in Christ. Yet no regenerate people seek to be justified by the law. For all the regenerate know that they have already been justified by grace: why, then, would they still seek to be justified by the law? Who seeks what he knows he has? It is certainly true that someone who says he is justified by the law can afterwards seek to be justified by grace, and that someone who says he is justified by grace can afterwards seek to be justified by the law. But in doing so, such people openly show that they were not truly justified before: they merely imagined themselves so. And what bearing has that on the regenerate, or true believers?

<!-- source-block:b012 -->
Thirdly, Heb.3.12. From this Bertius constructs the following argument: {3. pa.24.li.25 Pa.113.l.24}

<!-- source-block:b013 -->
> The Hebrew brethren can take on an evil, unbelieving heart and depart from the living God.
> The Hebrew brethren are believing and holy.
> Therefore some believing and holy people can take on an evil, unbelieving heart and depart from the living God.

<!-- source-block:b014 -->
# Answer

<!-- source-block:b015 -->
I can grant this without injury to the truth. For our question concerns not people simply called believing and holy, but the regenerate. If someone wishes to say that some Hebrew brethren were regenerate, and that consequently some regenerate people can also depart from the living God, I answer that this does not follow unless it is first proved that all the Hebrew brethren can depart from God. The apostle not only does not say this here; he even teaches the contrary plainly enough in verse 14:

<!-- source-block:b016 -->
> For we have come to share in Christ, provided that we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end.

<!-- source-block:b017 -->
From these words, applied to the preceding ones, I conclude as follows:

<!-- source-block:b018 -->
> Those who have come to share in Christ retain the beginning of their confidence to the end.
> Those who depart from God do not retain the beginning of their confidence to the end.
> Therefore those who depart from God have not come to share in Christ.

<!-- source-block:b019 -->
And further:

<!-- source-block:b020 -->
> Those who depart from God have not come to share in Christ.
> Some Hebrew brethren have come to share in Christ.
> Therefore some Hebrew brethren do not depart from God.

<!-- source-block:b021 -->
{* Nota. Dit wijst gelijck alleen op een door-reden / by maniere van concessie ten aensien der aengevogener woorden Pauli: niet dat de meyninge zy dat de rechtveerdige can afvallen. Want al en wort hier niet gesproken dan niet te connen afvallen / so wort sulcx nochtans op andere plaetsen duydelic genoech uytgedruckt.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m024">Marginal note: This is said by way of concession, with reference to the words of Paul just cited. The point is not that the righteous can fall away. Although Paul does not speak here of their inability to fall away, he expresses it clearly enough elsewhere.</span>

<!-- source-block:b022 -->
Bertius has nothing to say against this except that my conclusion speaks of what will happen, whereas our question concerns what can happen. But there is a good remedy for that. Let Bertius grant us that one who has once been born again will never depart from God, and I will—with my fellow brethren's permission—grant him that one who has once been born again can depart from God: that he can, I say, but never will. Will Bertius be satisfied then? At any rate he will have gained nothing, and we will have lost nothing, as far as the principal issue is concerned.

<!-- source-block:b023 -->
Fourthly, Marc. 9. 50. {4. Ibid. lin. 24 pag 38. li. 3. ende 23.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m025">Marginal reference: 4.; Ibid. lin. 24; pag 38. li. 3. and 23.</span>

**Answer.** Salt cannot mean righteousness; for salt can lose its savour, but righteousness cannot become unrighteousness, says Bertius. Nor can salt mean a righteous person, for Christ immediately adds: Have salt in yourselves. {Vers 50} Elsewhere it says: You are the salt of the earth. {Matt. 5. 13.} And: Everyone shall be salted with fire. {Marc. 9 49} And: Let your speech be mixed with salt. {Colos. 4. 6.} In these words salt cannot mean a justified person or righteousness, as anyone can readily see. Consequently the passage cited above does not serve this purpose at all.

<!-- source-block:b024 -->
Fifthly, Esai. 1. 4 {5. Joio lib. 27.}

**Answer.** God speaks of the Israelites in general. They are called children not because they had received Christ through faith, {Joan. 1 12.} but because God had admitted them to a covenant of grace. In this covenant God required them to know, serve and obey him alone as God. {Deut. 5. 9. Deut. 10. 12.} Yet they did not do so: through idolatry and disobedience they forsook the Lord and drew back, as it says here. Not that they were all so wicked—for a little good still remained in Israel—{Deut. 32. 15. etc. Eccli. 4. 5. Eze. 9. 26. 29. Esaie 1. 9.} but the greater part were like this. As for the good people, who had not yet drawn back, they were in the covenant of grace and also in grace. But those faithless children who forsook the Lord were only in the covenant of grace, not in grace. {Siet Weetst Pa. 19. li. 4.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m034">Marginal reference: See Weetst, Pa. 19. li. 4.</span> Thus these words too are wholly irrelevant here, where we are speaking of the falling away of God's believing children.

<!-- source-block:b025 -->
Sixthly, 1. Timoth. 4. 1. {6. Ibid. li. 30.}

**Answer.** To wish to conclude from the words 'some shall fall away from faith': 'Therefore the regenerate can fall away from faith', is such a masterly feat that schoolchildren would certainly be beaten for it. What does “some shall fall away” mean? Why should 'some' mean the regenerate? And if those who fell away were unregenerate, could one not then say 'some shall fall away'—some, that is, unregenerate people?

But perhaps Bertius wishes to reason thus: No one can fall away from faith except someone who has faith. The unregenerate do not have faith. Therefore the unregenerate cannot fall away from faith. Consequently, if some nevertheless fall away from faith, they must be regenerate.

**Answer.** Faith means more than one thing. See and read Gal. 3. 23., Marc. 16. 16., Titum 1. 13. and t 3. 15., Rom 12. 7. and c. 1 §., Jacob. 2. 19., and other passages. Thus, when it is said that the unregenerate do not have faith, this is true if one means justifying and saving faith; {Tit. 1. 1. 2. The. 3. 2.} but if one means knowledge of the gospel, outward religion, confidence in God's or Christ's omnipotence, or the like, then it is not true, for even the devils have faith.

As for the apostle's words cited above, 'some shall fall away from faith', faith there means the doctrine of faith and of the divine Word. This is evident from what is added: adhering to the doctrine of devils, forbidding marriage and commanding abstinence from food, etc. {Vers. 1. en 3.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m037">Marginal reference: Vers. 1. and 3.</span> Thus the apostle's intention is only to point out that false teachers will arise in the last times, and that some who have built up the true doctrine will adhere to them and fall away. Therefore Timothy should set this before the brethren, so that he may be a good minister of Christ, nourished in the words of faith and good doctrine. {Vers. 6. Siet oock 2. Ti. 4. 3. etc.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m038">Marginal reference: Vers. 6. See also 2. Ti. 4. 3. etc.</span> But what bearing does this have on our question?

<!-- source-block:b026 -->
Seventhly, 2. Petr. 3. 17. {7. Ibid. lin. 33}

**Answer.** Steadfastness does not mean righteousness, holiness, faith or love. Consequently this inference is invalid: Someone can fall from his steadfastness; therefore he can also fall from his righteousness, etc. Steadfastness and doubt are merely incidental qualities of faith, as fervour and coldness are incidental qualities of love. And this steadfastness of faith here means not the assurance of the confidence which believers have in Christ, but an undoubting acknowledgement of the truth of the doctrine of the divine Word which they have received. In particular, it concerns the doctrine of the world's coming destruction, which certain scoffers, unlearned and unstable people, seek to deny by twisting the words of the holy apostle Paul and the other Scriptures. {2 Petr. 3 3. 4. 10.} But you, says the apostle Peter, remember the prophetic Word, which is very firm, and also our teaching, we who are apostles of the Lord and Saviour; {2. Petr. 3. 2. ende ca. 1. 19.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m041">Marginal reference: 2. Petr. 3. 2. and ca. 1. 19.</span> and take care that you are not also misled by such scoffers and unstable people into doubting what is firm and certain, {2. Petr. 3. 17.} but grow in the grace and knowledge of Jesus Christ. {18.}

This is the proper meaning of these words. Nothing can be concluded from them except that someone who regards God's Word in general as firm and certain can be persuaded by wicked deceivers to doubt the truth and firmness of some particular point. Or someone who now imagines himself on firm ground in a particular point of doctrine can afterwards come to doubt it, as those who build their house on sand learn daily by experience. {Matt. 7. 26.} But this does not concern those founded on a rock, {Matt. 7. 24.} who do not merely imagine themselves on firm ground but are actually steadfast and have rightly learned Christ. {Ephes. 4 21.}

Even if such people could be brought to some doubt, it nevertheless does not follow that they can also be brought to denial and abandonment of the whole of saving faith, or that they must remain stuck in doubt. For that is why the Lord Christ always gives some to his church as pastors and teachers: so that we may no longer be children, moved and carried about by every wind of doctrine in human inconstancy. {Ephes. 4. 14.} And Peter says: Since you know it beforehand, take care. {2. Petr. 3. 17.} They are certainly not truly regenerate Christians who, having been well instructed by the Word of truth and knowing beforehand what could lead them away from the truth, nevertheless depart from the firmness of the divine Word. See Bertius's second indubitable proposition. {pa. 5. ll. 6. etc}

<!-- source-block:b027 -->
Eighthly, Heb. 12. 25. {8. Pag. 25. ll. 1}

**Answer.** 'Refuse him who is from heaven' means to despise Christ, as is evident from the text. But what does despising Christ mean here? Abandoning one's righteousness, holiness or faith? That cannot be its meaning. For the word *paraiteomai*, used in the text, never means in the New Testament abandoning or rejecting what one has once accepted, but not accepting what is offered. See Luc. 14.18/19: They excuse themselves. Actor. 25.11: I do not refuse. 1. Timoth. 4.7. and 5.11., 2. Timoth 2.23., Tit. 3.10.: Reject. Ebreo. 12.19: They entreated, or asked to be excused. Must it now be understood differently here? How, then, does Bertius prove that?

Surely the Hebrews to whom {Ebreo 3.1 et c. 12.22, etc.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m051">Marginal reference: Ebreo 3.1 and c. 12.22, etc.</span> the apostle writes here, whom he calls holy brethren who have shared in the heavenly calling and have come to Mount Zion, to the city of the living God, to that heavenly Jerusalem, and to the assembly of innumerable angels, etc.—these Hebrews, I say, were not all {Ebreo. 5.12.} regenerate Christians. For there has never been a congregation on earth consisting only of regenerate people. Believers have always been mixed with hypocrites, the spiritual with the carnal, mature and fully grown men with youths and children, the regenerate with the unregenerate. If this was also the case with the Hebrews, why do we apply to all the Hebrews individually what is said generally to the Hebrews as a whole? Must what is said to children be applied to men, or what is said to men to children?

And if someone invited to come to Christ—that is, to acknowledge and accept Christ fully as his only redeemer and high priest, and to walk in holiness and godliness—refuses this, excuses himself, rejects Christ and salvation, hardens his heart, disobeys the calling voice, etc., does his refusal give us sufficient grounds to conclude at once that even those who have already accepted Christ and godliness, rightly and fully as they ought, can abandon both altogether? I say no. Anyone with understanding may judge for himself as well.

<!-- source-block:b028 -->
Ninthly, 2. Joan. 9. {9. pag. 25. II}

**Answer.** To be in Christ's doctrine is not to be justified. Therefore not remaining in Christ's doctrine cannot be called abandoning one's righteousness. This is so well known that it is unnecessary to prove it further.

<!-- source-block:b029 -->
Tenthly, Matth. 24.12. {10. Ibia. liu. 7.}

**Answer.** Growing cold is not being extinguished. And even if love were wholly extinguished in many, how could one conclude from that that the regenerate could also entirely lose their love for God and their neighbour? For 'many' does not mean regenerate people; and love is found not only in the regenerate but also in tax collectors and sinners. {Matt. 5.44.} Therefore love can also grow cold in people other than the regenerate.

<!-- source-block:b030 -->
Eleventhly, Apocalip.2.5. {11. ibid lin 9. Siet vers.4} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m056">Marginal reference: 11. ibid lin 9.; see vers.4.</span>

**Answer.** Nothing more can be concluded from these words than that the fervour of love can cease for a time. But when the fervour of love has been taken away, love nevertheless still remains. Indeed, when such exhortations and threats are addressed to the regenerate as are addressed here, the fading fervour is thereby rekindled and strengthened. {vers.7.} These words, however, are not spoken to any one person individually but to the whole congregation of Ephesus. {vers.1.} And would Bertius dare say that when a congregation is praised or rebuked generally, all the individuals belonging to that congregation may rightly and truthfully claim it for themselves? See also what we have just answered concerning Ezech, 12.25. and Matth.24.12.

<!-- source-block:b031 -->
Twelfthly, 1.Thessal.5.19. {12. ibid.lin.11.}

**Answer.** This does not bear on the matter. For there are many different workings of the Spirit in a person. {1 Cori.12.6. Eph.12.6. Ephes.4.12.} Some, such as faith, are for the person who has them alone. [The description of these gifts is unclear; the contrast is with gifts given for others.] Others properly serve—or, rather, are given by God solely to benefit—others who do not have them, such as prophecy, healing, etc. Both these kinds of workings can and must be used, with sincerity and earnestness. But I deny that both could also be wholly extinguished; nor is that evident from the words of the holy apostle Paul cited above, unless everywhere and always, by 'the Spirit' used without qualification, we must understand all the gifts and workings of the Spirit of every kind. That is unfounded and false.

Here, therefore, I understand by 'the Spirit' not the gift of faith or the like, but those particular gifts and workings of the Spirit which he, in the days of the first apostolic church, abundantly worked in people for the service and adornment of the churches. Such were those which the apostle mentions in Rom.12., 1.Corinth.12. {Rom 12 7.8 1.Cor.12.28} I accept this interpretation because the apostle adds, as though in the same breath:

<!-- source-block:b032 -->
> Do not quench the Spirit. Do not despise prophecy. Test everything, and retain what is good. {ver.19.20.21}

<!-- source-block:b033 -->
He indicates that one can neglect to test everything, and that even if one does test everything, one can neglect to retain what is good. One can also despise the prophets—that is, the gift of interpretation given to us, or given to others—and refuse to put it into practice, or to hear and accept it. By doing so one can quench the Spirit: namely, not the Spirit of faith but the Spirit of prophecy which we have, or which others have; {Siet Theodoret en Græca Scholia over dese plaetse.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m063">Marginal reference: See Theodoret and the Greek scholia on this passage.</span> also the Spirit of discernment, or of the discerning of spirits, {1. Cor. 12. 10.} through which many could be brought to faith. {1. Cor. 14. 3.} Thus these words of the apostle do not contradict us. For we acknowledge that the Spirit—that is, such gifts of the Spirit—can be extinguished. Therefore the same apostle also exhorts Timothy: Stir up the gift of God that is in you. {2. Tim. 1. 6.} According to the force of the Greek word, one would say: Make the gift of God that is in you burn again. But we are speaking here of faith, righteousness and holiness, as has often been pointed out.

<!-- source-block:b034 -->
Thirteenthly, 1. Tim. 1. 19. {13. Ibid hu. 17. 1. Tim. 4. 15.}

**Answer.** Here the apostle exhorts Timothy concerning his conduct, not insofar as he is a Christian, but insofar as he is a bishop and teacher of Christians. Have, or keep, faith, he says, understanding by faith the words of faith and of good and sound doctrine. It is as though he said: Remain steadfast in the doctrine; keep a pattern of sound doctrine; remain in what you have learned. {1. Tim. 1. 10. en cap 4. 6. 1. Tim 6. 16 2. Tim 1. 13. en cap. 3. 14.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m068">Marginal references: 1. Tim. 1. 10. and cap 4. 6.; 1. Tim 6. 16; 2. That is: Divide the Word rightly; keep the commandment unstained and blameless; act without prejudice, not acting according to partiality. {2. Tim 2. 15. 1. Tim. 6 14 en cap. 5. 21. en cap. 6. 20.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m069">Marginal references: 2. Tim 2. 15.; 1. Tim. 6 14 and cap. 5. 21. and cap. 6. 20.</span> In short:

<!-- source-block:b035 -->
> O Timothy, guard what has been entrusted to you, avoiding profane, empty speech and the contradictory arguments of falsely named wisdom.

<!-- source-block:b036 -->
There follows: Some, rejecting this good conscience—that is, acting wilfully, out of vainglory or for profit—have suffered shipwreck of faith, that is, have strayed from the truth. {2 Tim. 2 18.} Among them is Hymenaeus, who says that the resurrection has already taken place; {ibid. vers. 18.} whom I have handed over to Satan, punishing him not so that he might be wholly lost, but so that he might learn not to blaspheme: that is, for the destruction of the flesh, so that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus Christ. {1. Corin. 5. 5.}

This is the proper sense of these words of the holy apostle Paul. I do not know what Bertius can draw from them to his advantage. For we acknowledge with Bertius that someone who once preached the Word of truth faithfully and sincerely can afterwards abandon sincerity and fall into false doctrines. But what had to be proved was that all who know the doctrine of truth and set it forth faithfully according to their knowledge are also true believers, righteous and regenerate Christians; and we deny that. See Philippen. 1. 16. etc., and capst. 2. 20 / 21., and cap. 3. 2. 18., and various other passages. The ancients also indicate this through Noah's ark: many worked at building it who did not enter it and were not preserved by it from the common destruction.

<!-- source-block:b037 -->
Fourteenthly, Ebreoz.10.35. {14. Ibid. lin. 20.}

**Answer.** What our translation calls confidence must, according to the force of the Greek word, be called boldness. It means not the boldness we have in Christ to approach God, {Ephes.3.12. Ebre.4.16.} but fearlessness in professing faith before people in a time of persecution. Thus, in exhorting the Hebrews to acknowledge the doctrine of truth they have received with fervent and fearless hearts, the apostle only indicates that they can be deficient in this. But are all who profess the truth even boldly regenerate, justified Christians? {1.Corin.13.2} And is failing to profess the truth boldly the same as denying it? {1.Reg.19.18} Indeed, does even denying the truth and dissembling [the second verb is unclear] mean falling away from the truth altogether? {Gal.2.13.14} Or how does this bear on our subject?

<!-- source-block:b038 -->
Fifteenthly, Gal.3.3. {15. Ibid. lin.22.}

**Answer.** Beginning in the Spirit is not beginning in godliness, and being perfected with the flesh is not ending in godlessness. Rather, the apostle here calls the works of the law flesh, and the doctrine of faith, or of the holy gospel, spirit. He indicates not that the Galatians had now wholly abandoned the doctrine of the gospel which they had once accepted and turned to the law, but that, while retaining the gospel, they let themselves be persuaded that they must also add the law to it if they were to be perfect. This is evident from the second verse of this chapter and from most of the whole epistle.

In short, nothing else can be concluded from these words than that someone who has accepted the doctrine of truth can be misled into believing some other doctrines alongside it. And who censures or denies that? Or does Bertius think that all who accept the truth as truth thereby immediately become justified and consequently regenerate? That is an error.

Surely in the congregations of Galatia they were not all regenerate, nor all unregenerate. Just as only some let themselves be deceived by the false teachers, so undoubtedly some will also have guarded themselves against them. {Men moet niet anders oordeel van de Gemeenten van Galaten/dan t'ghene men dagelijcx siet in anderen Gemeenten gheschien.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m079">Marginal note: One must not judge the congregations of the Galatians differently from what one sees happening daily in other congregations.</span> Therefore the apostle also says: You who began with the Spirit, are you now being perfected with the flesh? He means to indicate that those who still wanted to be perfected were not yet perfect: that is, they had not yet rightly received Christ through true faith, nor properly known him to be the perfecter of faith, {Ebr.12.2.} since they sought their perfection from the law. Nor did they have senses trained to distinguish good and evil; rather, like children, they were unskilled in the word of righteousness. {Ebr.5.14.} Consequently they were neither justified nor regenerate.

<!-- source-block:b039 -->
Sixteenthly, Ebreo. 10.38. {16. Ibid. lin. 25.}

**Answer.** To withdraw oneself is, through fear and faint-heartedness, to keep quiet in a time of persecution and not profess Christ together with the holy gospel. This is evident from the text. But is our question whether a regenerate person can withdraw in this way? Does the apostle also say that a regenerate person can so withdraw? And if he did so, that he would have to perish? By no means. The righteous shall live by faith, he says, and if anyone draws back—withdraws himself—then my soul shall have no pleasure in him. Why does he not say: Then he shall die? Or: Then God shall have no pleasure in him? He means to show that one can live and please God so long as one remains in Jesus Christ, without denying him in words and deeds, even though one did not have the boldness to profess Christ so openly. See what we answered before concerning the fourteenth passage. Not all Christians have received the same amount of strength from God. Paul had incomparably more than Ignatius, {Acto. 21.13.} of whom Bertius gives an account; {pag. 84. l. 34} and Ignatius had more than the apostle Peter had at first. {Mat.26.70} Yet were they not all believers nevertheless?

<!-- source-block:b040 -->
Seventeenthly, 2. Pet. 2.1. {17. Ibid. lin. 28. pag.117.l.15.}

<!-- source-block:b041 -->
> Here the apostle teaches, says Bertius, that some who had been redeemed by the Lord shall deny him and perish, etc.

<!-- source-block:b042 -->
**Answer.** It is unlikely that Peter teaches that some who had been redeemed by the Lord shall deny him, if this is understood of the redemption accomplished through Christ's blood. {1. Pet. 1.18.19} For the text mentions not redemption but purchase; not Christ but the Lord; not blood or any other price, but simply: who bought them. And from what did he buy them? From the devil's power? {Tit. 2.14. Eph.1.13.14. Galat. 3.13.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m088">Marginal reference: Tit. 2.14.; Eph.1.13.14.; Galat. 3.13.</span> That is not expressed either. As for the word 'buy', it does also signify a kind of redemption, but not necessarily a spiritual redemption. See Deut. 7.8. and 15.15., Jer. 13.21., 1. Samuel 14.45., 2. Sam. 4.9/ and cap. 7.23., and other passages where our common translation has the word 'redeem' and the Hebrew has Padah, which properly means to release or buy free, as in Exod. 13.13. and cap. 21.8., and throughout Holy Scripture. But passing over all this, and whatever else could suitably be adduced for a proper understanding of Peter's words cited above, attend only to what Bertius writes here:

<!-- source-block:b043 -->
> This statement, he says, 'The Lord Jesus Christ has bought the whole human race with his blood', is true not only by way of supposition and according to the nature of charity, but also in actual fact. {pag. 117. l. 26}

<!-- source-block:b044 -->
If it is as Bertius says, then the whole human race must be justified and regenerate; otherwise he cannot prove that these false prophets had been regenerate because they had been bought by the Lord. Unless the force of his argument consisted in its being said that they shall deny the Lord: for denying seems to presuppose knowledge and profession of the one whom one afterwards denies. But this does not help either. For neither Scripture, nor reason, nor any sensible Christian has ever taught that knowledge or profession of Christ made a person righteous before God. {Rom 2.13. Matt.7.21.}

<!-- source-block:b045 -->
Eighteenthly, Esai.1.22. {18. Pa.25.l.31.}

**Answer.** These words can be taken literally. Indeed, why should we not be bound to take them so? They describe the fraud practised in Judah: people helped themselves and cheated one another with counterfeit money and false merchandise. If one nevertheless wishes to interpret them figuratively, understand silver and wine as the pure Word of God, which the faithless false prophets turned to dross and mixed with water—that is, shamefully falsified. See Esai.3.11. and cap.56.10. etc. and cap.59.4. etc.

Jerome explains it thus, although he also adds that these words could also well be understood of holy and righteous men who had formerly been in Jerusalem and had now been turned or changed into heedless sinners. Not that those who had formerly been righteous had now become sinners, but that sinners and wicked people had now taken the place of the righteous who once lived there. For children do not always take after their fathers, and those who inherit their parents' goods do not always inherit their virtue too, as Bertius himself acknowledges. {Hiero- en̄ d. Iudæorum Quæstionum Probl. Class 2.lib 2. Vrag.33. Pag.69.k.5} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m092">Marginal title and locator: Hiero- and d., Questions of the Jews, Problems, class 2, book 2, question 33, page 69, k.5.</span>

Nevertheless, I should still like once to hear or see how Bertius would prove that silver and wine ever signified in God's Word a righteous, regenerate person, which is what our question concerns; for I cannot find it anywhere. [The disputed word can also read “never”; “ever” fits the challenge Bertius is asked to meet.]

<!-- source-block:b046 -->
Nineteenthly, Jerem.2.21. and Esai.5.1. {19. pag.25.l.35.}

**Answer.** God himself explains this figurative manner of speaking in Esai.5.7.:

<!-- source-block:b047 -->
> The vineyard of the Lord Sabaoth is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah his lovely vineyards. He looked for right dealing: behold, there is wrongdoing; for righteousness: behold, there is a cry of distress. {Siet Psalm 80.9.} <span class="marginal-translation" data-margin="m094">Marginal reference: See Psalm 80.9.</span>

<!-- source-block:b048 -->
What does Bertius mean to say by this now? That the house of Israel and the men of Judah had been justified in Jesus Christ and had been regenerate children of God? That is untrue. God's intention in these words is only to point out to the Israelites the many and excellent benefits he had bestowed upon them, for which they ought rightly to have been grateful in return. Since they were not, God had just cause to punish and afflict them. See Deuter.32.10. etc., Mich.6.3. etc., and other passages. But how does this serve our subject?

<!-- source-block:b049 -->
Lastly, Matth.24.29. {10. pag.26.ll.4.}

**Answer.** Since in these words Christ foretells what will happen shortly before he comes to judgement, it would have to follow that only then would believers also abandon their faith. Nonsense!

<!-- source-block:b050 -->
It is thus evident that what Bertius here puts forward is sheer untruth. First, that anyone who can fall away, turn from righteousness, and do the other things listed there can also abandon his former righteousness. Secondly, that some who are righteous—that is, justified in Christ and regenerate—can fall away and do all the other things added to it. And as for what Bertius further says: {pag.26.l.37.}

<!-- source-block:b051 -->
> If the righteous person could not abandon his righteousness, these ways of speaking would mean nothing at all.
> But it is absurd to say that. [The wording may instead mean “what they say is absurd.”]
>
> Therefore the righteous person can abandon his righteousness.

<!-- source-block:b052 -->
This is indeed very absurd. For, as has now been proved, these ways of speaking mean something, even though they do not mean what Bertius would like. [Another reading begins “For it has by no means now been proved …”; its connection to the claim about these expressions is unclear.] And if I now wished to be as unreasonable as Bertius, and said, 'These ways of speaking mean nothing at all unless they mean what we say they mean', then surely Bertius would be paid in the same coin he seeks to put into our hand.

But, dear God, how would he then deal with us! His smallest finger would be thicker than my loins, and although we dare not give him a thorough chastising with rods, he would certainly beat us with scorpions. So much depends not on what is said but on who says it. Yet, had Bertius not chosen expressly to exercise his liberty, of which he is so fond, I think he would have thought a little better of it.
